The Four-Minute Walk

At nine sixteen Dale Varga turned the bus wide around a hump of ice on King. Yesterday it had been a puddle with a grocery flyer floating in it. Tonight he could read the price of pork chops in his right mirror. The radio clicked twice and Carla said, “Varga, nobody's coming to take your twelve-oh-six. I got two reliefs and they're both on the mountain.”

“Wasn't counting on anybody.”

“Good. Saves me a call.”

Three hours left. At the bottom of his paddle it said THURSDAY 19 FEBRUARY, and below that somebody had printed GARAGE EAST in red, as if it might have moved. In eleven years with the city he'd worked three routes that finished there. This was the only one that got him in before the last westbound went past the gate. Four minutes from the farebox to the stop if he didn't go to his locker. At forty-seven he could still do it in three.

His daughter had sent him a picture at supper of a used Mazda. Thoughts Dad? He'd blown it up to look at the wheel wells and found himself looking instead at a reflection of the photographer in the blue passenger door. A man in slippers on a patch of churned-up snow. He'd sent back Ask for the service records. She still thought he had the Corolla. So did Carla, who once a week asked the drivers to leave the spaces by the salt shed clear and always added, “That includes you, Dale.” He'd parked crooked there exactly once, four years ago.

The hospital stop had a shelter with no glass on its street side. A boy in a green snowsuit stood in the gap, his boots placed carefully together. The woman behind him held a black duffel across her front with both hands as if somebody were about to take a picture of the three of them.

“Does he need to pay?” she asked.

“Not till he's bigger.”

“I'm four,” the boy said, in case there had been a mistake.

“That'll do it.”

The woman's tap went through. She got the boy into the first forward-facing seat and worked his hood down around his shoulders. His hair stood up fine and white in the light, then settled dark against his head. She put the bag at her feet, between her ankles. In the mirror Dale saw her hold her phone close to her mouth. She didn't speak into it. The light went out.

At Wellington he got a red long enough to take a drink. The tea had gone cold and tasted mostly of the rubber ring in the lid. Behind him the woman said, “Theo, if you pull it off you have to carry it yourself.”

“My head is cooking.”

“Fair enough.”

She reached out a hand and took the hat anyway. The boy watched Dale replace the cup in its wire holder. “What's your biggest button?”

“My biggest button? Probably the one for the doors.”

“It's not a button,” the boy said. “It's a little arm.”

“Well, you got me there.”

“What's your biggest actual button?”

Dale had to think about it. He looked over his left elbow. “The horn. Middle of the wheel. That whole part's a button.”

Theo sat back satisfied. Dale was glad he hadn't asked to hear it. Two girls got on with a slab of pink bakery cardboard held level between them. All the way to Ottawa Street he could smell the buttercream. When they stood up the boy said softly, “Cake going,” and by the time the girls made the sidewalk he was asleep against the woman's sleeve.

“Which stop do you want?” Dale asked. “I can give you a heads-up.”

“I'm going to my sister's.”

He waited. Sometimes people gave you the colour of a house or the name of a beer store that had closed in 1998. You could usually work it out.

“She's going to text me,” the woman said.

Out east they had five minutes at the loop. Dale pulled the brake, got out and walked to the back wheel, stepping across the frozen brown ruffles the bus had pushed up on its last trip through the slush. He put his hands in his pockets. Far down the dark there was the long pale building where he would leave the bus. He could see one lit upstairs window. A length of fencing separated him from a shortcut he wasn't supposed to take and never had.

When he got back in, the woman had moved with the boy one row farther from the door. “We go back the other way now,” he said.

“That's okay. Is it okay?”

“Sure.”

On the trip west a man in a quilted vest got on, said “Beautiful out” in a tone that required an answer, and sat down before Dale could give him one. The heater under the first long bench had begun to tick. It did six quick ticks and then waited just long enough for Dale to believe it had quit. He had written it up twice. Once he'd got the bus back with the words UNABLE TO DUPLICATE on a tag round the wheel. He wondered how long they'd sat there.

At the hospital the woman put her hand on the window. The shelter was empty. A plow had come along and shaved the road ice to a pale flour. Dale didn't stop. In the mirror she was wiping away the five little ovals her fingers had made.

His daughter wanted a car because her mother's place was nowhere near her new job. Dale had said he'd go with her to look when she had it down to two. That was the extent of the promise, though he knew she'd counted on him driving them there. He'd have to pick a day and tell her. In October the man who'd come for the Corolla had waited while Dale worked the little plastic garage remote off the visor. Funny thing to have saved. It opened the door at the house where he didn't live anymore.

At the west loop the woman asked, “Do you get to decide how hot it is?”

“Only up here. Out there it's its own boss.”

“It's nice. Our apartment, you can set it for whatever you want. What you get is twenty-six.”

“My daughter used to put her pyjamas in the freezer.”

The woman laughed. “To put on?”

“No, just to take out and complain that now they smelled like fish sticks.”

The laugh woke the boy a little. He pushed his chin deeper into her sleeve. She looked at her phone, turned it over and picked at a peeling corner of the case. Dale changed the sign for the last run east.

By Gage he had the two of them and a young fellow taking home a mop still wrapped in clear plastic. The fellow got off at Parkdale and the woman raised her head at the sound of the doors. It was twelve minutes after midnight. The bus was due in the bay at sixteen. His westbound passed at twenty-three. He'd left his lunch bag looped over his backpack strap so he could pick up both at once.

“Last public stop,” he said. “Next one's inside the garage.”

“I thought there'd be someplace open here.”

“There's the gas station but it's through a drawer this time of night.”

“Right.” She leaned over to get the duffel. The zip had parted along one side; Dale saw a little stack of picture books with library stickers on the spines. There was a bottle brush with a blue handle. She pulled the two halves together and ran the slider back and forth until the teeth caught.

“Stay sitting for now,” he said. “Gets rough going over the tracks.”

Carla came on to tell him bay six was blocked. “Come in on eight and leave it running. Relief's taking it out at quarter to one.”

“Copy eight.”

He brought them in past the long wash windows. Yellow brushes hung there in pairs. The heat inside the garage brought up the smell of the wet grit under his pedals. He pulled into eight beside a bus with its whole back end open and set the brake. In the mirror the woman still had her hand round the zip.

“Is there actually somebody coming?” he asked.

“Could I just wait on here till she answers?”

“They take this one out again.”

“Okay.” She said it like he'd offered her something. The heater ticked away. He opened the lid of the farebox keyboard and keyed in the end of his run. It asked for his mileage, which he got wrong, then it wouldn't let him go back. He put his finger on CLEAR and held it there while the woman took out her phone and showed him a screen full of green bubbles. He couldn't read them from where he sat. There wasn't a single grey one in among them.

“That's my sister,” she said.

The keyboard gave one long beep. Dale put in the right numbers. On the clock above the wash windows it was eighteen past.

“Carla, I've got two passengers for the office. Can I bring them through?”

“What do they need?”

“A warm place to make a call.”

There was a gap, then Carla said, “Visitors have to be signed in and sat with. I can't come down. I'm trying to get this relief in a cab at the moment.”

He could direct them to the gate. There was time to get them on the westbound with him; she'd have another forty minutes to sit and look at her phone. He pictured himself standing to pull the cord at his own corner, putting his gloves on while she watched.

“I'll sit,” he said, and unclipped his belt.

He took the duffel so she could carry the boy. At the steps Theo opened his eyes and said, quite distinctly, “Not here.”

“Just for a minute,” she said.

The office had a wired-glass door and two orange chairs bolted to a length of board. Dale filled in the date on the visitors' sheet, crossed out Thursday and put Friday instead. She printed ANA RUIZ in the first space and THEO in the second. In the column marked BADGE she wrote 29, then said, “Oh. I thought it said age.”

“It's for me anyway.” He put his number in next to hers. Through the glass the westbound went past on the street, glowing like a shop somebody had forgotten to close. The cab home last time he'd missed it had been thirty-eight dollars before tip. He set his lunch bag down.

Ana plugged her phone into the outlet by the chairs. “Could I use a phone that isn't mine?”

He pushed the desk set across to her and showed her the nine for an outside line. She didn't consult the screen of her own phone to dial. He turned his chair toward the window to give her what privacy there was. Behind him he heard, “It's me. No, don't hang up. Can you just listen for a second?”

Theo had slipped down off her lap. He stood by Dale's knee holding his hat. Across the bay a mechanic lowered the back panel of the broken bus, wiped both palms down his thighs and tried it again when it didn't catch.

“Is this where you wash them?” Theo whispered.

“Through there.” Dale pointed out the yellow brushes. “See the black marks? That's where they go along the windows.”

The boy pushed a finger against the wired glass and traced the height of a bus that wasn't there. Behind them Ana was quiet. Dale could hear a voice now, thin and busy in the earpiece. He drew his lunch bag over with his foot and felt in the side pocket for the two arrowroot biscuits he always brought and never wanted. He held up the packet where Ana could see. She nodded without taking the phone away.

Theo accepted one biscuit, considered the other through the cellophane, then broke his own in half against the edge of the desk. He slid half under his mother's free hand. Crumbs lodged in the ruled lines of the visitors' sheet. Dale lifted the clipboard so the boy could brush them into his palm.

The Last Stop

The heater ticked like a cooling engine. Dale had asked maintenance about it twice, but the sound kept coming back through the vents, regular as a heartbeat, loud enough to hear over the diesel idle. He sat at a red light on King, three hours left on his shift, the windshield fogging at the edges where the defroster couldn't reach.

The thaw earlier in the week had turned everything to slush, and now the cold had returned to freeze it all in uneven ridges. His tires cracked over them at each intersection. He'd driven this route—the 67 Mohawk—for six years, long enough that his hands knew the wheel's dead spot and his body leaned into the turns before his mind registered the street names.

The light changed. He accelerated gently through the intersection, watching a woman in a parka slip on the ice at the curb. She caught herself on a lamppost. Dale's chest tightened the way it always did when he saw someone nearly fall, the old instinct to do something meeting the reality that he was already past, already gone.

At the hospital stop, a woman climbed aboard with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a small boy at her side. The boy wore a puffy red coat with the hood up, and he gripped his mother's hand as she tapped her card against the reader. It beeped twice—insufficient fare.

"Sorry," the woman said. She shifted the duffel and dug in her pocket, coming up with a handful of change. "I thought I loaded it yesterday."

"It's fine," Dale said. He knew the sound of her voice, the particular steadiness of someone keeping it together. He'd used that voice himself when the bank called about the car payments. "Go ahead and sit."

"No, I have it—" She counted coins into the farebox with quick, precise movements, her eyes on her hands. The boy leaned into her leg.

"All set," Dale said when the machine registered the fare. He watched them in the mirror as they moved toward the back. The woman chose a seat near the rear door and pulled the boy up beside her. The duffel went on the floor between her feet.

Carla's voice crackled over the radio. "Fifty-three, you copy?"

Dale lifted the handset. "Go ahead."

"What's your twenty?"

"Just left the General."

"Okay. Anderson called in sick, so Mariella's picking up the 41 at James and Mohawk. You're clear to finish the circuit."

"Copy that."

He replaced the handset and checked the mirror again. The boy had his head against his mother's shoulder, but his eyes were open, watching the ceiling lights slide past. The woman had her phone out, the blue glow lighting her face as she scrolled.

Dale turned onto Mohawk and settled into the rhythm of the route: stops, starts, the hydraulic sigh of the doors. A man in a suit got on at Wentworth and sat near the front, typing on his phone with his thumb. An elderly woman with a wheeled cart boarded at Gage, breathing hard, and Dale waited while she locked the cart's wheels before he pulled away from the curb.

Twenty minutes later, the boy was asleep. Dale could see him in the mirror, slumped against his mother's arm, his mouth open slightly. The woman still had her phone in her hand, but she'd stopped scrolling. She stared at the dark window, her reflection faint against the passing headlights.

The route took them east, away from the downtown towers and into neighborhoods where the houses sat close together and the streetlights turned everything yellow. Dale knew these streets the way he knew the scars on his hands. He'd grown up not far from here, back when the steel plants ran three shifts and the air smelled like sulfur in the mornings.

His daughter Jenna was in Toronto now, working at an ad agency, living in a condo near the lake. She called once a week, usually on Sunday afternoons, and Dale told her about work, about the weather, about the neighbor's dog that barked at squirrels. He didn't tell her about the car. He'd said it was in the shop the last time she asked, and then he'd changed the subject to her job, her boyfriend, the vacation she was planning to Mexico.

At Ottawa Street, the man in the suit got off, and the elderly woman followed him, wrestling her cart down the steps. Dale waited, his foot on the brake, the heater ticking. When the doors closed, only two passengers remained: a teenage boy slouched in a middle seat with earbuds in, and the woman with her sleeping child.

The radio crackled. "All units, weather advisory for freezing rain starting around midnight. Watch your spacing."

Dale clicked the handset twice in acknowledgment and let it drop. Midnight was an hour after his shift ended, but he'd be out in it anyway, waiting for the 12 at the Eastgate terminal, the cold working through his jacket while he stood under the shelter watching headlights on the expressway.

The teenage boy got off at Parkdale. Dale checked the mirror. The woman met his eyes for a moment, then looked away, her hand over her son's back.

The route curved north, then west again, following Queenston toward the garage. Dale had eleven more stops on the schedule, but no one else got on. The streets were mostly empty now, just the occasional car rushing past, their headlights cutting through the dark.

At the Parkwood stop, the woman shifted in her seat. Dale watched her stand halfway, then sit back down. She looked at her phone, the screen lighting her face again, her expression unreadable.

They passed Nash Road. Then West Gate. The garage was three kilometers ahead, a flat-roofed building with a fenced lot and sodium lights that turned the pavement orange. Dale radioed in.

"Unit Fifty-three to base."

"Go ahead, Fifty-three."

"Coming in off the 67. ETA ten minutes."

"Copy that. Park in Bay Four. Maintenance wants to check your rear axle."

"Copy."

He replaced the handset and glanced at the mirror. The woman was looking at her phone again, her thumb hovering over the screen, not moving. The boy slept on, his hand curled against her coat.

Dale made the last turn onto Eastwood. The garage lights came into view, harsh and industrial, spilling across the empty lot. He pulled into the driveway, past the gate, and stopped in Bay Four. The engine idled. The heater ticked.

He turned in his seat. "End of the line."

The woman looked up, startled, as if she'd forgotten where she was. "Oh. Right." She didn't move.

Dale waited. She glanced down at her phone, then at the boy, then back at Dale. "Is there—do you go back out?"

"This bus is done for the night," Dale said. "There's a shelter at the terminal. The 12 runs every half hour."

"Right." She bit her lip. "Okay."

But she still didn't stand. The boy stirred against her, making a soft noise, and she stroked his hair with automatic gentleness. Her duffel bag sat between her boots, the strap twisted.

Dale turned off the engine. The silence felt sudden, just the creak of metal cooling and a voice from the dispatch office, muffled through the walls. He should log out, hand in his manifest, clock off. Instead he sat there, his hands still on the wheel.

"Where's your sister live?" he asked.

The woman blinked. "Sorry?"

"You said you were going to your sister's." He hadn't heard her say that, actually, but it was what people said when they didn't want to give an address. He'd driven long enough to recognize the phrasing.

"Oh. Yeah. Out past—" She gestured vaguely. "I'm just waiting to hear which bus to take."

Dale nodded. They both knew she was lying. He could see it in the way she held her shoulders, the way her eyes went to the window like she was checking for someone.

"Hospital visit?" he asked.

She hesitated, then shook her head. "Work. I work there. Housekeeping." The lie came easier this time, practiced.

"Long shift?"

"Yeah."

He turned forward and looked at the dashboard, the dark gauges, the clipboard with his route sheet clipped to the sun visor. Behind him, he heard the woman's phone buzz, a soft vibration against fabric.

Carla's voice cut through the radio. "Fifty-three, you clear?"

Dale lifted the handset. "Fifty-three clear."

"Okay. Don't forget your manifest."

"Copy."

He set the handset down. In the mirror, the woman was reading something on her phone, her face tight with concentration. The boy shifted in his sleep, and she adjusted her arm to support his weight.

Dale stood and walked down the aisle. The woman looked up, her expression wary.

He kept his voice low. "The 12 doesn't go back west. It heads north to the plaza."

"That's—that's fine. I can figure it out."

"It's nine degrees out." He nodded toward the boy. "And the terminal's not heated."

Her jaw set. "We'll be fine."

Dale thought about the sixty-two dollars in his wallet. Enough for a cab to the mountain, maybe, or the east end. Not enough to get expensive about it. He thought about his own bus, the 12 that would leave in thirty-five minutes, the forty-minute ride to his apartment, the cold walk from the stop.

"I can call you a cab," he said. "My cell's got minutes."

The woman's eyes filled, just for a second, before she blinked it away. "I can't—I don't have cash for a cab."

"It's handled."

"No." She shook her head. "I can't take that."

Dale pulled out his wallet and counted out sixty dollars, holding it out. "It's cold. You've got your boy. Just take it."

She stared at the money, then at him. "Why?"

"Because I've got it and you need it," he said, which was only half true. He needed it too, for groceries, for the overdue electric bill. But he also knew the look on her face, the careful blankness that came from holding yourself together. He'd worn that look for months.

She reached out slowly and took the bills, folding them into her palm. "Thank you," she said, her voice barely above a whisper.

Dale nodded and walked back to the front. He picked up the radio handset. "Base, this is Fifty-three. Can you send a cab to Bay Four?"

Static, then Carla's tired voice. "You calling for a passenger?"

"Yeah."

"Dale, you know that's not—okay. Fine. Where to?"

He looked at the woman in the mirror. She had her phone out, reading something, and she spoke without looking up. "Corner of West Fifth and Dundurn."

"West Fifth and Dundurn," Dale repeated into the radio. He heard Carla sigh, the sound of her writing it down.

"Ten minutes," she said. "And Dale? You're paying for this yourself. Policy."

"I know."

He hung the handset up and sat in the driver's seat, filling out his manifest with the pen that was always running out of ink. Behind him, the woman was murmuring to her son, waking him gently. The boy made a complaining sound, and she shushed him, soft and patient.

Dale signed his sheet and looked at the clock on the dashboard. His bus left in twenty-eight minutes. If the cab was late, if he had to wait to make sure they got in, he'd miss it. The next one wasn't for an hour, and the cold would sink into his bones, the kind of cold that found the gaps in your coat and stayed there.

He thought about calling Jenna, asking for a ride, but that would mean explaining where his car was, why he needed help. The lie would crack open and show what was underneath: the missed payments, the repo man at dawn, the shame of being forty-seven and riding the bus to work like he'd done at seventeen.

The woman stood, lifting her son into her arms. The duffel hung from her shoulder. She walked down the aisle, stopped beside Dale's seat. "I don't even know your name," she said.

"Dale."

"Ana." She shifted the boy's weight. "Thank you, Dale."

"Sure."

She carried her son toward the door, and Dale opened it, the cold air rushing in. They stood on the pavement between the bus and the garage wall, her breath fogging in the sodium light. The boy's head rested on her shoulder, his eyes half-open, unseeing.

Dale saw the cab's headlights turn into the lot. It pulled up beside the bus, and he watched Ana open the door, settle her son inside, then climb in after him. She looked back once through the window, and Dale raised his hand. She did the same, a slight lift of her fingers, and then the cab was pulling away, its taillights disappearing around the corner of the building.

Dale closed the bus door. The heater had stopped ticking. He stood in the quiet, his manifest in his hand, and looked at the empty seats, the overhead lights reflecting in the dark windows.

The clock on the dashboard said nine forty-two. He had twenty-three minutes to make his bus.

He grabbed his coat and his lunch bag and stepped off, locking the door behind him. The cold bit through his shirt before he got his arms into the sleeves. He walked across the lot toward the dispatch office, his boots loud on the frozen pavement, and somewhere in the building, someone laughed, the sound carried on the wind like smoke.

The Last One Through

The thaw quit on Tuesday. By Thursday night Barton Street was wearing what it had left behind—slush frozen back into grey plates along the curbs, gutters beaded with ice, the last of the snowbanks slumped and crusted over like something baked. Dale Varga pulled the 2 east out of the downtown loop at 10:47, three hours left on his board, and the heater under the front bench went on ticking the way it had for three days now, steady as a clock with nowhere to be.

"Varga." Carla, on the radio. "Reza gave up your relief, so it's you to the barn, you're on your own past Kenora. And Haq's asking again about your early Thursday, tell him yes or no tonight so I can write the board."

"Copy."

"He asks me every day."

"You have a nice voice, Carla," Dale said, and thumped the heater panel with the side of his fist at the red on John North, which quieted the tick for half a block.

In October two men had taken the Corolla out of the visitor spot at his building at ten to five in the morning, and he had watched from the kitchen window in his coat, coffee in his hand, and said nothing to anyone. Since then he rode the 5:05 down with his badge zipped into his breast pocket, hat down, back seat, one more man going to work who happened to work on a bus. He hadn't told Carla. Carla knew everything that moved on the property, and pity travelled faster than any route on the board. He hadn't told Julia either. The Corolla was "in the shop," had been since the week after Thanksgiving. She turned twenty-two on Saturday, and the plan was the pho place on Ottawa Street, and in his breast pocket, folded flat beside the badge, fifty-two dollars—two twenties, a ten, a toonie—which was the dinner, plus cake after if she wanted it, because his account would sit in single digits until payday on the twenty-sixth.

At Wentworth two of his regulars came in stamping, Woz and Ernie, night shift at the plant. "That thaw," Woz said, hanging off the pole. "Thirty years I flood a rink back of my garage and it took the whole thing in one afternoon. Then my sump died Tuesday night. Six inches in the rec room. Christmas totes going around down there like the Maid of the Mist."

"You flood a rink in this weather," Ernie said.

"Watch. March."

They settled into the front seats and argued about March until Sherman, then about Ernie's granddaughter's provincials in Brampton, then about nothing, and the bus went up Barton in the dark with its windows fogging and the heater ticking under it all.

At the General the stop was windy and lit like an operating room. A young woman came down the ramp with a duffel over one shoulder and a boy of about four asleep against it, the boy in a snowsuit with the hood up. She shifted the kid to one arm, tapped a card on the reader. The reader blinked red. INSUFFICIENT FARE. She dug in a pocket with her free hand and got out a loonie and some dimes and looked at them.

"Go on," Dale said. "Sit down, it's cold."

"Thank you," she said, and then said it again.

She took the left side behind the wheelwell, duffel between her boots, boy across her lap. She took out her phone and the screen lit her chin, and she put it face-down on her knee, and inside a minute she had it in her hand again.

Woz leaned toward the boy. "Where's this guy going, hey?"

"My sister's," the woman said.

"Good night for it," Woz said, which was all, because Woz could talk for forty years without asking anybody a question.

The radio came up. "Varga, while you're quiet. Friday's Stew Kernohan's last, thirty-one years. There's a card going around and somebody's bringing butter tarts, don't tell him, he hates fuss."

"He hates fuss," Dale said.

"He does," Carla said, like this settled something, and went back to her board.

The boy woke enough to eat. The woman had a paper sleeve of crackers from the hospital cafeteria, and he ate two with his eyes shut and went back down with his cheek on her chest. Her phone came out. Nothing on it that Dale could see, but she held it flat on her knee a while, like something that might move.

Woz and Ernie got off at the plant gate by Kenora, both of them waving. "See you Thursday, driver."

After Kenora the bus ran empty past the scrap yards, the money stores with their lights on, the strip of nothing where Barton stopped pretending. The sky to the south-east had the refinery's orange in it. Dale slowed at the drop-off stops anyway, eleven years of habit; nobody waited east of Kenora at midnight, but he slowed.

The woman came up the aisle in her seat, voice low. "This is the last one east?"

"Last one through."

"What time's the first one back?"

"Five-forty. At the pole." She nodded like she was writing it somewhere. The boy said, thick, "Are we at Auntie G's?"

"Soon, baby," she said, to the window. The boy went back to sleep. Her phone lit her chin again, and went dark, and Dale drove.

He crossed the creek and called it. "Last stop, everybody—end of the line." In the mirror her head was back, the boy sideways across her, her coat spread over the two of them like a tarp. The heater ticked. Dale pulled up on the apron, short of the gate, where the rule said no passengers past the fence, and keyed the mic once more. "Last stop."

Her eyes opened. Not like waking—like coming off a watch. She looked out at the yard a long moment: the sodium lights, the scrapers, the wash bay with its door up, the little orange flame on the stack behind everything.

He went back. "Miss. End of the line. I've got to take it in."

"I know. Sorry. Two minutes." She was gathering the boy, and the boy was all dead weight, four years of it, too big an armful to move fast.

"Where's your sister, exactly? I'll call you a cab, you can settle with him at the door."

"Barton West," she said. Barton West was behind them by twenty minutes of driving, and she looked at the floor while she said it.

"I'll wait for the 5:40," she said. "At the pole."

"It's minus fourteen," Dale said, "and that pole's on a guardrail. Wind comes up the creek." He said it the way he'd read it off a gauge.

"Please don't call it in." Fast and quiet, the boy waking against her. "They ask where you're going, and then it's hours, and they take him somewhere and I have to go get him, and I can't be doing that tonight. Please."

The radio broke in. "Varga, you're holding on the apron—vault's wanted at the fuel desk. Also any 51 cars come off Wellington at Cannon, water main, if anybody's listening to this. And if that heater's still ticking, put a card on it tonight, maintenance flagged it this morning, I don't need it biting me Friday."

"Two minutes," Dale said. "I'm carding the heater."

He pulled the vault out of its well, heavy and warm from the day's fares, and set it in the stepwell. Then the defect card on the dash, block letters, HEATER—TICKING, WON'T STOP, SEAT 1R, the tick going on over his shoulder the whole time he printed it. He put his thumb on the mic button. He took it off again. He took out his phone and called the cab, and gave them the garage, and asked how long, and was told twenty-two minutes, and told her so.

"No," she said. "I can't."

"Sit," he said. "Twenty minutes, then you're both somewhere warm."

The boy woke in the middle of being moved and looked out at the yard. "Do the buses sleep here?"

"They nap," Dale said. "Then they get a wash, then they clock out like everybody else."

"Do they get a bath?"

"Every night. Big brushes. They come down from the ceiling."

The boy thought about it with his mouth open. "I had a bath," he said, and the woman laughed, a small cracked one, and he leaned back into her like a door closing.

Dale swept the bus with the flashlight while they waited, which he was supposed to do anyway, and under her seat he found a knitted mitten with a snowman on it, wet at the cuff. He handed it back. "Yours." The boy took it and put it straight in his mouth.

"I keep telling him," she said. "We lose everything." She didn't finish it.

Pull-In

The thaw had lasted two days. It was long enough to wear the snowbanks down to gravel and grey lace, and now the cold had come back to lock everything in place. Dale Varga felt it through the floor every time the front doors folded open: a breath of air that smelled like wet iron and the lake.

Three hours left. Then pull-in at the east garage, the vault, the defect card, and the walk across the yard to the gate. Nobody at the garage knew what he did once he was past the gate. He had kept it that way since October, when a man with a clipboard and a tow truck took the Ranger out of the lot behind his building at six in the morning while Dale stood on the step in his socks.

Around nine the heater over the front door had started ticking. It wasn't a rattle. It was a tick, steady as a clock somebody had hidden in the ceiling panel. He'd tried thumping the panel at a red light and it kept going. He added it to the list in his head for the defect card: heater, front, ticking. Wiper, driver side, chatters.

At 10:40 he pulled into the hospital stop on Barton. A woman stood under the shelter light with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a small boy pressed against her leg. She waited until the doors were fully open before she moved, as if she didn't trust them to stay that way.

She tapped her card. The reader beeped low and flashed red.

"Try it again," Dale said.

She tried. Red.

"Go on, sit down."

"I had money on it," she said. "I'm going to my sister's."

He hadn't asked. He nodded anyway and waited while she steered the boy down the aisle. She took the first forward-facing seat behind the wheelchair area, where he could see her in the big mirror without turning his head. The boy was maybe four. He had on a snowsuit jacket, flannel pyjama pants with rockets on them, and running shoes with soles that blinked red each time his feet swung. No boots. A pillowcase stuck out of the duffel's zipper like a tongue.

When she pushed her hair back, Dale saw a white plastic band on her wrist. She saw him see it and tugged her sleeve down over it, and he looked at the road.

The radio clicked. "Four-one-two, dispatch."

"Four-one-two."

"Varga, it's Carla. Delgado called off for tomorrow. I've got the five-forty sitting empty. You want it?"

Five-forty meant signing on at five-ten. He'd be pulling in around one. He did the arithmetic on getting home and back and didn't like it.

"Can I think about it?"

"Think fast. I've got a whole board to fill and Pritchard's back is out again." She sounded like she was eating something. "And the Mountain's glazing over, so if anybody tells you they're running late up the Jolley Cut, believe them."

"Copy."

He went east. Barton was mostly dark storefronts and the bright square of a pizza place, then a long stretch where the houses sat right up against the sidewalk with their porch lights off. A man got on at Sherman, rode four stops, got off. Two teenagers in the back shared earbuds and laughed at something on a phone. Every few minutes, Dale glanced up and saw the woman's face lit blue from below. She would check the phone, put it face down on her thigh, then turn it over and check it again.

By Ottawa Street the boy had fallen asleep against her. His mouth hung open and his hand was fisted in her coat. One running shoe had come half off his heel and kept blinking.

At the Kenilworth light a pickup pulled up in the lane beside him. It was dark, with a lift kit and a rack of lights across the cab roof that wasn't switched on. In the mirror Dale watched the woman bend down over the boy as if she was fixing his zipper. She stayed bent over through the whole red. When the light changed, the pickup went off ahead of them, turned right at the next corner, and was gone. She sat up slowly. She didn't look out the window. She looked at her phone.

Dale drove.

At the loop where the teenagers got off, the bus was quiet except for the heater. Tick. Tick. He could hear the boy breathing, a small wet click at the end of each breath.

"Does it always do that?" the woman said.

"The heater? Started tonight."

"It's like a turn signal that won't turn off."

"I'll write it up. They'll ignore it for a month." He checked the mirror. "What's your name?"

She paused a little, then said, "Ana."

"Dale."

"I know. It's on the sign." She pointed at the operator card in its plastic sleeve by the door. Then she smiled for the first time, a quick tired smile that came and went. "Sorry. I'm not usually like this."

"Like what?"

"On a bus at midnight." She shifted the boy's weight. "This is Theo. He'll sleep through anything except me trying to put him down."

"My daughter was like that. Still is, kind of. She's seventeen."

"Oh no."

"Yeah." He signalled and pulled out around a parked hydro truck. "She failed her road test last week. Parallel parking. She's taking it again Saturday."

"Nobody in Hamilton parallel parks. They just stop in the middle of the road with the four-ways on."

He laughed, which surprised him. "That's what I told her."

"I used to move cars at the airport. For one of the rental places." Ana sat up a little, interested in something for once. "Tell her this. Pull up even with the car in front, mirrors lined up. Back up slow until you see that car's back bumper in the middle of your back window. Then full lock. When your front door's even with their bumper, straighten out, then full lock the other way."

"Back bumper, middle of the window."

"It works every time. They make you practise it with a shuttle van, and if you can do it in that, you can do it in anything."

"I'll tell her."

"Do you drive her? To practise?"

The question was simple and he answered it the way he'd been answering it since October. "When I can. Weekends, mostly."

"And you drive home after this? After driving all night?"

"Truck's in the lot," he said.

She nodded and went back to her phone. He watched her thumb hover over the screen, type something, delete it, type again. She didn't send it, or if she did, nothing came back. She put the phone face down.

The stops went by with nobody at them. Dale called them anyway, the way he'd been trained, his voice going out over the empty seats. Past the plaza with the shuttered Zellers sign still ghosted on the brick. Past the rail bridge where the slush had frozen into ruts that rocked the bus side to side. Theo didn't wake.

Two stops from the end, he said, "Ana. Where's your sister's? I'll let you off closest."

"It's okay. Keep going."

"Next one's the last real stop. After that I'm out of service to the garage."

"I know." She was looking at the phone. "She's going to text me."

He let the last stop go by. He told himself it was because he was waiting for her to ring the bell, but she didn't ring it, and he didn't stop. He flipped the destination sign to NOT IN SERVICE and turned into the garage lane. The yard was a big sodium-orange rectangle of parked buses lined up in rows, their roofs frosted where the melt had refrozen. Benny was in the fuel lane in his reflective coat, waving him in with a flashlight.

Dale stopped at the pump and set the brake. The heater ticked.

"Ana."

"I know." She didn't move. "Is there one going back the other way?"

"Not from here. Not till about five."

Benny knocked on the door glass. Dale opened it, and the cold came in with him.

"You're heavy tonight," Benny said, looking past him down the aisle. He kept his voice low because of the boy.

"Give me a minute."

"I don't see nothing. But Rourke's in the office." Benny stuck the nozzle in and walked off to the next lane.

Dale pulled the vault key off his ring and bent to the farebox. You had to seat the probe and hold it until the light went green, and tonight the light kept blinking amber, so he pulled the probe and seated it again. He could feel Ana watching him in the mirror the way he'd watched her for two hours.

Rule was rule. No passengers in the yard. If he radioed it in, Carla would do what she was supposed to do, which was call the supervisor, and the supervisor would call the police to do a wellness check. A cruiser would pull up in the fuel lane with its lights going across all those frosted roofs. Ana would have to say a street name then, and a last name, and whatever the bracelet on her wrist said.

Or he could open the doors at the gate and let her walk out onto Barton at one in the morning with a sleeping four-year-old in running shoes, and it was minus eleven.

The probe light went green. He pulled it, logged the vault, and wrote on the defect card: *Heater front ticking. Wiper DS chatters.* His hand was steady. He was almost proud of that.

"Four-one-two, dispatch. You in? I've got you sitting at the pump."

"I'm in. Fuelling."

"Did you think about the five-forty?"

He looked at Ana. She had turned the phone over again, and the screen was empty.

"Put me down for it," he said.

"Bless you. Five-ten sign-on. And the Mountain's a skating rink, so drive safe going home."

"Will do."

He took out his phone and called Hamilton Cab. They said twenty minutes, maybe thirty with the ice. Then he took out his wallet. Inside, folded separately from his other money so he wouldn't spend it, were three twenties. That was the rebooking fee for Saturday, plus a little extra so Mia could get a coffee afterwards, pass or fail. He'd promised her that on the phone on Sunday, and he'd said it like it was nothing.

He held the money out toward the mirror before he could start thinking about it.

"Cab's coming to the gate. That's for the cab. And whatever."

Ana looked at the bills and didn't take them. "I can't."

"You can tell him where you're going. You don't have to tell me."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood up carefully with Theo still on her shoulder, came to the front, and took the money. She folded it into her coat pocket without counting it.

"She works nights," Ana said. "My sister. That's why she's not answering."

"Okay."

They waited on the bus with the doors shut and the heater ticking until he saw headlights swing in at the gate. Dale carried the duffel. It was heavier than it looked, and something hard was rolled up in the middle of the clothes, maybe a picture frame. Ana carried Theo. The boy's shoe blinked red against her hip the whole way across the yard. Dale didn't look toward the office window, where Rourke might or might not be standing.

The cab driver popped the trunk without getting out. Dale put the duffel in and shut it. Ana slid into the back seat with Theo on her lap and leaned forward to say something to the driver. Dale stepped back so he wouldn't hear it.

She rolled the window down a few inches.

"Middle of the back window," she said. "Then full lock."

"Got it."

The cab pulled out onto Barton and went west, toward the city. Its tail lights got small, then turned and disappeared.

Across the road, the last westbound was sitting at the stop with its interior lights on and no one inside. Dale lifted his hand. The doors closed. It pulled away from the curb, and he watched it go all the way to the light at the next corner, where it turned green for it.

He checked his phone. It was 1:14. He opened the thread with Mia. Her last message was from Sunday, a thumbs-up and the word *Saturday!!* He typed: *Pull up even with the car in front. Back up till you see their back bumper in the middle of your back window. Then full lock.*

He read it twice. He didn't add anything about the money. He sent it.

Then he zipped his jacket up to the chin, put his hands in his pockets, and started west along Barton, walking on the frozen ruts where the salt hadn't reached.

The Last Stop

The thaw had turned the streets to slush, and the windshield wipers were fighting a losing battle. Dale Varga adjusted the heat, but the bus’s cabin stayed cold, the kind of cold that seeped into your bones and never quite left. He checked the clock on the dash—2:17 AM. Three more hours until relief. Three more hours of watching the same stretches of sidewalk, the same faces, the same tired eyes in the rearview.

He pulled up to the hospital stop and the doors hissed open. A woman stepped on, her duffel bag slung over one shoulder, a little boy clinging to her other hand. The boy—Theo, she’d said when Dale asked—was about four, with dark curls and a face that looked too old for his age. He stumbled up the steps, his sneakers squeaking on the metal, and immediately curled against his mother’s side, his eyelids already drooping.

Ana. That was her name. She’d gotten on at the hospital before, always with the same duffel, always heading east. She never gave a street, just said her sister’s and checked her phone like it held the answer to something. Dale had stopped asking.

“Evening,” he said, nodding at her.

She nodded back, her fingers tapping absently on her phone screen. “Hey.”

Theo’s head lolled against her arm, his breath slow and even. Ana shifted, cradling him closer, her gaze flicking to the empty seats at the back. She didn’t sit. Just stood there, swaying slightly with the bus’s motion, her free hand gripping the pole.

Dale pulled away from the curb. The route was quiet tonight—just the usual handful of riders, the ones who rode late because they had no choice. A man in a stained hoodie snored in the back, his head against the window. A college kid with headphones on dozed in the middle. No one else.

The bus hummed under him, the engine a steady growl, the heater clicking like a metronome. Dale adjusted the thermostat again, but the heat didn’t kick in. He sighed. Another thing to deal with when he got back to the garage.

He glanced in the mirror. Ana was still standing, her thumb scrolling on her phone. Theo’s eyes fluttered open for a second, then shut again.

“You heading far?” Dale asked, just to say something.

Ana looked up. “Yeah. East side.”

“Sister’s place?”

She hesitated. “Yeah.”

Dale didn’t press. He knew better than to ask questions on this route. People got on, people got off, and most of them didn’t want to talk. But Ana always got off at the same stop—the one before the garage. He’d seen her do it before.

The bus rumbled over the King Street bridge, the water below black and still. The city lights blurred in the slush on the windshield. Dale wiped a smear with his glove, but it just smeared worse.

Carla’s voice crackled over the radio. “Varga, you got a relief at 0500. You’re good.”

Dale exhaled. Relief meant he could finally go home. His apartment was a shoebox with a space heater that wheezed like an old man, but it was home. Or at least, it was until the bank took the car. He hadn’t told anyone about that. Not Carla. Not his daughter, Jess. Not even Ana, though she probably wouldn’t care.

He checked the route display. Still two stops before the garage. Ana hadn’t moved from her spot by the doors.

The bus lurched as he turned onto Cannon, the tires splashing through a puddle. Theo stirred, his eyes blinking up at his mother.

“You okay, buddy?” Dale asked, keeping his voice low.

Ana nodded, but her grip on Theo tightened. The boy didn’t answer. Just burrowed closer.

Dale turned onto Wentworth, the last stretch before the garage. The streets were empty here, the buildings dark. A flickering streetlamp cast long shadows on the sidewalk.

Ana shifted Theo in her arms, her phone still clutched in her other hand. She looked at Dale in the mirror, her expression unreadable.

“You sure you’re going to your sister’s?” he asked, because sometimes people lied, and sometimes lies mattered.

Ana’s fingers stilled on the phone. “Yeah.”

Dale nodded. He didn’t believe her. But it wasn’t his business.

The bus slowed as he pulled into the garage lot. The automatic doors groaned open, the fluorescent lights inside too bright after the dark streets. Dale killed the engine and stood, stretching his back. The heater clicked one last time before going silent.

Ana didn’t move. Theo was still asleep, his head tucked against her shoulder.

Dale turned to face her. “This is your stop.”

Ana swallowed. “I know.”

A beat of silence. Then she shifted Theo in her arms, her jaw tightening.

Dale waited. He’d seen this before—the hesitation, the way people lingered like they were trying to decide whether to jump or not.

Ana finally stepped toward the doors. But instead of getting off, she turned back to him, her eyes dark in the dim light.

“Can you do me a favor?” she asked.

Dale crossed his arms. “Depends.”

She hesitated again, then pulled a folded bill from her pocket. A twenty. “Can you hold onto this? Just for a little while.”

Dale frowned. “Why?”

Ana’s gaze flicked to Theo, then back to him. “I’ll come get it. I promise.”

Dale studied her. There was something in her voice—something tight, something scared. He reached out and took the money, tucking it into his shirt pocket. “You sure?”

Ana nodded. “Yeah.”

She stepped off the bus, Theo still asleep in her arms. The doors hissed shut behind her.

Dale watched her walk away, her duffel bag swinging at her side, her shoulders hunched against the cold. She didn’t look back.

He turned back to the wheel, his fingers drumming on the dash. The twenty in his pocket felt heavy.

Carla’s voice came over the radio again. “Varga, you’re clear. See you tomorrow.”

Dale exhaled. He should just drive the bus into the garage, park it, and go home. That’s what he was supposed to do.

But Ana’s face flashed in his mind—the way she’d held Theo, the way she’d asked for the favor like it was the last lifeline she had.

He checked the mirror one more time. She was gone, swallowed by the dark.

Dale made his decision.

He put the bus in drive and pulled forward, the tires crunching over the slush. He turned the bus around in the lot, the engine groaning, and headed back out onto Wentworth.

He didn’t know where Ana lived. But he knew the east side. And he knew how to find a sister’s place.

The twenty in his pocket burned.

Deadhead to Wentworth

At ten-fifteen on Thursday night, the slush along Main Street East had finished turning back into stone. Two days of forty-degree rain had stripped the grey snowbanks down to their gravel ribs, and now the temperature had dropped hard enough that every puddle at the curb wore a skin of black glass. When Dale Varga brought the forty-foot Nova bus into the bay at MacNab, the front tires cracked the crust in the gutter with a sound like a boot going through a crate.

He popped the kneel switch, watched the amber light blink on the dash, and waited while two teenagers in soaked canvas sneakers clambered aboard. One of them tapped a Presto card that chirped red. The boy tapped it again, slower, as if the plastic needed time to think, while the defroster fan under Dale’s left knee kept up its dry, rhythmic tick—a bent fin catching the housing every third revolution.

"Go ahead," Dale said, before the kid could start digging through his hoodie. "Just take a seat."

The radio handset beside the steering column gave a short squelch. "Control to fifty-one-oh-four," Carla said. Her voice had the flat, gravelly drag it always got after nine o’clock, fuelled by weak breakroom coffee and three packs of menthols a week. "You copy, Dale?"

Dale keyed the thumb switch. "Fifty-one-oh-four. At MacNab, heading east."

"Put thirty seconds of drag on your loop at Gage. Eleven-twelve threw an alternator belt behind the Centre on Barton, and I got nobody in the yard to cover the gap till Henderson comes off split. Keep your spacing wide."

"Ten-four," Dale said. "Wide to the barn."

"You still parking near the Wentworth gate?" Carla asked. "My nephew’s Civic has a soft tire in the back lot. Wanted to know if you still had that little twelve-volt compressor in your trunk."

Dale’s thumb stayed half an inch above the transmit button. A draft leaked around the rubber seal of the driver’s window, cold against his jaw. The blue Hyundai Elantra—along with the twelve-volt compressor, an ice scraper with a broken red handle, and an extra pair of winter work gloves in the spare-tire well—had been winched onto a flatbed out of his apartment lot on Concession back in October.

"Left it at my place," Dale said into the mic. "Trunk latch has been sticking since the freeze."

"Figures," Carla said. "See you at one-ten. Control out."

He released the air brakes and eased out into the lane. Thursday the nineteenth of February: three hours left on the board, two full loops down to Eastgate and back to the downtown transfer, then the deadhead run into the Wentworth garage. At forty-seven, after eleven years with the city, Dale knew the grade of every crown in the asphalt between Dundurn and Stoney Creek. He knew which manholes sat half an inch too high after the frost heaved them, and he knew that if he clocked out at one-ten sharp and walked the three blocks down to Barton with his collar turned up over his transit patch, he could catch the last westbound night bus at one-twenty-two without any of the relief guys seeing him board.

His phone vibrated once inside his breast pocket. When he hit the red light at Wellington, he glanced down at the screen without taking it all the way out. It was a text from Paige: Still good for Saturday? Need to get the calc workbook from Indigo before five.

He typed back Yes with his thumb before the light turned green. The workbook was thirty-eight dollars plus tax, and they always went to the noodle place on King William afterward. Inside his wallet, tucked behind his commercial licence, were two twenties and a ten that he had taken out of the machine on payday so the bank’s automatic overdraft sweep wouldn’t eat them before Saturday afternoon.

At Victoria Avenue, the amber beacon above the St. Joseph’s clinic shelter was flashing.

Most nights after ten, the hospital stop meant a nurse coming off shift in fleece-lined clogs or an orderly smoking ten feet from the glass. Tonight a woman stood just inside the windbreak with a navy canvas duffel wedged between her shins and a small boy hoisted against her collarbone. She wasn't wearing a toque. Her dark hair was pulled back with a plastic claw clip, and a grey wool coat hung open over a thin cotton scrub top that didn't look like it belonged to her—the V-neck sagged sideways across one collarbone, showing a crescent of yellow bruise just above the hollow of her throat.

Dale pulled the nose of the bus tight to the curb and dropped the suspension.

The woman hefted the duffel by one strap—the zipper down the middle was safety-pinned shut over the bulge of a stuffed dinosaur tail—and stepped onto the treadplate. The boy was maybe four. He had on rubber slush boots with no laces and a red puffer jacket zipped to his chin, and his face was buried in the wool below her ear, fast asleep.

She set the bag down between her feet and fumbled in her coat pocket with her free hand, her fingers stiff from the wind. She pulled out a crumpled five-dollar bill and a loonie, then dropped the loonie. It rolled on its edge past the yellow line and clattered against the metal base of Dale’s seat cage.

"Sorry," she said, starting to bend with the sleeping boy slipping down her hip. "Hold on, Theo, don't—"

"Leave it," Dale said, reaching down with his boot heel to trap the coin and kick it back toward her toe. "Little guy’s under five, he rides free anyway. Drop the five in if you don't need change, or keep it until you get one of the new cards."

She hesitated, looking at the farebox glass as if she expected an alarm to go off, then pushed the five through the slot. "I'm Ana," she said, though drivers didn't ask names. She hitched the boy higher. "We're just going to my sister's out east."

"Take a seat behind the bulkhead, out of the draft," Dale said, nodding toward the sideways priority bench. "What cross street are you looking for?"

"Out past Kenilworth," she said quickly. Her eyes flicked to the green digital clock above the windshield, then down to the smartphone clutched in her left hand. "She's just texting me which corner to walk from. She moved last month."

"Plenty of time," Dale said, pulling the door lever shut. "Sing out when you know."

They rolled past Emerald and Wentworth, the tires humming on the salted patches where the city spreaders had come through at dusk. In the big convex interior mirror, Dale watched Ana settle onto the priority bench. She didn't take her coat off. She wedged the duffel beneath her knees, kept one arm locked around Theo’s back, and tapped her thumb against the side button of her phone. The screen lit her chin with a pale blue square. She stared at it for five seconds, scrolled half an inch with her thumb, then locked it again.

The two teenagers got off at Gage, leaving a wet ring of road salt on the ribbed rubber floor. At Ottawa Street, an old man in a tweed cap boarded with two plastic grocery bags full of oranges and rode four stops to Harmony. As the old man stood by the rear doors waiting for the brake interlock to hiss, he looked down at Theo’s red puffer coat.

"Out like a stone," the old man said to Ana. "My grandson used to sleep best over a diesel engine. You could run a vacuum under his crib, he'd scream the roof down. Put him in the back of an Econoline van, he was dead to the world by the end of the driveway."

Ana managed a tight, polite smile, her hand coming up automatically to cover the boy’s ear, though the knit cuff of his sleeve was already pulled down over his fingers. "He had a long afternoon," she said.

"Wait till they're sixteen," the old man said cheerfully as the rear doors folded open. "Then they sleep till two on a Sunday and eat a whole loaf of bread standing by the sink. Night, driver."

"Watch the ice on the curb," Dale called back.

At Kenilworth, Dale slowed by the illuminated shelter outside the discount pharmacy. Nobody was waiting. In the convex mirror, Ana didn't look up at the street sign. Her thumb tapped the side button of her phone again.

"That's Kenilworth," Dale said over his shoulder, pitching his voice low so it wouldn't carry past the boy’s hood. "We're coming up on Parkdale in five minutes, then Queenston."

"Right," Ana said. She didn't look up into the mirror. "She said closer to the mall. She’s still finishing her shift at the bakery, she's gonna meet us at the corner."

Dale looked at the dashboard clock. It was ten-fifty-two. No bakeries on the Queenston strip stayed open past eight on a Thursday in February.

He didn't say so. When Paige was seven, the first winter after the split, Dale had stood with her in the lobby of the Westdale theatre for forty minutes after a matinee, telling her that her mother was just stuck behind a train at the Aberdeen crossing, while he stared at a dead phone screen with twelve percent battery and a voicemail he’d already listened to twice. You didn't poke holes in what someone said when a kid was within earshot, even a sleeping one.

They reached Eastgate Square at eleven-fifteen. The terminal platform was nearly empty, swept by a raw wind coming off the escarpment that smelled of wet slag from the steel mills down by the harbour. Dale set the parking brake and stood up to stretch his lower back, rolling his shoulders until the cartilage popped.

"End of the line for ten minutes," he said, turning around in the aisle. "Then this bus turns back west toward downtown, makes one more loop out here, and pulls into the garage."

Ana’s knees tightened around the canvas duffel. Theo stirred against her chest, his mouth opening slightly, a damp dark curl sticking to his forehead. On his left wrist, peeking out half an inch from the frayed elastic of the red jacket, was a strip of white plastic hospital tape with a barcode printed on it.

"Can we stay on till it warms up?" she asked. Her voice stayed level, almost casual, as if they were discussing a transfer window. "My sister’s car wouldn't start in the lot. She’s waiting for a boost from her neighbour, then she’s picking us up back down near Sherman."

Dale looked at the white plastic band on the boy's wrist, then at the safety pin holding the duffel shut. "Sure," he said. "Heat works better when the engine's idling high anyway. I'm just stepping out to check the tires."

He walked the length of the coach on the concrete island, kicking the rear duals out of habit and listening to the heater fan tick through the open door. In his pocket, he touched the edge of his wallet. On his last loop west to MacNab and back east again, only four other riders boarded the entire way—a line cook from a bar on James who smelled of fryer oil, a woman with a small terrier zipped inside her parka, and two men in reflective vests who rode from Victoria to Gage without speaking.

Through it all, Ana sat behind the bulkhead. Every three or four blocks she pressed the side button on her phone. At midnight, when they stopped at the red light under the railway bridge on Birch, the overhead interior lamps reflected off the dark glass behind her head. Dale glanced into the mirror and caught the inverted reflection of her phone screen against the window.

There were no text bubbles on the glass. She was looking at the lock screen wallpaper—a photograph of the boy in grass with a yellow bucket—and in the upper left corner, where the carrier name belonged, the tiny white letters read No SIM.

The light turned green. Dale took his foot off the brake and fed the diesel.

"Control to all late units," Carla’s voice crackled over the speaker at twelve-forty. "Salt trucks are back out on the Claremont access and the Jolly Cut. Watch your braking distance on the downgrades. Fifty-one-oh-four, you're cleared direct to Wentworth garage from Eastgate. Skip the deadhead loop on Barton, they want your unit over the pit by one-fifteen so the night crew can swap that blower motor."

Dale reached for the handset. The heater fan under his knee went tick-tick-tick, faster now that he had the blower on high to keep the frost off the lower windshield.

"Fifty-one-oh-four," Dale said. "Copy direct to Wentworth."

At Eastgate for the final time, the platform lights clicked onto half-power, casting long orange shadows across the frozen slush. Twelve-fifty-five. Dale didn't open the rear doors. He left the transmission in neutral, set the yellow parking knob, and turned in his seat.

"Last run's done," he said. "They called me straight into the barn at Wentworth and Sanford."

Ana’s head came up. The claw clip in her hair had slipped crooked against the vinyl headrest, and the skin under her eyes had the grey, papery look of someone who hadn't slept since Wednesday. "You go past Sherman on the way?"

"Down Main to Wentworth, then north to the yard," Dale said. "Look, it’s twenty below with the wind off the bay, and nothing's open on Sherman."

"She's almost there," Ana said. Her knuckles went white around the phone, her thumb pressing the side button so hard the nail turned pale. She didn't turn the screen toward him. "She just—the traffic on the mountain access was backed up."

"The salt trucks are the only thing on the access right now," Dale said quietly.

She looked down at the crown of Theo’s head. The boy’s breathing was slow and heavy, his small boots dangling six inches above the floor. For three seconds nobody spoke, and the only sound in the bus was the bent fin of the defroster fan striking its metal housing—tick, tick, tick—and the air dryer underneath the chassis letting go with a sharp pneumatic sneeze.

"I can take you as far as the front gate at Wentworth," Dale said, turning back to the wheel before she had to answer. "After that I have to pull over the inspection pit."

He drove the last two miles in the curb lane at twenty-five miles an hour, catching every red light on purpose so the boy could have another ten minutes of eighty-degree air from the floor vents. When he swung the nose of fifty-one-oh-four through the open chain-link gates of the Wentworth garage at one-oh-eight, the yard floodlights turned the bus interior as bright as an operating room. Rows of striped city buses sat nose-to-tail along the fence, exhaust pluming white into the dark.

Dale stopped just short of the big yellow rollup doors of Bay Three. Inside the glass booth by the pedestrian gate, he could see Miller from the night maintenance crew holding a clipboard and waiting to wave him onto the grease pit.

If Dale cleared the bus now, signed his log sheet at the dispatch window by one-twelve, and walked fast down Wentworth to Barton, he would make the one-twenty-two night bus heading west with four minutes to spare. He’d be inside his apartment by two, with eight hours before his Thursday-Friday turnaround and fifty dollars still folded in his wallet for Paige on Saturday. If he missed the one-twenty-two, there wasn't another westbound bus until the five-fifteen steelworker run.

He reached overhead, flipped the destination sign toggle to NOT IN SERVICE, and pulled the main farebox vault key on its chain to print the end-of-shift tally slip. The tiny thermal printer whirred, jammed on a curled edge of paper, and beeped twice.

"Come on," Dale muttered. He popped the small metal cover with his thumb, pinched the paper roll to straighten the feed, and clicked the latch shut until the three-inch strip of numbers spat out into his palm.

In the mirror, Ana was standing up, trying to slide her left arm through the duffel strap without waking Theo. When the boy’s weight shifted, his head rolled back against her shoulder and he whimpered once, his eyes staying shut, one hand clutching a fistful of her grey coat.

"Door's open?" she asked, her voice small against the roar of the garage exhaust fans outside.

Dale looked at the tally slip in his hand, then at the digital clock: 1:11.

"Hold on," Dale said.

He didn't open the door. He pulled his own phone from his breast pocket, opened the browser, and typed in the dispatch number for the Hamilton East cab stand on Barton. Before he hit call, he turned halfway around in the seat.

"That hotel by the Red Hill ramp—the one with the weekly rooms and the twenty-four-hour desk," Dale said. "Or the St. Martha’s shelter on Wellington. Which one do you have ID for?"

Ana froze with the duffel strap halfway up her forearm. The defensive stiffness went out of her shoulders all at once, leaving her looking five years younger and half as tall. "Martha's," she whispered. "My health card's in the bag. My—he took my purse off the table before we left, so I only had what was in my coat."

"Sit down a second," Dale said. "Don't wake him up yet."

He dialed the cab company and waited through four rings while Miller stepped out of the maintenance booth in his earmuffs and slapped his gloved hands together, pointing at the Bay Three rollup door. Dale held up one finger through the windshield—one minute—and spoke into his phone when the cab dispatcher picked up.

"Need a sedan at the Wentworth transit garage, pedestrian gate on the north side," Dale said. "Going to Wellington North. Yeah, right now."

He hung up, unclipped his seatbelt, and stood in the stairwell by the farebox. He took out his wallet, angled his body so the overhead camera by the destination box only saw his back, and slid the two twenties and the ten out from behind his commercial licence.

He stepped back to the priority bench and held the three bills out flat, folded once to the size of a matchbook.

"Cab's twelve dollars from here to Wellington," Dale said. "Give him the ten and five out of a twenty. Use the rest for a phone card at the variety store in the morning so you can call your sister for real."

Ana stared at the green and purple bills in his hand. Her throat worked. "I can't pay you back. I don't even know your name."

"Take it before the mechanic comes out here and writes me up for blocking the wash bay," Dale said. When she still didn't move, he tucked the folded bills into the side pocket of the navy duffel bag, right beside the safety pin. "They have cribs at Martha's, and the night coordinator's name is Brenda. Used to ride my route on the mountain."

He turned back to the console and hit the pneumatic release. The front doors folded open with a rush of frigid air that smelled of diesel soot and frozen mud.

Ana stepped down the stairwell carefully, turning sideways so Theo’s slush boots wouldn't catch on the yellow handrail. On the pavement outside, she adjusted the boy’s hood over his ears and looked back up at the high driver’s window. She didn't say thank you; she just gave a single, sharp nod, as if they had agreed on a schedule, and walked toward the lit pedestrian gate where a bruised white Crown Victoria taxi was already turning into the drive with its roof lamp glowing orange.

Dale closed the doors, released the brake, and eased fifty-one-oh-four through the rollup bay onto the steel tracks over the inspection pit.

"Took your time, Varga," Miller called up from the concrete apron, hooking the yellow exhaust hose onto the tailpipe. "Blower motor's ticking like a grandfather clock."

"Bent fin on the squirrel cage," Dale said, shutting down the master ignition switches one by one until the dashboard went dark and the ticking under his left knee finally stopped. "Needs a five-sixteenths wrench and ten minutes."

By the time he dropped his defect card and the thermal tally slip into Carla’s tray at the dispatch window, the clock on the cinderblock wall read one-twenty-four. Down on Barton, four blocks south, the last westbound bus was already past Sherman.

"Night, Dale," Carla said without looking up from her run sheets. "Watch the lot, it’s slick by the back fence."

"Night, Carla," he said.

He went into the locker room, pulled his navy wool toque down to his eyebrows, and wrapped his scarf twice around his chin before zipping his winter shell to the throat. Outside the pedestrian gate, the white taxi was gone, leaving two fresh tracks through the grey frost at the curb.

Dale took his phone out with his gloved hand before the battery could freeze on the walk. He opened his messages to Paige and typed: Hey kiddo. Let's do pancakes at my place Saturday morning instead of King William. Pick up the workbook Monday after school, my treat.

He hit send, shoved the phone deep into his inside pocket, and turned west down the middle of the sidewalk on Wentworth. It was four miles to the mountain stairs at Dundurn and three hundred steps up the steel risers after that, and his boots crunched loud and steady through the rime with every stride.

The Last Stop

By nine o’clock, the thaw had turned the streets of Hamilton into a long, dirty reflection.

Dale Varga watched the traffic lights smear across the wet windshield as he brought the bus to a stop outside the General. Water ran along the curb in black streams, carrying cigarette ends and scraps of salt-stained paper. The temperature had dropped again. A skin of ice was forming over the puddles.

Three hours left.

He checked the mirror, the doors, the mirror again. The bus was half full. An old man in a blue work jacket sat behind the front wheel well, holding a paper bag between his boots. Two teenagers shared earbuds near the back. A woman in a long coat stood by the middle doors, looking at the hospital entrance.

Dale had driven this route for eleven years. He knew the pauses in the traffic, the stops where people ran for the bus with their coats half buttoned, the corners where the rear wheels needed room. He knew which intersections flooded after a thaw and which stretch of Barton always seemed to be under construction.

He also knew how long it took to get home from the garage without a car.

The thought came automatically now, whenever he approached the end of a shift. He'd learned the connections, the last departures, the stops where he could wait indoors. He'd memorized the schedules on his phone and stopped looking at them in public. There was no reason for anyone at work to know. Carla had never asked. His daughter hadn't either.

The repossession had happened in October, while he was at the dentist. By the time he got home, the car was gone and the neighbours had seen the truck. He'd told his daughter the transmission had finally died. She'd said she'd known it was coming. He hadn't corrected her.

The woman at the hospital stop boarded with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a small boy pressed against her hip. The boy's head lolled sleepily against her coat. She had a paper cup in one hand and a phone in the other.

She tapped a fare card against the reader. It gave a long red beep.

"Sorry," she said.

"Try again."

She did. Red.

"Card's not reading," Dale said.

She turned it over, wiped the chip against her sleeve and tried once more. The reader flashed green.

"Thank you."

He nodded and closed the doors.

The woman took a seat near the front, leaving the duffel on the floor between her shoes. The boy was wearing a grey knitted hat with a little pom-pom. One of his mittens was missing. She tucked his bare hand into the sleeve of her own coat.

Dale pulled away.

The old man in the blue jacket got off at James. The teenagers stayed on, their heads bent together. The woman checked her phone, turned it face down, then picked it up again.

At the next red light, Dale glanced over.

"Long night?"

She looked up, startled. "A bit."

"Hospital shift?"

"No. Just leaving."

He let the light change before he answered. "Right."

The boy stirred and lifted his head. His eyes were dark and unfocused.

"Are we there?"

"Not yet, Theo."

"Is this the one with the big stairs?"

"Not this one."

He settled against her again.

Dale watched them in the mirror. The woman's hair was pulled into a loose knot. There was a small white strip of tape near her wrist, the kind used to cover a puncture mark. Her coat was open, though it was cold enough to make his own fingers ache.

"Where are you headed?" he asked when the traffic slowed.

"My sister's."

"Which part of the city?"

"East end."

"Okay. This one runs out to Parkdale and then finishes at the garage."

She nodded.

"There's a connection at Parkdale, depending where you're going."

"That's fine."

He waited for more, but she had returned to her phone.

The bus rattled over a patch of broken pavement. Somewhere behind him, the heater began its familiar ticking. It had done that for three weeks, a little metallic sound that came from beneath the driver's side panel. Tick, tick, tick. The shop had replaced a valve, then a relay, and the sound had returned the following morning.

Dale tapped the side of the panel with his boot. It stopped.

"Is something wrong?" the woman asked.

"Just the heater."

"It sounds like a clock."

"That's what I keep telling them."

She smiled faintly. The boy opened one eye.

"What's wrong with the clock?"

"Nothing," she said. "It's just noisy."

He looked at Dale. "Our clock doesn't make noise."

"Then you've got a good one."

The boy considered this, then looked toward the windows.

"Do you like dinosaurs?" Dale asked.

A small nod.

"Which one's your favourite?"

"Triceratops."

"Good choice."

"Because it's got three horns."

"Can't argue with that."

The woman looked at him, and this time her smile reached her eyes.

"He knows more about them than I do," she said.

"He's probably got a better collection than me."

"You have dinosaurs?"

"Not anymore. My daughter had a plastic one when she was little. Used to leave it in the bathtub. Nearly gave me a heart attack one night."

"What was it?"

"Some kind of long-necked thing. Big enough to make me think there was an animal in the water."

"Brontosaurus," Theo said.

"Could've been."

"Brontosaurus isn't a real dinosaur anymore."

Dale raised his eyebrows. "What happened to it?"

"They changed its name."

"That's rough."

Theo gave a solemn nod.

For a few minutes, the bus moved through the east end without conversation. The boy watched the passing shops. The woman held her phone low in her lap, her thumb resting against the screen.

Dale passed a pharmacy with its metal shutters down. A convenience store was still open, its fluorescent lights making the wet pavement look green. At the next stop, a young man boarded carrying a pizza box. He sat across from the woman and spent the next five minutes trying to keep the box level while texting.

The heater started ticking again.

Dale turned the temperature control down a notch. The noise continued.

His radio crackled.

"Thirty-eight, you copy?"

"Go ahead, Carla."

"Relief for the King run is confirmed. Don't need you to take the extra loop. Just finish regular and bring it in."

"Copy."

"And make sure you log the defect on that heater again. Maintenance says they haven't seen it."

"Tell them to sit in it for a shift."

Carla gave a tired laugh. "I'll pass that along."

The radio went quiet.

He'd known Carla for eight years. She had worked dispatch for longer than he'd been driving. She could tell from his voice whether he'd had coffee, whether a passenger had been difficult, whether he'd been stuck behind a delivery truck. She didn't ask personal questions, which was one reason he liked her.

The woman checked her phone again. The screen lit her face blue.

A call came in. She looked at the name, then rejected it.

A moment later the phone vibrated. She read the message and pressed the side button.

Theo shifted in his seat.

"Can I have my drink?"

"Not right now."

"I'm thirsty."

"I know. We'll get you one."

"When?"

"Soon."

Dale watched the road.

At Parkdale, the young man with the pizza box got off. The woman stood, gathering the duffel. She looked at the stop sign, then at the dark stretch of road beyond it.

"This is the connection?" she asked.

"No. You want to stay on."

She sat again.

"How much farther?"

"About twenty minutes."

She lowered herself into the seat with a carefulness that made Dale think her legs hurt. The boy was asleep now, his cheek against her coat. She pulled the missing mitten from a side pocket of the duffel and slipped it onto his hand.

The phone vibrated again.

She didn't look at it.

Dale turned onto the next stretch of Barton. The traffic had thinned. A truck passed in the opposite direction, throwing a sheet of water against the curb. The bus rocked through the spray.

He thought of the meal waiting in his refrigerator: half a container of soup, bread that was probably stale, a carton of eggs. He'd told his daughter he would call her after work. She wanted to know whether he could come by on Sunday. He'd said he'd see how he felt. She'd taken that to mean he might be driving.

He'd considered telling her the truth more than once. The first time had been in November, when she'd asked if he could pick her up from a friend's house. The second had been Christmas, when she'd given him a small emergency car kit. He'd put it in a drawer.

The bus hit a pothole. The woman caught the duffel before it slid.

"Sorry," Dale said.

"It's okay."

"Road's a mess tonight."

"I know."

A few stops later, Theo woke. He stared at the window, then at the dark reflection of his own face.

"Is Daddy coming?"

The woman went still for a moment.

"No, sweetheart."

"When?"

"Not tonight."

"He said he'd come."

"I know."

Theo looked down at his mitten.

Dale kept his eyes on the road. He could hear the heater ticking, louder now that the other passengers had got off. He wondered whether to say something, then decided against it.

The phone lit again. A preview appeared across the top of the screen.

I can see your location.

The woman turned it face down.

Dale checked the mirror. She was looking at him, not the road.

"How much longer?" she asked.

"Ten minutes or so."

She glanced toward the windshield. "Does this go past the station?"

"Which station?"

"The bus station."

"No. We're heading to the garage now."

"Oh."

"If you need to get downtown, I can tell you where to transfer."

"No. That's okay."

He wondered why she'd asked. There was nowhere to go from the garage except into the yard or back onto the road. He'd explained the route when she'd boarded.

At the next stop, a man in a brown parka climbed aboard, shook the wet off his hood and dropped into the first empty seat. He was carrying a thermos and a folded newspaper.

"Cold one," he said.

"Getting colder," Dale answered.

"After all that rain, too. My driveway's a skating rink."

"Watch your step."

The man laughed and opened his newspaper. The front page was creased down the middle. He glanced at the woman's duffel, then at the sleeping boy, and returned to the paper.

The bus continued east.

Dale's final time point came and went. He called out the stop, waited, opened the doors. No one got off. When he pulled away, the clock above the dashboard read 11:47.

He had twenty minutes before the garage.

Carla came back on the radio.

"Thirty-eight, your pull-in is showing twelve-oh-eight."

"That's right."

"Security needs the bay clear by twelve-fifteen. They're bringing a contractor through."

"Understood."

"Don't forget your paperwork."

"I won't."

The woman had gone back to her phone. Her thumb moved across the screen, stopped, moved again. She seemed to be reading something for a long time.

Dale glanced at the route display. The bus was nearly empty. The man with the thermos had got off. Only the woman and Theo remained.

At the next red light, the boy woke again.

"Are we at Auntie's?"

"Almost."

"Is she making pancakes?"

"Maybe."

"With chocolate chips?"

"We'll see."

He smiled, then rested his head against her arm.

Dale caught the woman's eye in the mirror. She looked away.

The heater clicked in a steady rhythm. He left it alone.

By the time the bus reached the industrial stretch near the garage, the temperature had fallen below freezing. The roads shone under the streetlights. Dale took the turn toward Wentworth, slowed for the railway crossing and watched a freight train crawl through the dark. Its wheels hammered one after another, a long uneven rhythm that filled the bus.

Theo slept through it.

The garage gates were open. A security light swept across the windshield. Beyond the fence, buses stood in rows, their windows dark. A mechanic in reflective coveralls walked between two parked vehicles with a clipboard tucked under one arm.

Dale brought the bus to a stop outside the entrance.

The clock read 12:09.

He set the brake, put the transmission in neutral and switched off the route display. The bus gave a little shudder as the engine dropped to idle.

"End of the line," he said.

The woman looked up.

"Sorry?"

"We're at the garage."

She stared through the windshield. The duffel was still at her feet. Theo was asleep against her shoulder.

"Oh."

Dale opened the front doors.

She didn't move.

He waited. The cold entered around the door seals, carrying the smell of wet concrete and diesel. He could see the security guard in the booth beyond the gate, looking down at a monitor.

"Ma'am?"

She looked at him.

"You need to get off here."

"Can I stay a minute?"

"I have to bring the bus in."

"Just a minute."

He looked at the clock. Twelve-ten. His bus connection left from Barton at twelve-eighteen. He had enough time to walk to the stop if he got out now. The next bus wasn't until after midnight-thirty, and the stop had no shelter. He'd be standing in the wind, waiting for headlights.

"Where's your sister?" he asked.

"She lives near here."

"Which street?"

The woman lowered her eyes.

"Just near here."

"Do you know the address?"

She pressed her lips together. Her phone vibrated in her hand. She didn't look at it.

Dale could hear the traffic from the road behind them. A car passed through the intersection and disappeared. The bus idled, heater ticking.

"Does she know you're coming?" he asked.

The woman smoothed Theo's hair.

"She's probably asleep."

It was an answer, but not to the question.

"Can you call her?"

"I don't want to wake her."

Dale looked at the duffel. It was small, too small for a week away, with one strap twisted around the handle. There was a child's water bottle in the side pocket. A hospital envelope stuck out of the top, its corner bent and damp.

He looked back at the woman. Her hands had started to shake. She put her phone in her coat pocket and wrapped both arms around Theo.

He had seen people sleep on buses before. People who didn't want to go home. People who'd missed the last connection and sat with their faces against the glass until the driver told them to leave. Most accepted it. Some got angry. A few asked to stay until the first morning service.

The garage wasn't a place to stay. The drivers weren't supposed to bring passengers inside. Security would see them, and there would be questions. He could open the doors, point toward the pavement and get on with his shift.

He could also leave them standing beside the fence, with the boy half asleep and the woman holding a duffel bag she hadn't put down all night.

His radio crackled.

"Thirty-eight, you're sitting at the gate. Everything okay?"

Dale took the microphone.

"I've got two passengers still aboard."

A pause.

"Two passengers?"

"Mother and child. I'm trying to sort it out."

"You're clear to bring it in. Security's waiting."

"I need a few minutes."

"Dale, we need that bay."

He looked at the woman. She was watching him now, but she didn't ask him to do anything.

"Can you put me through to the supervisor?" he said.

Carla exhaled over the radio. "I'll call him. Don't open the doors again until you've got it sorted."

Dale put the microphone down.

The woman bent to pick up the duffel.

"I can get off," she said.

"Where are you going?"

She looked toward the dark road.

"Somewhere."

He didn't answer.

He reached across to the console and switched the passenger heater up. The ticking grew louder. He tried the control again, then gave up. He took his phone from the pocket of his jacket and opened the transit employee page. There was a number for after-hours incident support. He'd used it twice in eleven years, once after a passenger had fallen and once when a man had punched a window.

He called.

The first person who answered asked for his operator number, bus number and location. Dale gave them. He explained that two passengers had remained on board at the garage and that the woman seemed unsure where she was going. He didn't mention the message on her phone. He didn't know whether it mattered. He said the child was asleep and the woman hadn't asked for emergency services.

The person on the line asked him to stay with them while they arranged assistance.

Dale looked at the clock.

Twelve-fifteen.

His connection had gone.

He put the phone down and rubbed his thumb along the edge of the steering wheel. There was a crack in the vinyl where the seam had split. He'd been meaning to report it.

The woman was still watching him.

"You don't have to do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Wait."

"Looks like I do."

She looked down at Theo. "He's tired."

"Yeah."

"He doesn't usually fall asleep like this."

Dale nodded. He didn't ask what she meant.

The phone rang again. He answered. The woman on the line asked whether Ana had somewhere safe to wait. Dale hadn't known her name until then. He repeated the question to her.

She looked toward the windshield.

"There's a place," she said.

"Where?"

"Downtown."

"Do you know the address?"

She shook her head.

Dale gave the phone a small distance from his ear. The woman on the line asked if he could bring them to the security office. He said he could. She told him not to leave the vehicle unattended and said someone would meet him at the gate.

He hung up.

The security guard had come out of the booth. He was standing near the fence with his hands in his pockets, looking at the bus.

Dale switched off the engine.

The silence made the heater's ticking stop.

"All right," he said to Ana. "We'll go inside for a minute. There's someone coming."

She didn't move immediately. Then she bent and lifted the duffel.

Dale opened the front doors and stepped down onto the wet pavement. The cold caught him across the face. He walked around to the curb and held out his hand toward Theo.

Ana hesitated.

"He can stay with you," Dale said.

She shifted the boy carefully. Theo's head rolled onto Dale's shoulder, his breath warm against the collar of Dale's uniform. He weighed less than Dale expected. His mitten had slipped halfway off.

The security guard approached.

"Everything okay?"

"Just need somewhere warm while we get a call sorted."

The guard glanced at Ana, then at the child.

"Office is open."

Dale followed him toward the pedestrian gate. Behind them, the bus stood with its front doors open and the headlights shining on the wet concrete. The duffel bumped against Ana's leg as she walked.

At the gate, Dale's phone vibrated.

A message from his daughter.

Sunday still good? I'll make coffee.

He read it, then put the phone away without answering. Theo stirred against his shoulder.

"Don't put me down," the boy mumbled.

"Not yet," Dale said.

The guard held the gate open. Beyond it, the garage smelled of hot rubber and old diesel. A radio played somewhere inside, low enough that Dale couldn't make out the song.

He stepped through with the boy in his arms, and Ana followed, carrying the bag.

The Westbound

At a quarter to nine on Thursday, February 19, Dale Varga took over a bus with a full tank and a wet seat. The relief driver said the seat was only melted snow. Dale pressed a paper towel to it and got a brown print the shape of Australia.

“Big country,” the relief said, putting on his gloves.

Dale folded the towel and pressed it down again. Three hours left. If he got through the garage gate at eleven forty-five and didn't get buttonholed by anybody, he could make the last westbound off the industrial road. He'd timed the walk in work boots: four minutes if the crossing went his way.

The thaw had taken the snowbanks down to the height of a curb, then the cold had come back and put a shine on everything. At the first stop Dale watched a man with a pizza box test the pavement with one heel before committing his whole weight. The man made it aboard and carried the box level all the way to the back. Dale drove the next corner like there was soup in it.

Carla called to ask if he'd seen his relief.

“He relieved me of a dry seat.”

“Then he's alive. Did he say where he was going?”

“Home, I hope.”

“He's got an extra piece at nine.” There was a pause in which Dale could hear her turning pages. “No, he doesn't. That's tomorrow.”

She let go of the radio without saying good night. Eleven years and he could tell from the length of the silence whether she was looking at paper or a screen.

At the hospital stop a woman brought a boy and a blue duffel aboard. The boy had his coat on over pyjamas and one of those hospital stickers with a smiling apple on it. The woman's fare card gave a hard double beep. She rubbed it against her sleeve and tried again.

“It's not dirty,” Dale said. “It's out of money.”

“Oh.” She set the duffel down and opened a pocket at the end. There were two toothbrushes in it, loose, and she zipped it right back up. “I have change somewhere.”

“Get him sitting first.”

The boy picked the seat just behind the front wheel. He put his palm on the glass to see how cold it was, which Dale knew because he announced the result. By the time they passed Wentworth he was asleep against his mother, his mouth open over the zipper of her coat. She counted out the fare over his knees, catching a dime in the fold of his pyjama leg. At the next light she brought it up in two small stacks.

“Does this go all the way east?” she asked.

“This trip turns at the mall. Last one goes in to the garage.”

“I'm going to my sister's.”

He waited a second for the rest. “If you tell me where, I can tell you which stop.”

“She can pick us up.”

The light changed. In the mirror he saw her sit down carefully so the boy's head would land back in the same spot.

By ten there were five people on the bus. A man in kitchen whites got off carrying his shoes in a grocery bag. Two girls debated whether a cake could be iced while it was warm. Dale wanted to tell them no. He had iced a warm cake once for his daughter's birthday, and the icing had slid down and sealed the cake to a dinner plate. She'd eaten the icing with a spoon while he sawed wedges out with a steak knife. She was twenty now and would have known to put the thing in the freezer.

At the mall the last of the others got off. Dale had six minutes at the stand. The woman took the boy's boots off and tucked his feet under her thigh.

“He's going to have wet socks,” Dale said.

“Other way round. His socks are wet already. He stood in a puddle at the hospital to see how deep it was.”

“How deep was it?”

“Exactly one boot.” She pulled one sock straight at the toes. It had a shark with its mouth open. “He told the nurse the other foot was going to do it on the way out.”

Dale laughed, a little louder than he meant to. The boy rearranged his face and slept on.

While Dale ate half a ham sandwich, the woman checked her phone three times. The third time she held it low against the boy's back and typed with one thumb. She had taken off her gloves but was still wearing a blue paper visitor's band around her wrist. Dale looked away before she could see him reading it.

“You staying on?” he asked when his six minutes were up.

“Is that allowed?”

“Till I pull in.”

“I'm Ana, by the way. Ruiz.” She said the last name after a little pause, as if he had a form out. “This is Theo.”

On the way back west the lower heater began to tick. It had done that on Monday and somebody had put a strip of felt under the grille. The felt was gone now. Dale could hear the tick even over the blower, regular enough that when it skipped one he found himself waiting for the next. At a red he took the defect slip from the clip and laid it on his knee.

His own phone stayed in his lunch bag until the next layover. There was a text from his daughter asking if he could come by Saturday and look at her wipers. *Don't buy anything yet*, she'd added. He'd bought her a set last winter that fitted according to the book at the store, and not according to the car. This time he had the part numbers in his wallet and forty dollars folded around them. He wrote *Sure*, then deleted *I'll drive over*. He'd been deleting little bits of sentences since October, when the finance company sent a man for the Pontiac. The man had given him five minutes to clean it out. He'd slid the ice scraper from under the seat and left the papers in the glove box, including the divorce agreement he'd been meaning to take inside for two years. It was easier with Carla, who still thought the rusty Pontiac in the far row was his. There were two rusty Pontiacs in the far row. He didn't even know whose she'd been looking at.

On the last trip east, Ana stood up at the stop across from the all-night pharmacy. She got the duffel strap onto her shoulder without waking Theo, then looked past Dale at the cars in the lot. A grey vehicle was idling by the pharmacy door. It might have been anything from where Dale sat, a station wagon or one of the low new vans. He opened the doors. Cold air reached his hands.

“Not this one,” she said, and sat down again.

He shut the doors. A block later her phone lit up against the dark window. She turned the screen inward, but not before he saw a map and the little blue dot. There were maps on everybody's phones. With her thumbnail she worked at the edge of the case until it came loose. Then she held the power button down.

Carla came on at eleven thirty-six. “Skip the loop. Water main at the south entrance. Go straight in off the service road. You carrying?”

“Two.”

“Can you let them off at the turn?”

Dale looked at the turn as it came up. The pole there had been knocked crooked by a plow. Behind it was forty feet of frozen grit and then the fence of a pipe yard.

“No,” he said. “I'll bring them in.”

“Keep out of lane three. They're washing the floor.”

The garage gate opened slowly enough for Dale to watch the minute change on the dash. He swung into the receiving lane at eleven forty-four. Under the lights he could see that Ana had got both boots back on Theo, though the tongues were bunched under the laces. She still had the duffel on her shoulder. Neither of them moved when he set the brake.

“This is it,” he said.

She nodded. “Is there a phone I could use?”

“I've got one.”

“Is there one that belongs to here?”

He stopped with his hand halfway to the lunch bag. Outside the bus, a man in rubber boots pushed a squeegee across the next lane. They weren't allowed to leave passengers in the receiving bay. The vestibule had a wall phone with a line out, but the outer door went to staff fobs after eleven. If he took them there and went for his bus, whoever came would have to get somebody inside to hear the buzzer over the wash. He'd stood outside with a forgotten fob once for eighteen minutes.

“Carla,” he said into the radio. “Can I put my two in the front vestibule to make a call?”

“Not by themselves.”

He could make the westbound if he left now. There was a cab app on his phone that he'd installed the week the Pontiac went, and he knew what the trip cost because he'd looked it up on the first really cold night and walked the last leg instead. Tonight the westbound would get him three blocks from his building. He could see himself on it, gloves on the defroster ledge if Ahmed was driving.

“I'm with them,” he said.

“Then fine. I need the number on that coach. Your run's coming up as a spare.”

He had to stand to see it, stencilled over the windscreen. He read it out and wrote it on the defect slip, along with *lower heater grille ticks on high*. He hadn't actually checked it on low. He turned the knob down and waited. The tick went on, very faint, while Ana bent over the boy to get an arm under his knees. Dale crossed out *on high*. Then he shut the bus down and the tick stopped before the fan did.

In the vestibule he showed Ana how to dial out and moved away enough that she could speak without lowering her voice. She lowered it anyway. Theo had woken up. He was facing a vending machine that held one packet of cheese crackers tipped forward against the glass. He put both hands flat on his thighs and leaned in from the waist to look at it.

Dale's phone showed the westbound leaving in one minute. He could still see the road from the vestibule, through the wire in the glass. A minute later the bus went past, lit up, the far windows fogged and the near ones clear. It wasn't Ahmed. Dale couldn't tell who it was.

The cab app quoted him thirty-eight dollars and sixty cents. He pressed the button. Then he took the folded bills out of his wallet anyway. The machine wanted two seventy-five for the crackers and only took coins. Theo watched him read the slot, then went back to looking at the packet.

Ana hung up. “She said to wait inside.”

“Okay.”

“Can you see the cars from there?”

He moved over to give her his place at the glass. From that angle she could see past the gate to the road, but anybody turning in would have the headlights full on her. She looked for a second and stepped back beside the vending machine.

His phone vibrated. The cab was six minutes out. He took two quarters and two loonies from his coat. Theo pointed at the floor.

“There's one in there.”

Dale could see something silver under the machine. He got down on one knee. At forty-seven he'd started making a noise doing that even when there was nobody around to appreciate it. He opened his wallet again and used the slip with the part numbers to rake the thing toward him. It was a dime. Further back was a quarter wedged upright against a rubber foot. He got the corner of the slip behind it.

“Here,” he said. “Put your hand flat.”

Theo went down on his stomach. Dale pulled the slip sideways and the quarter rolled out, right over the boy's fingers. Theo slapped his other hand down on it.

Bay Six

At three minutes past nine, Dale Varga pulled the wheel to the left and felt the bus slide, just a little, where the day’s meltwater had frozen across Barton Street.

He eased off the accelerator. Eleven years driving for the city had taught him that a bus did not need to be going fast to surprise him. Neither did a road. The thaw had left banked snow leaking into the gutters all afternoon; now the puddles had a skin on them, and the salt laid down at suppertime made white veins along the curb.

Thursday, 19 February. Three hours left on his shift, then the deadhead to the east-side garage. He was forty-seven and could usually keep himself content with arithmetic like that: three hours, three trips, then the 12:45 home.

His phone moved in his shirt pocket. At the next red light he glanced down.

Did the car start okay? his daughter had written.

He typed Yes, then put the phone away before the light changed.

The Volkswagen had been taken away in October. He had bought it used after the divorce, and he had watched from the apartment lobby while the repo man and a kid in a yellow coat figured out the angle at the curb. Since then he had caught the 5:12 to work, or the 4:48 when he was on an early board. Carla did not know. His daughter did not know. In his lunch pail, under the folded rain cover, was a pay envelope with CAR written on it in pencil. It held eleven ten-dollar bills and two loonies.

The radio popped. “Two-one-four, Carla.” Her voice was flat with the end of a long day.

Dale picked up the handset. “Go ahead.”

“Tell Sood his Friday relief is Mancini. If he’s not at the garage, leave him a note. Also, I’m short two Saturday mornings.”

“Not this Saturday.”

“That’s what they all say. Out.”

The next two trips went by in wet floors and fogged windows. On the last eastbound, at the General’s hospital stop, the doors opened on a woman who looked about twenty-nine, with a red duffel bag and a boy hooked against her hip. She got the bag onto the first step, then the boy, then had to catch him when his boots slipped. He looked about four. His toque had been pulled down so far that only his nose and mouth showed.

The woman touched a fare card to the reader. It gave its hard little double beep. She turned it over, as if the other side might contain more money, and tried again.

“Just a second,” she said.

The boy began to slide down her leg. She hiked him up with one arm and shook coins into her palm. Two quarters, a dime, a button.

Dale waved her through. “Take a seat.”

“I have more in here.”

“It’s all right.”

She looked at him then. Her face was narrow with tiredness, and there was a dried crack at one corner of her mouth. “Thank you.”

She sat on the sideways seat behind him and arranged the duffel so it would not roll. The boy’s cheek went against her shoulder. Before the bus had crossed Wellington, his mouth had fallen open.

Dale watched them once in the mirror, then watched the road.

At Kenilworth, two men got off arguing amiably about whether a hockey fight counted if neither man landed a punch. At Lottridge, an old woman with a grocery cart rang the bell twice and apologized twice. The woman with the duffel stayed. Each time her phone lit, she bent over it. Once, when Dale checked the mirror, he saw her thumb hovering above the screen without touching it.

Near Centre Mall, she came forward with one hand on the rail. The boy was asleep against her chest now.

“Does this keep going east?” she asked.

“To Eastgate. Then I go in.”

“My sister’s place is out this way.”

“What street?”

She looked through the windshield. Snow was not falling, but grains of old salt and ice ticked against the glass. “I’ll know when I see it.”

Dale waited for more. She rocked the boy slightly when he stirred.

“You can tell me the intersection,” he said. “I’ll get you as close as I can.”

“It’s okay. She’s coming to get us.”

Her phone lit in her hand. She turned it facedown, but not before Dale saw the top of a message in the mirror.

MARA: I can’t do this again.

The woman went back to her seat.

At Centre Mall, Carla first told him to hold six minutes for the late 10, then changed her mind and switched a Walmart parcel pickup to his bus. Dale wrote the new time on his trip sheet. The parcel was a television in a box too wide for the doors. He helped its owner angle it in and listened, all the way to Parkdale, to an account of why the old television had been perfectly good except for the line through it.

When the man and the television left, the woman and boy were still aboard.

The boy woke at a long red light and peered down the length of the empty bus.

“Where do buses sleep?” he asked.

“Theo,” the woman said softly. “Don’t bother the driver.”

“He’s not bothering me.” Dale kept his eyes on the mirror. “They sleep in a garage. Big room, little buses in little beds.”

Theo considered this with grave distrust. “Buses don’t have beds.”

“You’ve caught me. They park nose to tail.”

“Do they get scared?”

“No. The old ones tell stories.”

“What stories?”

“Mostly about people putting gum under the seats.”

Theo smiled into his mother’s coat. She smiled too, briefly, and kissed the damp hair at his temple.

“He wants to drive a bus on Tuesdays,” she said. “The rest of the week he’s going to be a doctor who fixes only elbows.”

“Good hours, either way.”

“That’s what I told him.”

Theo was asleep again before they reached Nash Road.

At the last stop, Dale opened the doors and waited. Cold air travelled the floor to his ankles. In the mirror, the woman was putting on the boy’s mittens, working each slack finger into place.

“This is as far as I go with passengers,” he said.

She looked out at the dark plaza. The pharmacy had closed; ice shone blue under one remaining sign. “Is this Queenston?”

“Eastgate. Queenston’s back that way.”

She nodded but did not stand. “Just a little farther?”

“I can’t drop you at the garage.”

“My sister’s coming.” She checked the phone. This time it did not light. “Can we just stay until you stop?”

Dale thought of the procedure notice clipped above the farebox. He thought of the boy’s mittens, one blue and one grey, and of the wind moving a sheet of frozen newspaper across the empty lot.

He closed the doors.

The garage gate rose under his headlights. He drove past the fuel pumps, the parked snowplow, the rows of dark buses with their destination signs blank. At Bay Six he set the brake and switched off the engine. For a moment there was only the heater fan. After he shut that down too, some piece of metal behind the dash began to tick as it cooled.

The woman stood, swaying under the boy’s weight. “Can we wait in here until she comes?”

“No. I have to clear the bus.”

She looked toward the black windows. “Outside?”

Dale took his lunch pail from beside the seat. “There’s a drivers’ room. It’s warm.”

The radio popped. “Two-one-four, are you clear? I need the fuel sheet, and your Wednesday relief is Mancini now, not Sood.”

He keyed the handset. “Carla, I’ve got a woman and a child still aboard.”

A pause. “Then you’re not clear.”

The woman adjusted the duffel on her shoulder. The boy woke enough to say, “Mum,” and she answered, “I’ve got you.”

Carla came back. “Are they refusing to leave?”

Dale looked at the woman. She was watching the handset.

“No,” he said.

“I can call non-emergency. They’ll send somebody. I’ve also got three reliefs out sick and a 5:20 that’s currently a bus without a driver, so don’t disappear on me.”

“Understood.”

“No police,” the woman said.

Carla heard her. There was another pause, filled with faint static. “Drivers’ room, then. Bring your paperwork. Out.”

Dale helped with the duffel. It was heavier than he expected. Something inside clinked—a jar, maybe, or a ring of keys. On the lower step, the woman stopped and tried her phone again. Nothing.

“What’s your sister’s number?” he asked.

She gave it. He entered it into his own phone and handed it over. She held it to her ear for a long time.

“She’s not answering,” she said finally.

Dale could hear no ringing at all.

The heater ticked behind them. He unclipped his trip sheet, wrote 00:14 in the arrival box, and checked the mirrors out of habit. Empty seats. A brown paper bag under the rear bench. The woman’s red duffel reflected in the farebox glass.

“Ana,” she said, when he asked her name. “Ana Ruiz. This is Theo.”

“Dale.”

She shifted Theo higher. “If she calls back, can you tell her we’re here?”

“What street does she live on?”

Ana looked down at Theo’s mismatched mittens. “I haven’t been there in a while.”

Dale put the trip sheet into its plastic sleeve. He did it slowly because his hands needed something ordinary to do. He thought of the drivers’ room: plastic chairs, vending machine, the notice about not microwaving fish. At five in the morning the first drivers would bang through the door. Before that, Carla could call somebody. Ana could sit all night with her phone in her lap. Theo could wake up under fluorescent lights.

He took the envelope from his lunch pail.

“There’s a motel on Queenston,” he said. “The one with the sign missing half its letters. Clean enough. Drivers use it when they’re stuck between boards.”

Ana looked at the envelope.

“I can call a cab,” he said.

“No.”

The answer came quickly. Dale waited.

Theo had begun to cry without making much noise, his face pushed into her neck. Ana rubbed his back. “Just for tonight?” she asked.

“The room’s yours until checkout.”

She nodded once.

Dale called the cab company, then the motel. The night clerk said a single room was seventy-eight dollars, tax included, and there would be a ten-dollar key deposit. The cab dispatcher said twenty minutes, which Dale knew meant half an hour.

They waited in the drivers’ room. Ana would not take the chair he offered, so she stood beside it with Theo asleep against her. Dale made a cup of vending-machine hot chocolate and set it near her elbow. She drank it without seeming to notice.

At 12:41, Carla radioed to ask why two-one-four was still active. Dale said he was finishing his sweep. She told him the morning 8 would leave from Bay Two because of a hydraulic spill and that he could forget Wednesday’s relief change because Mancini had just called in sick.

“Copy,” Dale said.

“And don’t volunteer for the 5:20 just because you’re still here. I need you on your regular board Friday.”

Dale looked at the wall clock. His last connection left in four minutes. Beyond the garage fence, the road shone blackly under the lamps.

“Copy,” he said again.

The cab came at 12:53. Dale carried the duffel. In the back seat, Ana held Theo across her lap and stared at the phone in her palm. No one spoke except the driver, who wanted to know whether the cold snap meant winter was back or whether this counted as winter having never left.

“Could go either way,” Dale said.

At the motel, he gave the driver twenty dollars. Inside, the office smelled of carpet cleaner and burnt coffee. The clerk slid a registration card under the glass. Ana lowered Theo into a plastic chair and picked up the pen. Her hand stopped over the line for a home address. Then she wrote only her name.

Dale counted out eighty-eight dollars. The clerk counted it again and gave him a key attached to a piece of orange plastic.

The room was at the back. Ana went in first and stood beside the bed while Dale set the duffel on the luggage rack. The heater under the window rattled, then settled into a steady ticking. She laid Theo down without taking off his boots. He rolled toward the wall, still asleep.

On the dresser, her phone came suddenly to life. Its screen lit, buzzed, and lit again. Ana turned it facedown.

“Thank you,” she said.

Dale handed her the key. “Checkout’s eleven.”

She nodded. Her eyes moved to the heater, then to the window.

Outside, the cab was gone. Dale put the envelope back into his lunch pail. Four dollars remained in it.

He walked as far as the road before taking out his phone. It was 1:18. There were no more buses that would get him home. Under his daughter’s question about the car, he typed: The car’s gone. It happened in October. I’m at the Star Motel on Queenston. Can you come get me?

He stood under the buzzing vacancy sign and pressed send. Before he could put on his gloves, the phone buzzed in his bare hand.

Stay there, his daughter wrote.

Dale sat down on the low concrete wall with the lunch pail between his feet.

Last Westbound

The thaw had lasted two days and quit that afternoon, and by nine-thirty the slush along Barton had set into ridges that caught the front tires and pulled. Dale Varga drove with his thumbs loose on the wheel the way they taught you not to. Eleven years. The bus knew the ruts better than he did.

Three hours left. He'd done the math at the layover at the Gage loop, eating half a sandwich over the steering column with the doors shut: three more east-west trips, then the pull-in to the garage at twelve-forty, then the walk out to Barton and the one-oh-four westbound, which was the last one, which he had never once missed. The first week in October he'd timed it by walking from the yard gate to the stop with his phone out, like a man checking a parking meter.

The radio clicked. "Twenty-two-oh-eight, Dispatch."

"Go ahead, Carla."

"You want the split tomorrow? Mihail called in. It'd be a five-fifteen report."

He thought about the walk in the dark to the stop by his apartment, the four-forty bus that ran every forty minutes, the twenty-two minutes he'd stand on the corner if he caught it and the hour if he didn't. "What's the finish?"

"Nine-forty, then back out at two."

"I'll take it."

"You're a prince. I'll put you down." Her voice already turning away. "Watch Kenilworth, there's a crew out at the watermain. They say they'll have one lane."

"Copy."

He had a pass in his shirt pocket, the same kind he'd sold out of the farebox to teenagers for a decade. It was not the kind of thing you mentioned to Carla, who had a Dodge Caravan and a husband who did the brakes in the driveway. It was not the kind of thing you mentioned to Kayla, who was seventeen and had just finished her G1 and kept texting him pictures of used Civics with the mileage circled, as if he were the one who'd be co-signing. He had told her the Pontiac was in the shop. Then he'd told her the shop had found something else. By now it had been in the shop for four months, which was long enough that she'd stopped asking, which was worse than if she hadn't.

He let that go and watched the road.

---

At the General, the hospital stop, two nurses got on in their coats and sat together without talking. Behind them a woman came up the steps with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a boy on her hip, four, maybe, in a blue snowsuit with the hood down and his hair stuck up with sleep. She got the bag onto the floor and shifted the boy and tapped her card.

The reader beeped twice and flashed red.

She tapped it again. Red.

"It's fine," Dale said. "Go sit down."

"I put money on it." She was looking at the screen like it owed her something. Under the cuff of her parka there was a plastic hospital band, the white kind with the printing, and when she reached to fix the boy's hood her sleeve rode up and he saw it and then didn't look at it again.

"System's been doing that since the thaw," he said, which was a lie, but a harmless one. "Where you headed?"

"My sister's. East."

"Which stop?"

"I'll know it when I see it." She smiled with the bottom half of her face and took the boy down the aisle to the sideways seats over the wheel well, where the heater blew hardest, and set the duffel between her boots.

He closed the doors and pulled out into the ruts.

---

By Sherman the nurses were gone. By Gage it was the woman and the boy and an old man in the back who got on every night at Wentworth and rode to Ottawa Street and never said anything, and a girl with headphones who might have been asleep.

The boy had been awake and then he was not. He'd been talking about something in a low, steady voice, and Dale caught pieces of it in the mirror — the boy pointing at the ice on the window, the woman nodding — and then his head went down against her arm all at once, the way they do, like someone had cut a string.

She checked her phone. The screen lit her face blue. She turned it over on her knee and looked out the window, and then checked it again.

"He asleep?" Dale said, not loud.

"He goes down fast. He was up at five."

"Mine was like that. You'd be fighting her for an hour, then you'd turn around and she'd be face down on the floor like she'd been shot."

"How old is she?"

"Seventeen." He slowed for a man crossing against the light with a case of beer. "She's learning to drive. She sends me pictures of cars. Honda Civic, two hundred and eleven thousand kilometres, asking forty-two hundred. Like I know."

"Do you know?"

"I know that's too many kilometres."

She laughed, a real one, short. "He wants to drive a bus," she said. "This one. Not any bus. He asked the lady at the desk today if the forty-four went to the moon."

"It goes to Ancaster."

"That's what I told him."

The radio clicked. "All units, Kenilworth northbound is closed at Barton, repeat closed, they've lost the lane. Eastbound Barton detour via Ottawa to Cannon and back up Parkdale until further."

"Copy," Dale said, and then to her, over his shoulder, "You hear that? If your sister's anywhere around Kenilworth I'm going to be going around."

"No," she said. "She's further."

"Parkdale?"

"Around there."

"Which side?"

She didn't answer right away. Her phone lit up on her knee. She looked at it, and her thumb moved once, and it went dark. "I'll tell you when we're close," she said.

He turned down Ottawa Street. The watermain crew had a light tower up and the water had come up through the asphalt and frozen in a sheet across the intersection, flat and black and shining like something poured. A guy in a vest waved him through with a flashlight. The bus slid half a foot sideways and caught.

In the back the girl with the headphones got off. The old man got off at his corner and said nothing, as always. Dale came up Parkdale and the detour put him back on Barton and he called the stops one by one the way he always did, even on an empty bus, because you did it the same every time or you didn't do it at all.

Parkdale. She didn't move.

The sideways seats were behind him; he could see her in the mirror if he looked, and he mostly didn't. Her phone lit her face every three or four blocks. Theo slept with his mouth open.

Woodward. Nothing.

Nash. Her hand on the boy's back, flat.

"Last stop's the garage," he said, finally, at the light before Centennial. "You want me to let you off here?"

"Is it far? Your garage."

"Mile. There's nothing out there. There's a Tim's at Parkdale, that's about it."

"I'll call her," she said. "She's probably not even home yet. I'll just — can I call her when we get there?"

---

The yard was lit orange, the pull-in lane marked with cones somebody had kicked crooked. He took it slow over the frost heave at the gate and brought the bus in along the fence and put the brake on and shut it down, and the engine went quiet and the heater under the dash kept ticking the way it did, the metal cooling, tick, tick, like a cheap clock. He had written it on three defect cards. Nobody had fixed it, and he'd stopped expecting them to.

He got the defect card out of the slot anyway, and a pen.

"You're in?" Carla, on the radio.

"In."

"Six-three-one's on the pad if you want to swap your box out, otherwise just leave it. I've got you for the five-fifteen. Go home."

"Night, Carla."

Behind him she was on the phone. He could hear the ring in the quiet, four of them, and then a voice that was not a person, and she said, soft, "It's me, I'm — call me back, okay," and hung up, and sat there.

He wrote *heater ticking* on the card. He wrote the date, *19 Feb*, and the bus number, and signed it. The boy shifted and his hood fell off and she put it back.

"She's not home," she said.

"Okay."

"She works nights sometimes. She'll call."

"Okay." He put the card back in the slot. "There a number for whoever's at the house? Her husband, roommate, anything?"

"She lives alone."

Outside, a yard man was walking down the line of buses with a clipboard, checking numbers. The ticking kept on. Dale looked at the clock over the door, which said 12:51, and at his hands.

He could put her off the bus. That was the actual answer. The bus was pulled in and the yard was closed to the public and the supervisor's office had a chair and a phone and a man named Rick who would say what Dale would have said at thirty: *this isn't a shelter, there's a Tim's up the road.* Rick would be right. Rick would be right, and the walk to Parkdale was twenty minutes on a sheet of ice with a bag and a kid, and somewhere in the boy's pocket there was probably a toy bus with the number forty-four on it.

He got out his wallet. Sixty-three dollars, which was what was left of everything until the Friday after next, the twenty he'd set aside for Kayla's bus pass and the rest for groceries. He got out his phone and looked up a cab company and called it and gave the yard address and said, "Front gate, ten minutes, going to Parkdale," and hung up.

"No," she said. "You don't have to —"

"They won't let you wait in the office. It's not me, it's the rule." He stood up. His knees did what they always did at the end of a shift. He went down the aisle and crouched by the duffel so he was under her, not over her, and held out the bills folded in half. "This'll get you to Parkdale and then some. If your sister's further, it'll get you further."

She looked at the money. The band on her wrist showed again. There was a name printed on it and a date and he didn't read either.

"I can pay you back," she said. "If you give me — I don't have a pen, but if you tell me —"

"Pay the next guy."

She took it. She didn't put it in her coat; she put it inside the boy's snowsuit, in the chest pocket, and zipped it, which told him something he didn't want to know.

"Was he okay?" Dale said. "At the hospital. Theo."

"He's fine." She lifted the boy against her shoulder and his legs hung. "It wasn't for him."

---

The cab came at one. He carried the duffel to the gate and it was heavier than it looked, heavy like plates or books or everything. He put it in the trunk himself. She got in the back with the boy across her lap, and the driver, a man about Dale's age with a tuque pushed back, looked at Dale in the mirror and then at her and said, "Where to, love?"

"Parkdale and Barton," she said. "The Tim Hortons there."

The driver waited for the rest. She didn't give it. He shrugged and put it in gear.

Dale shut the door. The cab went out through the gate and turned west, and he watched the taillights until they were two red marks among the others on Barton, and then he looked at his phone, and it was 1:03.

He walked. He did not run; running on that ice was how you ended up in the General yourself. The stop was a hundred and forty steps from the gate and he knew it was a hundred and forty steps, and the bus was already coming, two headlights and the lit sign saying 2 BARTON WEST, and he put his arm up anyway and it slowed and knelt and the doors folded open with the sound they made.

The driver was Benny Tran, who'd come on three years after him. Benny looked at him and at the yard behind him with its orange lights and its rows of parked buses.

"Varga," Benny said. "Where's your car?"

Dale got out the pass and held it to the reader. It beeped once, green.

"Taking the long way," he said, and sat down in the sideways seat over the wheel, where the heater was, and the bus pulled out, and he watched the road he'd just driven come back at him the other way.

Out of Service

By nine o’clock the afternoon thaw had turned back into iron. All day the gutters along Barton had run brown with meltwater, and now the drop to minus nine had caught every puddle in place, glazing the intersections and freezing the plow ridges into grey concrete along the curbs. Dale eased bus 1408 across Wentworth on a fresh green, feeling the back tires twitch once over the black sheen before the weight of the axle pressed them down.

Three hours left. Eleven years behind the wheel had taught him to break a late shift into pieces he could swallow: Wentworth to Ottawa, Ottawa to the turnback, one more loop, then the deadhead run into the east-side garage. His left knee ached where the draft leaked around the steering column, and somewhere under the third row on the curbside, a floor heater had started clicking again. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Maintenance had signed off on it twice since January without finding a thing.

The radio popped on his left dash. "Control to all east-end runs." Carla’s voice came through thin and dry, like paper rubbed together. "Watch the dip east of Sherman, water main’s weeping onto the curb lane. Also, anybody on the Barton board looking for four hours Saturday morning, give me a shout after you pull in. Kowalski’s out with his back again."

Nobody answered. On a Thursday night in February, nobody wanted Saturday morning.

"Copy on Sherman, Carla," Dale said into the boom mic. "Fourteen-oh-eight."

"Thanks, Dale. You’re running two minutes hot at Victoria, don’t leave anybody standing."

"Holding at the hospital."

"Appreciate it. Tell me you’ve seen Rami’s relief on the five."

"Not since eight."

"Great," Carla muttered, not quite off the key before she cut out. "Love a Thursday."

Dale eased off the throttle as the lit bricks of Hamilton General came up on his right. Even at ten-twenty the emergency bay glowed hard white against the slush, an ambulance idling with its rear doors open. He pulled into the lay-by, set the brake, and let the air hiss out of the kneeling valve. Nobody at the shelter. He reached into the space beside his seat, checked the zipper on his canvas lunch bag out of habit. The envelope was still there, tucked behind his empty thermos—seven twenties from the kid in Stoney Creek who had come by Dale’s building at two o’clock to buy the four winter tires off his Civic. The repo truck had taken the car in October while it was still on all-seasons, leaving the winters stacked on steel rims in the basement locker. Dale had walked past them for four months before putting them online. Tomorrow morning he’d deposit the cash before the bank opened and clear the overdue Alectra notice taped to his fridge.

He had not told Carla about the Civic. He had not told his daughter, Tess, either. When Tess had texted last Sunday from her mother’s place up on the Mountain asking if he could help her haul a used bookshelf, he’d told her the alternator was acting up and sent her twenty dollars for a cab. At the end of his night shifts he changed out of his uniform jacket in the locker room, pulled on a plain dark parka, and walked four blocks south to Main so he could catch the westbound bus home without sitting behind one of the Barton drivers. He even tapped a regular green PRESTO card instead of flashing his employee badge. Pride was a stupid thing to carry at forty-seven, and he knew it, which hadn’t stopped him from carrying it.

Movement caught his right mirror. A woman came trotting out of the hospital’s side doors, head down against the wind, dragging a heavy navy duffel by one strap and half-carrying a little boy on her other hip. Dale leaned on the door lever before she had to wave.

Cold rushed up the steps, smelling of road salt and exhaust. She climbed aboard breathing hard, setting the boy down on the ribbed rubber floor so she could dig into her coat pocket. She looked late twenties, twenty-nine maybe, her dark hair pulled back too fast so loose strands stuck to her damp collar. No gloves. Her knuckles were raw pink.

She tapped a card against the reader. It beeped red, flat and sour. She tapped it again, faster, her shoulders hunching.

"Machine’s picky when it’s cold," Dale said, though the little screen read *INSUFFICIENT FUNDS*.

She unzipped a side pocket on the duffel, pulled out a handful of change, and dumped it into the slot—a toonie, two quarters, a dime that bounced and rolled back toward the steps. She bent for it, the boy’s sleeve catching on the metal stanchion.

"Leave it," Dale said gently. "Get him off the stairs before I roll."

She straightened, looking straight at him for half a second. Under the yellow doorway bulb he saw a narrow white strip of paper tape stuck just below her left cheekbone, right where the skin went shadowy. She pulled her coat tighter across her chest. "Does this go past Kenilworth?"

"All the way to Parkdale."

"Okay. I’m going to my sister’s."

"Grab a seat near the heater. Back row’s drafty."

She nudged the boy down the aisle. He couldn’t have been more than four, swallowed up in a puffy blue coat and oversized boots with Velcro straps. As she hoisted the duffel onto the third-row bench behind the wheel well, the cuff of her left sleeve rode back two inches. Snapped tight around her wrist was a white plastic hospital band with a black barcode printed across it.

Dale closed the doors, checked his mirror, and eased 1408 back into the lane.

By Gage Avenue the last two hospital aides had pulled the cord and stepped out into the dark, leaving only an older man near the rear exit and the woman and her boy. In the wide overhead mirror Dale watched the kid slump sideways until his cheek rested against her ribs. His blue knit hat slipped down over his eyebrows. She didn’t push it back; she just rested one bare hand on his shoulder and fished a cracked phone from her pocket with the other.

The screen lit her face pale blue. Her thumb moved across the glass, paused, moved again. She locked it, rested it on her knee, stared out into the black reflection of the window. Thirty seconds later she woke the screen again. Nothing changed on her face.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.* The heater kept time beneath her boots.

The radio crackled. "Fourteen-oh-eight, you still breathing out there?"

"Still here, Carla."

"Good. Listen, Devon finally showed up for the five, so my board’s holding together with spit. You’re looking clean to the end. Somebody left the breakroom pot on dry again, by the way—smells like a tire fire in here."

"You want me to grab a double-double on my way in?"

"Don’t be a hero, Varga. Just bring the bus back in one piece." A pause, the faint shuffle of schedules over the mic. "Oh, and tell the guys if you see ’em—salt truck hasn’t touched the north end of the lot yet. Watch yourself pulling out."

"Copy that," Dale said, his eyes on the red taillight of a pickup drifting across the slush ahead of him. He didn’t say he wouldn’t be in the north lot.

At Ottawa Street the last passenger in the back got off, leaving the three of them. Dale was three minutes ahead of his timepoint, the road empty now that the bars along the strip had quieted down for a Thursday. He pulled to the curb outside a darkened fabric shop, shifted into neutral, and set the brake. Across the street the orange glare from the steel plants hung low in the cloud deck, turning the frost on the rooftops the colour of rust.

Behind him something thudded softly on the floor.

Dale glanced up at the mirror. One of the boy’s heavy Velcro boots had slipped off his dangling foot and landed sideways in the aisle, leaving a white smear of dried salt on the rubber. The woman leaned down carefully so she wouldn’t wake him, hooked the boot with two fingers, and set it beside the duffel.

"Sorry," she said toward the front, her voice tight. "Got salt everywhere."

"That’s what the floor’s for," Dale said. He reached down and tapped the side of the heater vent near his own knee with the toe of his boot, as if that might reach row three. "You hear that clicking under your seat?"

She listened a moment. "Yeah. Sounds like a stove."

"Been doing it since October. I write it up every second Tuesday, night mechanic writes back *could not replicate*. Whole shop’s deaf."

Her mouth moved—not quite a smile, just a softening at the corners. She tucked the boy’s wool-socked foot against her thigh to keep it warm.

"My daughter had boots like those when she was his age," Dale said, watching the traffic light cycle green and yellow to nobody. "The ones with the thick grey liners you had to pull out every night."

"He loses the liners," she said quietly. "Takes them out and puts his plastic dinosaurs inside the shells so nobody steals them."

"Smart kid. Tess used to leave hers right on top of the radiator until the whole apartment smelled like wet sheep. Every February, same smell."

"Theo’s four," she said, looking down at the boy’s dark lashes. "Four and a half, he’ll tell you. Yesterday it was warm enough he wanted his runners. I told him wait a day, it’ll freeze again."

"Always does. Hamilton doesn’t let go of February without biting you once."

She looked out the window at the dark storefronts. For ten seconds the only sound was the idling diesel and the steady *tick-tick* under the floor. Then her phone sat dark in her lap, and she tapped it awake anyway. Her jaw tightened. She locked it and shoved it deep into her coat pocket.

Dale checked the dash clock: 11:19. He released the brake and eased back onto Barton.

"What cross street’s your sister near?" he asked, keeping his eyes on the road. "Streetlights are out along the bridge, easy to miss a stop."

A beat passed. "Just past Kenilworth," she said. "Near the little plaza. I’ll know the corner when I see it."

They passed Kenilworth. The plaza was dark except for a humming beer sign in a convenience store window. She did not reach for the yellow cord. They crossed the rail bridge, the suspension groaning over the frozen expansion joints. Ahead, the houses thinned into wrecking yards, brick warehouses, and chain-link fences coated in frost.

In the mirror Dale saw her turn her head twice to look out the back glass whenever headlights came up behind them. Both times the cars passed in the left lane and kept going. She never reached for the cord.

At 11:44 Dale pulled into the last regular stop on the route, a windblown concrete pad near Parkdale where one side of the glass shelter had been kicked out weeks ago. Snow grit skittered sideways across the asphalt. Across the road a Tim Hortons sat dark, chairs stacked upside down on the tables.

Dale pulled the lever. The front doors folded open and the wind slapped straight down the aisle, rifling the transfer pad beside his steering wheel.

"End of the line, ma’am," he said, keeping his voice level. "Garage from here."

In the mirror, she didn’t move. Theo’s head lolled heavier against her shoulder, his mouth slightly open, breath puffing faint white in the cold air pouring through the stairwell.

She looked up into the mirror, meeting Dale’s eyes. "Is there another bus going east?"

"Not till five-fifteen."

"What about back the other way?"

"Last westbound Barton already left the loop ten minutes back."

She glanced out past the open doors at the empty shelter, the busted glass, the dark industrial road stretching toward the highway. Her hand stayed buried in her pocket around the phone. She didn’t reach for the duffel.

Five seconds ticked by. Ten. A scrap of yellow flyer tore loose from the bus-stop post and whipped across the headlights.

Dale pulled the door lever shut. The sudden quiet felt thick. He reached up, tapped the keypad to flip the exterior sign to *OUT OF SERVICE*, and pulled back onto Barton toward the east-side yard.

The garage sat half a mile east, a low brick barn lit by sodium floods that turned the diesel haze yellow. Dale rolled through the open security gate, waved once at the booth—empty, Benny probably on his break—and eased 1408 into lane four outside the wash bay. Two buses ahead of him, a service hostler in an orange vest was climbing down from a coach, clipboard under his arm. Five minutes, maybe six, before the kid walked back for 1408.

Dale shifted into park, set the air brake, and let the engine idle so the cabin wouldn’t drop to freezing. Under row three, the heater kept right on going. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

The radio snapped alive. "Fourteen-oh-eight, I show you in the yard," Carla said. "Give me your odometer so I can close your run."

Dale leaned forward, squinting through his reflection at the green digits. "Four-one-eight, nine-six-two."

"Got it. Anything for the shop?"

"Same floor heater behind the wheel well. Clicking nonstop."

"Write it on the yellow slip, Dale, otherwise they’ll swear you made it up. Have a good one. Stay upright in that lot."

"Night, Carla."

He reached for his clipboard and a stub of blue pen. His fingers were stiff. At the top of the yellow defect slip he wrote *1408*, then *19 Feb*, then *Heater #2 clicking—won’t cycle off*. He pressed hard so it went through the carbon copy, tore the pink sheet off for his pocket, and wedged the yellow half under the wiper of the dash console. Next he leaned down to swipe his operator card across the farebox, waiting for the three slow beeps that logged the vault shut.

He was stalling and he knew it. Through the windshield he watched the hostler pull the bus ahead of him forward into the steam of the wash bay.

Dale unbuckled, stood, and walked back to row three.

Up close, the duffel looked packed in a hurry—a corner of a yellow fleece blanket stuck out of the strained zipper, alongside a crunched white pharmacy bag. The boy hadn’t stirred, one small hand curled around the button of her coat. She sat very straight, watching Dale’s uniform sleeve rather than his face.

"Hostler’s going to pull this coach into the wash bay in about three minutes," Dale said softly. "You can’t stay on board."

She nodded once, fast, like she’d been expecting it. "Yeah. Sorry. I just—let me get his boot back on."

"Your sister coming to meet you?"

Her hands paused over the Velcro strap. "She works nights," she said, not looking up. "Leaves her phone in her locker at the plant. She’ll see it on her break."

In her lap, the phone screen lit up by itself—a silent banner across the top, three or four lines of text. She didn’t open it. Her thumb came down hard on the side button and killed the light.

Dale looked past her shoulder at the frost spidering the corner of the window. The rulebook was sitting in his locker ten yards through the brick wall: *Passengers refusing or unable to disembark at terminus—notify Control immediately, await transit supervisor or police.* If he picked up the handset and called Carla, a supervisor would be at the bay in four minutes. They’d take her name, maybe call the non-emergency line to have a cruiser do a welfare check, and Dale would punch out by eleven-fifty-five and walk four blocks to Main with eight minutes to spare for the 12:04 westbound.

He looked at the white band around her wrist. At the sleeping boy’s boot, sitting half-fastened in her hand.

"Wait here," Dale said.

He walked back to the front, grabbed his plain parka from the hook behind the seat, and pulled his canvas lunch bag from the floor. He unzipped the side pocket, took out the envelope with the tire money, and slid it into his parka pocket. Then he turned the master switch.

The diesel coughed and died. The fluorescent strips blinked off, dropping the bus into the dull amber glow of the yard lights. Under row three, the heater finally stopped ticking.

"Come on," Dale said from the aisle, keeping his voice low. "Take the bag—no, give me the bag. You carry him."

She hesitated half a second, her eyes darting toward the wash bay where the hostler was walking back out through the steam, then nodded and lifted Theo tight against her chest. The boy murmured something into her neck and went limp again. Dale took the duffel—heavier than it looked, dense with bundled clothes—and opened the front doors manually with the release valve.

Instead of heading toward the lit garage office, he turned right along the dark brick flank of the maintenance wall, keeping them in the shadow between the parked buses and the fence. The frozen gravel crunched loud under his boots. Nobody called out. At the far corner he pushed through the narrow pedestrian gate onto the side street off Barton and let it latch quietly behind them.

The wind hit harder out here, straight off the bay. Dale set the duffel down by the chain-link post, shifted so his back blocked the worst of the gust from the boy, and pulled his cell phone from his jeans. He dialled Blue Line. Three rings, a tired dispatcher, an estimate of eight to ten minutes because of the ice.

"Where’s he taking us?" Ana asked. Her teeth were starting to chatter, though she was trying to hold her jaw still.

"There’s a motor inn on Queenston," Dale said. "Eastgate. Ten minutes down."

She looked at him sharply, stepping half a pace back with Theo against her shoulder. "I don’t have—I can’t—"

"I’m not coming with you," Dale said, looking down the empty street toward Barton. "Just getting you out of the wind."

They stood under the buzzing amber light of the gatepost without talking. A freight train clanked somewhere north near the steel yards, slow and heavy, the sound carrying far in the cold air. Dale checked his watch: 11:57. If the cab got here in three minutes, and if he jogged south down Parkdale, he might still make the 12:04 on Main.

The cab didn’t come in three minutes. Its yellow roof light turned off Barton at 12:03, creeping slow over the refrozen ruts until it eased to the curb beside them, exhaust blooming thick white from the tailpipe.

Dale opened the rear passenger door and swung the duffel onto the floorboard. Ana climbed in carefully, ducking Theo’s head past the frame, and slid across the vinyl bench. Dale leaned down to the driver’s open window. The cabbie was an older guy in a wool cap, radio tuned low to late-night talk.

"Eastgate Motor Inn on Queenston," Dale said, pulling one twenty from the envelope and laying it on the console. "Or wherever she tells you. Keep the meter easy if she needs a minute at the desk."

The driver glanced back once, then at the twenty, and nodded. "Got it."

Dale stepped back to the open rear door. Ana was already tucking the boy’s hat straight, her face half-hidden in the cab’s dim dome light. Dale reached in and pressed the folded six twenties against her bare hand.

Her fingers curled back instantly. "No. That’s too much, I can’t—"

"Desk guy takes cash if you’ve got a health card," Dale said briskly. "Tell him just tonight. Keep the chain on."

She looked up at him then, really looked, her eyes bright and exhausted above the narrow white strip of tape. For a second he thought she was going to say something about her sister, or the hospital, or the message she hadn’t opened. Instead she just closed her cold fingers around the bills.

"Thank you," she said.

Dale nodded once and pushed the door shut until it clicked solid. The cab’s brake lights blinked off, its tires bit the salted ice, and it rolled away east down Barton, shrinking into the yellow haze until it turned south toward Queenston.

Dale stood by the gatepost for two breaths, then zipped his parka up over his uniform collar, hoisted his lunch bag, and started walking fast toward Main.

The sidewalks hadn’t been salted since the thaw. Halfway down the second block his heel skidded on a tongue of black ice coming off a driveway, and he had to catch himself against a hydro pole, his bad knee flaring sharp. He kept moving, breathing hard through his nose, watching the gap between the low warehouses ahead.

Half a block short of the corner, the traffic light at Main turned green. A westbound bus—route 1, lit amber across the brow and mostly empty inside—glided smoothly through the intersection, its wheels throwing up a fine grey mist of salt, and disappeared west over the slight rise toward the city.

Dale slowed to a walk.

At the corner he stopped beside the empty bus-stop pole. Next westbound wouldn’t come through until after one, if the night owl wasn’t running behind on the ice, and he had no cash left in his wallet. He slipped his right hand into his parka pocket. His fingers brushed the flat edge of his PRESTO card, three loose quarters, and the smooth brass teeth of the Honda key he still hadn’t bothered taking off his ring.

Above him the streetlight hummed in the freeze. Dale shifted his lunch bag to his left hand, stepped carefully over the hard grey ridge of slush at the curb, and started west into the wind.

The Last Connection

At nine-oh-three, the heater under Dale Varga’s seat began ticking again. Three ticks, a pause, then a fourth, heavier one. He pressed his heel against the metal housing until it stopped.

Outside, Hamilton shone under a skin of ice. The thaw had left puddles across the sidewalks, and now the cold had tightened them around cigarette ends and frozen leaves. At each stop he brought the bus close to the curb, but not too close. Ice could carry a person’s foot straight under a tire.

Three hours left. Then the garage, the westbound night bus, a transfer downtown. If everything held, he would be in his kitchen by one.

He still caught himself calculating how long the drive would take.

At the hospital stop, a woman stood back from the curb with a duffel over one shoulder and a little boy leaning against her leg. Dale lowered the bus.

“Watch that first bit,” he said.

She lifted the boy over the gutter. Her boot slid, caught, and she came aboard without looking at Dale.

“Two,” she said, holding her bank card against the reader.

“He’s free.”

The reader flashed red.

She tried again.

“Give it a second.”

Behind her, the boy examined the yellow line with grave attention. His coat zipper had been started crooked, leaving one side higher than the other.

The card flashed red again.

“Go ahead,” Dale said.

“I have money.”

“It’s the machine.”

It might have been. Eleven years driving for the city had taught him not to make promises on behalf of a fare reader.

She took the first forward-facing seat behind the accessible area. The boy climbed beside her, then across her lap.

“Feet down, Theo.”

He lowered one boot.

Dale closed the doors.

At the next light he saw her reflection. Twenty-nine, perhaps thirty; about the age his daughter would be in another seven years. Dark hair pulled through the back of a cap. Hospital bracelet? No, a pale cuff showing between glove and sleeve. He looked back to the road.

“Do you go near the east end?” she asked.

“Eventually.”

“I’m going to my sister’s.”

“What street?”

She bent to adjust the boy’s boot. “I’ll know when we’re close.”

He met that answer several times a week. Usually from someone who had been drinking, sometimes from someone who hadn’t ridden a bus in twenty years. He told her they would turn north after the plaza.

“Okay.”

Her phone lit her face. She held it near her mouth, then lowered it without speaking.

***

Carla came over the radio at nine-thirty.

“Varga, your relief tomorrow is Singh, not Bell. Bell’s still off.”

“Copy.”

“And keep your running time through the construction. We’ve had complaints about early departures.”

“I’m four down.”

“Then you’re doing beautifully.”

He smiled despite himself.

At the plaza he waited through his recovery time while two men argued outside the closed pharmacy. The woman’s boy had fallen asleep against her chest. One small hand rested inside the open collar of her coat.

She caught Dale looking in the mirror.

“He likes buses,” she said.

“Picked a good night for it.”

“He likes the accordion ones better.”

“Everybody does until they have to reverse one.”

“You have to reverse them?”

“On the test. They put cones out and make you feel bad about yourself.”

She smiled. It changed her face enough that he looked away.

“My father drove a truck,” she said. “He could back it into a space this big.”

She held her hands a foot apart.

“Did he tell you that, or did you see it?”

“He told me.”

“There you go.”

She laughed once, quietly, so as not to wake the boy.

“What’s his name?” Dale asked.

“Theo.”

“Good name.”

“He’s decided it’s short for dinosaur.”

“Not Theodore?”

“Not this week.”

Dale’s daughter had once insisted she was changing her name to Sparkle. He nearly told the woman, but the radio clicked and Carla started moving somebody’s Friday shift to Saturday. By the time she finished, the woman was looking at her phone again.

At nine-forty-six, she made a call.

“Hi. It’s me.”

Dale kept his eyes on the illuminated strip of road.

“No, I’m—”

A pause.

“Can you just call when you get this?”

She ended the call and put the phone beneath her thigh.

He released the brake.

***

By ten-thirty the bus had emptied twice and filled again with a different assortment of people: a cleaner carrying work shoes in a grocery bag, three students smelling of fries, a man with a rolled carpet under his arm. The carpet man wanted to stand beside Dale.

“Behind the line, please.”

“It won’t fit.”

“It’ll fit farther back.”

The woman lifted her duffel out of the aisle. The man went past her, brushing the carpet against the poles.

At a long red light Dale flexed his right hand. The knuckle below his thumb had begun aching in December. His doctor had suggested driving less, then laughed when Dale looked at him.

His phone buzzed inside his lunch bag.

He didn’t touch it until the terminal.

His daughter had sent a photograph of two coats on a shop rack.

Which one is less real-estate agent?

He enlarged the picture. One was beige. One was green.

Green, he wrote.

Knew you’d say that.

You asked.

She sent a heart.

He had told her the car was in for work in October. In November, when she asked whether he could pick up a desk she’d found online, he said the transmission was waiting on a part. She hadn’t asked since. He disliked that more than another question.

The repossession had happened on a Tuesday morning while he was asleep after a split shift. He still had the second key in his kitchen drawer.

“Is this the last stop?”

The woman was standing near the front with Theo against her shoulder. His open mouth pressed a damp mark into her coat.

“Not my last. I go back east from here.”

“Could we stay on?”

“You haven’t passed your sister’s?”

“She’s not home yet.”

He glanced at the clock. Ten-fifty-one.

“I’m going out of service at midnight. East-side garage.”

“That’s okay.”

He wanted to ask how it was okay. Instead he pointed to the seat.

“You might as well put him down.”

“He’ll wake.”

She sat with the boy still held against her.

Before Dale pulled out, she came forward again.

“Ana,” she said.

“Sorry?”

“My name. In case you need it for the fare.”

“No report for that.”

“Oh.”

“I’m Dale.”

She nodded and went back.

On the final eastbound trip, the roads were nearly bare. Wind pushed loose grains of salt across the intersections. The bus windows had clouded above the heater vents, turning the storefront lights into blurred rectangles.

Ana plugged a cable into her phone and searched along the wall beside her seat.

“No outlets on this one,” Dale said.

“Right.”

“How much battery?”

“Eight.”

“There’s a cord here. If it’s the right kind.”

At the next stop he took his power bank from his bag and passed it back. She studied the little row of blue lights before connecting her phone.

“You’ll need this.”

“Not till later.”

He had bought it after missing a connection with a dead phone in November. His own battery was at thirty-one percent.

At eleven-thirty-eight, the last other passenger got off.

Dale left the doors open a moment.

“Ana.”

She looked up.

“I need an actual stop now. We’re heading into the garage after the next turn.”

Her thumb moved across the phone.

“Can we do one more time around?”

“There isn’t another time around.”

“I could pay.”

“It’s not that.”

The heater ticked. He pressed his heel against it, too hard, and pain jumped up through his knee.

Ana looked out at the dark storefronts. A laundromat had chairs along its window, but its lights were off.

“Is there somewhere open?” she asked.

“The hospital.”

“Not there.”

It came quickly. She shifted Theo higher and rubbed his back, though he had made no sound.

Dale closed the doors before the cold reached them.

“Let me check something.”

He called Carla.

“Any late eastbound service from the garage?”

“Not unless you’re volunteering to drive it.”

“I’ve got a woman and a small child. Their ride’s fallen through.”

Ana looked down.

“Where are they going?” Carla asked.

“Working on that.”

A pause, then the scratch of paper near Carla’s microphone.

“I’ve got the after-hours housing number here.”

Dale glanced in the mirror.

Ana was watching him now. She shook her head, barely.

“Hang on,” he told Carla.

He turned in his seat.

“It doesn’t have to mean anything gets decided tonight. You could ask what they’ve got.”

Ana rubbed the edge of her phone case.

“Will they call somebody?”

“I don’t know. You can ask that first.”

For several seconds she said nothing. Then she nodded.

***

The garage gates were open when they arrived at eleven-fifty-four. Beyond the fence, buses stood beneath white yard lights, their wet sides streaked with salt. He stopped in the holding lane outside the wash entrance.

He could not take passengers through. He could not leave them aboard and sign out.

His connecting bus would pass the stop on the main road at twelve-twelve. The next one was at five.

Ana had been on the housing line for nine minutes. Mostly she listened. Once she spelled her surname. When they asked for an address, she gave the hospital’s.

Dale looked away from the mirror.

Carla called his unit.

“Wash crew needs that coach.”

“I know.”

“You got an arrangement?”

He held the microphone against his chest until Ana looked up.

“They’re checking a room,” she whispered. “They said don’t hang up.”

He brought the microphone back to his mouth.

“Still waiting.”

“I can give you till twelve-ten in the holding lane. After that, bring them into the drivers’ entrance. You’ll have to stay with them.”

The drivers’ entrance was six minutes from his stop if he hurried. Maybe four if he ran across the employee lot.

Theo stirred and began to cry without opening his eyes.

“Nearly there,” Ana whispered.

Dale took his hand off the gear selector.

“Okay, Carla.”

“And sign your sheet at twelve. I can’t authorize the extra.”

“Understood.”

Deadhead

The heater in the 2200 had been ticking since Dundurn, a metallic knock every eight or nine seconds that Dale couldn't fix and couldn't stop hearing. He'd written it up twice. The first time the note came back with *unable to replicate* in someone else's handwriting. The second time it just came back.

He turned onto King Street and the ticking paused, then resumed at a slightly different interval, as if the bus were adjusting to the grade. Three passengers sat spread out behind him. A man in a parka near the rear doors who'd been riding since Westdale. A woman with grocery bags in the priority seats. A teenager with earbuds whose transfer had expired twenty minutes ago, and Dale had decided not to care.

It was quarter past nine. He had the route until twelve-fifteen, then the deadhead back to the garage on Kenilworth. Three hours. The thaw had broken two days ago and everything that had melted was now a skin of black ice under a half-inch of dry snow. He took the turns wide. The 2200 was rear-heavy when it was empty, and tonight it was close.

Carla came on the radio. "Dale, Fournier called in. I need you to hold the 5 at Eastgate until ten-oh-five for a connection."

"Copy."

"And shut down the reader at the rear door, it's been flagging ghost taps all week."

He reached over without looking and toggled the switch. The woman with the grocery bags pulled the cord and he eased to the stop at Wellington. She gathered her things, looked at the step down, looked back at Dale.

"It's icy," he said.

"I can see that."

She went down sideways, holding the rail. He waited until she was on the sidewalk before he closed the doors.

Ana Ruiz got on at the hospital stop at twenty to ten. She was carrying a blue duffel bag and a boy who was mostly asleep, his head against her neck. She had her Presto card out but the front reader took three taps before it caught.

"Sorry," she said. The boy stirred.

"Machine's slow when it's cold," Dale said, which wasn't true, but it filled the moment.

She sat in the second row, on the right side, and arranged the boy so he was lying across the seat with his head in her lap. She unzipped his jacket but didn't take it off. The duffel bag she kept between her feet.

Dale pulled away from the curb. In the mirror he saw her take out her phone, look at it, put it in her coat pocket. Thirty seconds later she took it out again.

The man in the parka got off at Ottawa Street. The teenager rode to Eastgate and left without looking up. Dale held the bus at the terminal as Carla had asked, engine running, the heater ticking. The mall was dark. A security guard walked past the glass doors and didn't look out.

Ana's boy — Dale could see now that he was maybe four, sandy hair, one sneaker untied — was fully asleep, mouth open. Ana was looking at her phone again. She caught Dale's eye in the mirror and put it away.

"Are you going far?" Dale asked. He said it because the route was thinning and she had a duffel bag and a sleeping child and had gotten on at a hospital.

"My sister's. She's on the east end."

"Okay. I go all the way to the garage on Kenilworth. You'll want to get off before that, unless you're transferring."

"Right. I'll figure it out."

She didn't ask about connections. She didn't name a street.

At ten-oh-five Dale closed the doors and pulled out. Nobody had been waiting for the connection Carla had mentioned. The bus was empty except for Ana and Theo.

Between Eastgate and Queenston he picked up a couple coming from the arena, still wearing their coats unzipped, flushed from the heat inside. They sat across from Ana and the man said something to her about the boy.

"He's out cold," the man said. "Ours are the same age. Put them in a moving vehicle and they're gone."

"He can sleep through anything," Ana said. She was smiling. It was the first time Dale had seen her face without the phone in front of it.

"How old?" the woman asked.

"Four in March."

"The best age," the woman said, and her husband or boyfriend said, "You say that about every age," and she said, "Because it's true about every age," and they laughed quietly. Ana laughed too, low, careful not to shake the boy.

The couple got off at Parkdale. The woman waved at the sleeping Theo through the window.

Then it was the three of them. Dale, Ana, and the boy.

Carla came on at eleven. "Dale, you good? You're running four minutes hot."

"Light traffic."

"Must be nice. I've got Fenton stuck on the mountain with a mechanical. Take your time on the eastbound, let the schedule catch you."

"Copy."

He slowed through the Barton stretch, took the lights easy. The street was quiet. A few men outside a bar on Gage, smoking in shirt sleeves as though the thaw were still on. A cat bolted across four lanes and made it.

In the mirror Ana was sitting very still, one hand on Theo's back. Her phone was face-down on the seat beside her. It buzzed once, twice. She didn't pick it up.

Dale watched the road.

At eleven-thirty he started the last eastbound run. Two more people got on and off. A nurse in scrubs riding three stops. A man who asked if this bus went to Stoney Creek and Dale said no and the man got off and stood on the sidewalk looking at his phone like it had lied to him.

By quarter to twelve the route was done. The last scheduled stop was Nash Road, and after that he'd deadhead south on Kenilworth to the garage. Ana hadn't moved. Theo hadn't woken up.

Dale pulled to Nash and opened the doors. Cold air came in. He looked in the mirror.

"This is the last stop on the route," he said. "After this I head to the garage."

Ana looked at him. She had her hand on the duffel bag strap.

"I know," she said. "I'm sorry. I thought — I thought she'd have texted by now."

Dale kept the doors open. The heater ticked. The cold was coming in fast and Theo shifted in his sleep, curling tighter.

"Your sister," Dale said.

"Yeah."

"You have her address?"

A pause. Not long, but enough. "She moved recently. I haven't been to the new place."

Dale closed the doors. He sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel. Outside, the snow was blowing sideways under the streetlight, fine and dry, the kind that wouldn't stick.

The radio crackled. "Dale, you at Nash?"

"Just left."

"Okay, bring her in. I'm locking the board at twelve-thirty."

"Copy."

He pulled out and turned south on Kenilworth. The garage was six minutes away. He had his walkthrough to do — lights, fare equipment, damage check, the write-up on the heater that he'd submit for the third time. After that he'd have fourteen minutes to reach the stop on Burlington Street where the 4 still ran at twelve-fifty-one, the last bus that would get him to his apartment on the west side. If he missed it he'd be walking, or calling a cab with money he'd already counted and allocated down to the dollar, because that was how February worked when you were still making payments on a car you no longer had.

He glanced in the mirror. Ana was putting Theo's hat on without waking him, working it over his ears with the particular care of someone who'd done this in worse situations than a cooling bus.

Dale pulled into the garage lot and parked in the third bay. He cut the engine. The ticking stopped.

He turned in his seat.

"Where does your sister live. Roughly."

"Off Cannon somewhere. East of Sherman, she said."

He knew the area. Fifteen blocks north. Not far in a car. Very far at midnight with a duffel bag and a sleeping four-year-old and nothing waiting at the other end of the phone.

"Sit tight," he said.

He did the walkthrough fast. Lights, fare readers, the rear door seal that stuck in cold weather. He wrote up the heater: *metallic tick from unit, 8–10 second intervals, persisted full shift, third report*. He signed the clipboard and hung it on the hook inside the garage office. Carla was at the desk with a Thermos and a sudoku book.

"Quiet night," she said.

"Mostly."

"You good to deadhead tomorrow's first if Fenton's still down?"

"I'm on at six anyway."

"Good man."

He walked back to the bus. Ana was standing in the aisle with Theo on her hip, the duffel over her shoulder. The boy was awake now, blinking under the fluorescent bay lights.

"Is there a bus from here?" she asked.

"Not until five."

She absorbed that. She hitched Theo higher on her hip.

Dale took out his phone and called Beck Taxi. He gave them the garage address and said one passenger, Cannon and Sherman area. They said twelve to fifteen minutes. He said fine.

"I'll wait with you," he said. "They'll come to the lot entrance."

"You don't have to do that."

"I know."

They stood outside the bay doors. The wind had picked up and the snow was finding the gaps between his collar and his neck. Theo put his face against Ana's shoulder and she turned so her back was to the weather, shielding him without deciding to.

"What's his name?" Dale asked. He'd heard her say it once, quietly, when the boy had stirred on the Barton stretch. But it was something to say while they stood in the cold.

"Theo."

"Good name."

"It was his grandfather's."

Dale put his hands in his pockets. His phone said twelve-forty-one. The 4 left Burlington Street in ten minutes. He could make it if he left now. He'd have to jog. He'd done it before, in October, the week after the car was towed, when the whole machinery of his life had shifted and he'd started learning the transit schedules not as a driver but as a rider, memorizing connections he'd never needed when he could just drive himself home.

Headlights turned into the lot. The cab stopped and the driver leaned across to look at them through the passenger window. Dale walked over and handed him a twenty and a ten.

"Cannon and Sherman area," he said. "She'll direct you from there."

The driver took the bills and nodded.

Ana was at the cab door. She looked at Dale. She didn't say thank you, which he was grateful for, because he didn't want to be thanked, he wanted to be standing somewhere warm.

"Text your sister," he said.

She got in. Theo's face appeared in the rear window for a moment, pale and round, and then the cab turned out of the lot and the taillights shrank down Kenilworth and were gone.

Dale walked to Burlington Street. His hands were already numb. He checked the time — twelve-forty-nine — and he could see the stop from here, the shelter with the cracked ad panel, and there was no bus yet, which meant it was either late or he was early, and either way he was going to stand here in the cold and find out.

The Heat Left Over

At nine fourteen, the heater began ticking again.

Dale Varga held his glove against the grille beside his knee. Heat came through, so he left it alone. A mechanic had told him once what made the noise, a flap or an actuator, and for a week he had known the proper word.

Outside, Hamilton had frozen in the shape the thaw had left it. Ridges of black snow stood along Barton Street. Water lay hard and clear in the footprints at the stops. Thursday, February 19: three hours left, two more runs, then the east-side garage.

At a red light, he checked the folded timetable in his shirt pocket. He knew the times. He checked them anyway.

If he signed off at twelve seventeen and walked quickly, he could catch the westbound at twelve twenty-six. At the transfer, he would have four minutes. Missing that connection meant a forty-three-dollar cab or sitting somewhere until morning service started.

He had been making this calculation since October, when the finance company took his Hyundai. Carla still sometimes asked whether the shop had finished with it. His daughter thought he parked behind his building, out of sight from her mother’s car.

The light changed. He put the timetable away.

At the hospital stop, a woman lifted a duffel bag onto the step, then helped a small boy climb after it. The boy wore a blue hat with two soft spikes. One mitten hung from his sleeve by a string.

The woman tapped a fare card.

It chirped a refusal.

“Try it flat,” Dale said.

She pressed it flat. The machine refused it again.

“There should be—”

“Come on in. We’ll sort it out.”

He said that perhaps twice a week. Most people kept moving before he could change his mind.

She stayed beside the reader, holding the card.

“You can sit down.”

The boy had his forehead against her thigh. She shifted the duffel with her foot and took the first forward-facing pair behind the accessible seating. Dale waited until they were down.

In the mirror, she looked younger than he’d first thought, perhaps thirty. Her coat was buttoned wrong near the bottom. The boy climbed sideways into her lap, his boots resting on the empty seat.

“That’s encouraging.”

“In.”

“No.”

“I can walk,” she said.

The Last Run East

At nine forty the melt started to freeze again.

Dale could feel it through the wheel before he saw it. All day the snowbanks along King had been leaking into the gutters, black water running over the manhole covers, and now the intersections shone under the streetlights in a way that meant the bus would want an extra foot to stop. He eased off early at Sherman, let the weight settle, and brought her in close enough to the curb that the old man with the grocery buggy did not have to step into the slush.

"Careful there," Dale said.

The man nodded and hauled the buggy up one wheel at a time. "They said minus ten by morning."

"They said Thursday would be dry, too."

"Thursday is dry. It's just wet dry."

The man laughed at his own joke all the way to the second seat, shaking water off his hat.

It was Thursday, February 19th. Dale had it written at the top of his trip sheet in the little block letters he had used for eleven years. Route eastbound, last three hours of a split that had started at five that morning. He had done the mountain run twice, had a four-hour break in the middle at the room on John where drivers played cards without ever quite finishing a hand, and now this. Finish at the Wentworth garage, fuel, farebox, defect card, clock out at twelve twenty-six. If he was quick at the gate he could cross to the stop on the far side of the fence, out of sight of the windows, and catch the 12:19 Barton bus west. That bus took him to within four blocks of his apartment.

Carla thought he parked in the north lot. His daughter thought he parked at the building.

"My dad's truck is picky in the cold," he had told Alina at Christmas, because she had asked why he had not driven to her place in Dundas, and the word truck had come out before car, as if a truck sounded harder to repossess. She was twenty-two and too polite to ask what kind of picky meant a cab from the mall with the turkey in a bag at his feet.

The finance company had taken the Civic on a Tuesday in October while he was on the early mountain run. He had come home to a rectangle of clean pavement where it should have been and a notice taped to his door that he read three times through the plastic sleeve. He kept meaning to tell people after he caught up. Catching up kept moving.

Carla came on the radio at Kenilworth, under a burst of static.

"Varga, you got anybody past Parkdale?"

He pressed the button. "Two. And a buggy."

"Your relief for tomorrow is gone. Petrakis called in, his kid's got the flu again and his wife's on nights. I'm short for the five twelve pull-out if you want first refusal before I go begging."

"What is it?"

"I did the five twelve last week," he said.

"You did. You also owe me for swapping your Sunday."

"I owe everybody."

"That's the spirit. Let me know by end of run. Don't sit on it or I'll give it to Hassan and he'll be saintly about it for a month."

"I can't stand saintly."

"Nobody can. That's why it works."

The old man got off at Ottawa Street with a careful step down, testing the curb with his boot. The other passenger, a girl with earbuds and a Tim Hortons cup she had been carrying empty for six stops, got off with him and turned toward the lit windows of the shops that were all closed. After that the bus was empty and sounded larger. The heater under Dale's left foot ticked, stopped, ticked again. It had been doing it since the thaw started. He had written it up Monday and again Tuesday. Maintenance had written HEATER OPER NORMAL in green pen and underlined OPER.

His face was turned into her coat. She tapped a fare card. The box rejected it with a flat buzz. She tapped again, harder, as if force would help.

"Just hold it flat," Dale said. "They're slow when they're cold."

She moved the card and held it. The box took it on the third try.

"How far east do you go?" she said.

"Right to the end, near the garage. Where are you headed?"

"My sister's. East end." She adjusted the boy. "This is fine."

"Past Parkdale?"

"Yeah. Past that."

She did not give a street. She carried the boy to the seats three rows back on the curb side and sat with him sideways on her lap, duffel at her feet. When the bus pulled away he was already tipping, his head finding the place below her collarbone. She put her palm over his ear against the engine noise.

Dale watched her in the mirror at the next two lights. She checked her phone each time they stopped. She held it low, beside the boy's back, read, typed with one thumb, stopped, read again. At Gilmour she put the phone away and zipped the duffel open an inch, then zipped it closed without taking anything out.

"You need the stop called?" Dale said, loud enough to carry.

"No. I know it when I see it."

"Okay."

"He sleeps through anything on a bus," she said after a moment. "Cars, no. Bus, he's out."

"Engine does it. My daughter was the same. She could sleep through a transfer at Gore Park with all the bells."

"How old?"

"Now? Twenty-two. Then, about his size. She used to hold the yellow cord like it was a job."

The boy slept with his mouth open. One boot had come unlaced. The woman noticed Dale looking in the mirror and tucked the lace inside the boot.

"I'm Ana," she said. "This is Theo."

"Dale."

"He lost the mitten inside," Ana said. "At the hospital. I went back to the chairs but someone took it or it went under. They give you a bag for your stuff and his hat was in it and the mitten wasn't."

"One mitten's a standard issue for four."

"I have socks in here if it gets bad. Put a sock on his hand. He hates it."

"He'll live."

"He makes that sound. Like you're ruining his whole night."

She smiled for the first time. It changed her face enough that Dale could see how tired she had been before it. Then her phone lit up through the pocket of her coat. She took it out fast, read, and typed, her thumb moving while she kept the other arm around Theo. She deleted what she had written. She typed again and sent it before she could seem to change her mind.

The bus went on past the Centre, past the rows of houses where people had put their garbage out early and the bags sat shining in the slush. Nobody else got on at Queenston. A man at the traffic circle looked up from the shelter, saw the head sign, and looked down again.

Ana said, "Is there a stop after the lights at the big intersection, by the park?"

"There's two. One before the bridge, one after."

"After is better."

"By Gage?"

"Close to there."

She checked the phone. Nothing had come back, but she checked it twice more before the next stop. At the stop after the bridge a woman with a stroller folded flat under her arm got on, rode exactly one stop, and got off again with a nod to Dale, as people did when they knew it looked strange and did not care.

Carla came back at eleven fifty.

"Varga, garage wants you in three tonight, not two. Wash rack's backed up and they're moving the five a.m. pull-outs around because of that watermain on King."

"Three," Dale said.

"Three. And they changed tomorrow's detour. If you take Petrakis you're not going through the core, you're looping at Wellington. I'll leave it in your box."

"Leave it for Hassan."

"You turning me down?"

"I'm thinking."

"Think faster. Also bring your farebox all the way in. Night guy says yours short-read on Tuesday."

"It short-reads when the cards stick."

"Everything sticks. It's February. Bring it in."

Ana's phone buzzed at the last commercial strip, where the fast-food signs put red and yellow light across the wet road. She read standing still, though the bus was moving, her whole body still. She put the phone in her pocket and took it out again immediately.

"Everything all right?" Dale said.

"She's just getting home late," Ana said. "My sister. She works late."

Dale nodded at the road. The last passenger stop before the garage was coming up, a pole and a bench and a low fence where the sidewalk gave out by the service road. Usually nobody was there after midnight. The bus would go out of service, run the half kilometre to the gate, turn in. The sign would go dark.

"This next one is the end of the line," he said.

"I thought you went further."

"Garage only after this. No passengers."

"My sister's is walking from here. It's not far." She did not move to wake Theo. She looked out the window at the bench, the fence, the empty sidewalk shining black. "Can we just sit a minute while I text her? He's asleep so hard."

"The heater's better in here," Dale said.

He pulled in and opened the door. Cold came up the steps with wet in it, melt and diesel and salt. Nobody on the bench. He left the door open five seconds longer than the schedule wanted, then closed it. Ana typed. She held the phone so the light fell on Theo's boot. She wrote, *we're here*, and Dale saw her erase it in the mirror and write, *almost there*, and then erase that, too. She finally sent two words and put the phone face down on her knee, where it lit again almost at once. She turned it over. Her jaw moved. She set her hand flat on it, screen down, and kept it there.

Dale called it in. "Control, Varga, end of line."

Carla answered, "Copy. Bring her home. Bay three, and don't forget the box."

"Bringing her home."

He switched the sign to NOT IN SERVICE. The letters went out in sections. He drove the half kilometre with the interior lights still on because turning them off would have made a point of the two of them in the back. At the gate he badged the bus in and waited for the arm. The lot beyond was wide and bright, buses in rows with their windshields fogged, a wash rack steaming at the far end. The night man in the booth lifted a hand without looking up from his screen.

Ana had not stood up.

He parked in the lane for three, set the brake, and let the engine idle. The heater ticked. Tick, tick, then a long pause, then tick again, faster, as if something in there were cooling at its own uneven rate. He had to do the walk-around, the mileage, the farebox pull, the defect card for the heater they would again call normal. His trip sheet lay on the dash with the pen clipped to it. The odometer reading went in one box, fuel in another. He wrote the numbers carefully because his mind was on the back seat.

He turned in the seat. "Ana. This is the garage. I can't keep you on here."

"I know." She did not wake Theo. "Just give me till she answers. Two minutes. She said she was coming home late, that's all. If I get out here where do I even--" She looked at the lot through the window. "There's nowhere to stand with him."

"Is she near Gage?"

"Near there."

"What street?"

She looked at her phone under her palm. "I can find it from the park."

"Dale," Carla said on the radio, so sudden Ana jumped and Theo stirred and settled again. "You in the lane?"

Dale reached for the handset. If he said he had passengers, Carla would follow the book because Carla had to. Supervisor, security to the bus, names taken, a call to the shelter line or to police non-emergency if the shelter line would not take a walk-in referral. It was all reasonable and all of it would happen under the lot lights with Theo carried between buildings to an office that had plastic chairs. If he said the bus was clear, he could do his sheets, pull the box, write the heater up again in the time it took for an answer to come, or not come.

"What do you need?" he said into the radio.

"Nothing. Just tell me if you're taking the five twelve before I call Hassan. I got him on the other line being rested and responsible."

"Give me a minute to get this box out. It stuck twice tonight."

"Cards or bills?"

"Cards. Every third one."

"Hit it on the side. Not hard. The night guy loves paperwork."

"Copy."

He put the handset back. The heater ticked while he worked, which it did whether the engine ran or not, something in the housing contracting. He took the farebox key from the pouch, fitted it, and had to jiggle it the way he always had to jiggle it, past the burr. The vault inside slid out heavier than it looked. He put it on the floor by his boot and filled in the defect card standing with one knee on his seat, writing slow.

HEATER TICKING UNDER DRIVER FLOOR AT IDLE. INTERMITTENT. FARE READER SLOW ON CARD TAP, THIRD ATTEMPT.

He dated it. February 19. He signed it.

"Please," Ana said, very quietly, not as a speech. "He just got asleep properly. In the hospital he didn't. Those chairs."

Dale looked at the clock on the dash. 12:11. His Barton bus crossed at the far stop at 12:19, and the walk from bay three to the gate and across to that stop, if the guard waved him through quickly and the light was with him, took six minutes in dry shoes. His were not dry.

He could still be out. He could carry the vault in, hand the sheet over, tell the night man he had left a woman and a sleeping kid in the lane for security, and be walking fast by 12:13. Carla would put his name in the lot column out of habit, and nobody would ask in the morning why he was at the stop because none of them had ever seen him do it.

He put the vault down again.

"I can give you till I finish fuelling," he said. "That's a few minutes. I have to move the bus up when they call."

"Okay."

"Text her the garage. Tell her the side street by the gate."

Ana picked up the phone. She typed with care this time. She sent it. They waited. The wash rack steamed. A bus two lanes over backed up with its alarm going and stopped. Theo breathed against her coat. The phone stayed dark.

At 12:16 Carla called the lane forward. Dale drove up to the fuel island, got out, put the nozzle in, set the latch, and stood in the cold that came off the concrete while diesel went in by the litre on the counter. Through the windshield he could see Ana holding Theo upright as he slept, her chin down, her phone in her free hand with the screen lit up and no new thing on it that he could see. She kept the screen lit by touching it every time it dimmed.

The counter stopped. He hung the nozzle, capped the tank, wrote the fuel number on the sheet.

12:21. The Barton would have gone past the stop while he stood with his hand smelling of diesel.

He climbed back up. "Nothing?"

She shook her head.

"I can ask dispatch about the shelter shuttle," he said. "It's not police. It's a van. They come here, or they come to the Tim's on the corner. But she'll have to log where you came from."

"Not the hospital," Ana said, too quickly. Then, "I don't want him woken up in a van full of-- I don't want him woken up yet."

"Where does your sister think you are?"

The phone lit. Ana read, and read again. She pressed her mouth with the back of her wrist. She typed, *okay*, and sent it, and turned the phone off in a deliberate way, holding the button until the screen went black.

"She's home now," she said. "It's fine. If we can get to the corner by the park, I can find it."

Dale sat down in the driver's seat. The heater ticked twice and stopped and started again under his foot, right through the sole of his boot. He radioed Carla.

"Control, Varga clear of fuel. Bus is empty. I'm putting her in three."

There was a pause with breath in it. "Copy, Varga. Have a good night. I gave Petrakis to Hassan, since you never answer a direct question."

"I'll live with it."

"Your car in the north lot tonight? It's going down to minus ten and that Civic of yours hates it. Security's doing a sweep at one if you want a boost marked."

"No," he said. "I'm good."

He shut the engine down. The heater went on ticking in the quiet, slower. He did his walk down the aisle the way he was supposed to, checking under seats, and stopped at Ana's row and waited while she got the duffel strap up onto her shoulder with Theo against her chest, his head heavy on her neck. He carried the bag down the steps for her without asking. At the booth he told the night man he was walking a fare out to the pedestrian gate who had stayed on past the end, just a fare, he had it, and the night man, looking at Theo asleep, buzzed the side gate without reaching for the phone.

The Tim Hortons on the corner had its windows all lit and nobody in it but a woman mopping between the tables. Dale held the door. The cab he had called from the island took eleven minutes. He spent them buying a tea he did not drink so they could sit at a table, and Ana spent them with Theo across two chairs, her coat folded under his head, the dead phone in her coat pocket with her hand around it. When the cab pulled in with its light on, Dale gave the driver forty-five dollars from his wallet, all of it folded behind his licence, which had been for the landlord on Friday. He gave the intersection by the park the way Ana said it. The driver said he knew it.

Ana lifted Theo, who woke enough to put his arms around her neck and then went slack again. "Thank you," she said.

"Get that lace or he'll be down," Dale said.

She settled Theo in the back seat and tucked the lace into his boot before she closed the door. The cab pulled out onto the wet road and turned west at the light, brake lights smearing red on the black shine.

Dale stood in the doorway with the tea going cold in his hand. He set it down untasted on the ledge by the door and went out. The sidewalk on his side had refrozen in ridges. He walked east first, the wrong way, to the corner where the Barton stop was, because there was a chance the bus had been held at the terminal and a chance was easier to stand in front of than the whole walk was. The sign was there. The bench was there. A transfer lay in the slush under the bench, pulped white. No bus.

He turned west and started walking on the service road, staying up on the gravel edge where the trucks had not polished the ice, his bag banging once against his hip before he shortened the strap. A NOT IN SERVICE bus came out of the garage behind him and passed without slowing, throwing a low sheet of melt off its tires. Dale stepped back onto the bank, felt the cold come through his left boot where the seam had opened, and waited until it was past before he stepped down again and kept walking.

The Last Pull-In

On Thursday, the nineteenth of February, Hamilton looked rinsed and left in the freezer. All afternoon the thaw had run down the curbs in dirty ropes, and now, close to nine, the water had hardened again in the ruts and along the gutters. Dale Varga watched the shine of it in his mirrors as he eased the bus away from the stop at King and James, the tires whispering where the salt had worked and ticking where it hadn’t.

Three hours left. Less, if the traffic held and nobody decided to stand in the back doors arguing with a girlfriend on speakerphone. Route 5C out past the hospital, east along Main, loops and jogs, then the long deadhead to the garage near Nash. Pull in, sweep, sign out. If he was quick, he could make the 12:17 across Barton and transfer once, home by one-thirty.

If he missed it, he was looking at a walk in boots with cracked soles, or a taxi he couldn’t afford.

The bus hissed and rocked. Fluorescent light flattened the faces in the windshield reflection: a student with earbuds; an old man holding a grocery bag against his chest; two teenagers smelling of weed and wet wool. Dale sat upright, shoulders square, left hand on the wheel, right close to the brake. Eleven years and his body knew the route better than his mind did. His mind kept wandering to the car.

The tow truck had come in October, before sunrise, polite as a funeral director. He had watched from his apartment window while they took the Civic off Woodward Avenue, its rear wheels buckled up, the hazard lights blinking weakly as if embarrassed. Since then he had told people it was in the shop. A long shop visit. Transmission. Parts. Back order. Everyone understood parts on back order.

His daughter, Emily, had believed him or had chosen not to ask. Carla at dispatch had never asked because Carla did not ask personal questions unless they affected a schedule. Dale had made sure they didn’t.

“Five-C, Varga,” Carla’s voice cracked over the radio. “You copy?”

Dale pressed the button. “Five-C. Go ahead.”

“Relief on forty-three is late. If you see Sandhu at Queenston, tell him to call in. He’s not answering his cell.”

“Copy. Queenston, Sandhu, call in.”

“And watch Main by Wentworth. Standing water froze up. Roads has been notified, which means nobody’s coming.”

“Copy.”

Carla sounded as tired as the heater fan. Practical, flat, built out of coffee and fluorescent bulbs. Dale pictured her with one elbow on the console, hair pinned up badly, a schedule board full of holes in front of her.

At the hospital stop, three people got off and two got on. The second was a woman with a navy duffel bag dragging against her leg and a little boy asleep on her shoulder. No, not asleep yet, Dale saw as she climbed aboard—nearly asleep, lids sinking, cheeks flushed from indoor heat. The boy was bundled in a red coat with one mitten clipped to a string and the other clutched in his fist.

The woman shifted the duffel and fumbled for a card. She was young, though the set of her mouth had an older tightness. Dark hair tucked under a knitted hat, hospital bracelet half hidden beneath her sleeve. She tapped the card.

The reader flashed red.

She stared at it as if it had spoken in another language.

“Try again,” Dale said.

She did. Red.

The boy stirred and made a small complaining sound into her neck.

“It had money,” she said. Her voice was low. “I just used it yesterday.”

“Machine’s been moody in the cold,” Dale said, which was only sometimes true. He thumbed the override. “Go on.”

Her eyes lifted to his. “Thank you.”

“Hold the rail. Floor’s wet.”

She moved down the aisle, careful with the child and the duffel, and took the sideways seat near the middle doors. The duffel went under her knees. The boy slid fully asleep against her, mouth open, one damp curl stuck to his forehead.

Dale shut the doors and pulled away.

At Dundurn, the teenagers got off laughing. At Locke, the old man shuffled forward and said, “You got the heat on, driver?”

“As much as she’ll give me.”

“She’s giving it to the ceiling.”

“That’s where the rich people sit.”

The old man snorted, pleased enough, and stepped down into the night with his grocery bag. Dale waited until both feet were clear before closing the doors.

Behind him, the little boy woke for a minute and lifted his head. In the mirror Dale saw him looking around, solemn and dazed.

“Is this the long bus?” the boy asked.

His mother touched his hair. “Shh, Theo.”

Dale didn’t answer at first. Then, because there were only five passengers left and because the question seemed to hang there, he said, “Long enough.”

Theo blinked at the aisle. “Does it bend?”

“Not this one. The bendy ones are on different routes.”

“I saw one. It was like a caterpillar.”

“An expensive caterpillar,” Dale said.

Theo seemed to consider that. “Does it sleep in a nest?”

“A garage.”

“With other buses?”

“Rows of them.”

Theo nodded, satisfied, and put his head back against his mother. She smiled despite herself. It changed her face quickly and then vanished as she looked at her phone.

Dale caught pieces in the mirror: her thumb lighting the screen, the angle of her wrist, the way she turned the phone slightly toward the window so no one behind her could see. She checked it at every light. Once she typed fast, stopped, erased. Once she lifted it as if to call, then lowered it when a pickup pulled alongside and its headlights filled the bus.

At Main by Wentworth the bus slid half a foot though Dale was already crawling. His stomach tightened, old reflex, hands steady. The ABS chattered beneath him. The bus straightened and rolled on.

“Roads notified,” he muttered.

A woman near the front said, “What’s that?”

“Nothing. Just praying to the salt gods.”

She laughed and went back to her paperback.

At the next stop, the woman with the duffel stood suddenly, then sat again before the bus had fully braked. Dale opened the doors for a man in a McMaster hoodie. The woman watched the sidewalk, her phone in her palm, then looked away from the doors as if the cold air itself had changed her mind.

Dale waited an extra second. “You getting off?”

“No,” she said. Too quick. “Not here.”

“Okay.”

The man in the hoodie tapped on and took a seat. The doors folded shut.

Dale knew better than to ask people where they were going. Half the job was not asking. People brought all kinds of things onto buses after dark: flowers wrapped in plastic, court papers, black eyes, birthday cakes, anger too large for their bodies. You drove. You called if there was blood or a weapon or someone who stopped breathing. Otherwise you drove.

Still, after Ottawa Street, when the bus had emptied except for the woman, the boy, and a man snoring gently in the back corner, Dale said, “You need a particular stop?”

The woman’s eyes came up in the mirror. “Near my sister’s.”

“Which one’s near your sister’s?”

“I’ll know it.”

That was not an answer, but it was shaped like one. Dale nodded and kept driving.

The heater under the front panel began its tick again, the fast metallic sound it made when the fan was fighting ice. Tick-tick-tick. Tick-tick-tick. He leaned forward and slapped the panel with the heel of his hand. It stopped for four seconds, then resumed.

“Thing’s been doing that all night,” he said, mostly to himself.

Theo lifted his head. “Is there a bug?”

“A big one,” Dale said. “Union bug. Can’t fire him.”

Theo smiled sleepily. “Bugs don’t have jobs.”

“You haven’t met the ones at the garage.”

His mother let out one breath that might have been a laugh. “He likes insects.”

“I like beetles,” Theo corrected.

Dale said, “There used to be June bugs all over when I was a kid. Hit the screen door like popcorn.”

“I had those in Windsor,” she said. “My grandmother hated them. She used to say they were drunk.”

“Maybe they were.”

“On what?”

“June, I guess.”

This time she did laugh, quietly. For a few blocks they talked about bugs, of all things. Theo, half awake again, told Dale that a bombardier beetle could shoot hot stuff from its bum. Dale said that sounded like half the riders after chili night, and the woman covered Theo’s ear too late and said, “Please don’t teach him bus-driver jokes.” Dale said those were the clean ones. She told him her name was Ana. Theo said his full name was Theo Ruiz and he was four and a half, which Dale knew meant four.

The bus moved east through light traffic. Storefronts shone and went dark behind them. Snowbanks along the curb had collapsed during the thaw and frozen into gray reefs. Ana’s face softened while Theo spoke, but each time the phone buzzed or seemed like it might, she tightened. The screen did not ring. Dale saw that too.

At Queenston, he spotted Sandhu outside a shelter, smoking with his hood up.

Dale opened the front door. “Carla says call in.”

Sandhu swore gently. “My phone died.”

“She guessed something like that.”

“Tell her five minutes.”

“You tell her. I’m not marrying into this.”

Sandhu grinned and stepped back. Dale closed the door.

Carla came on before he’d cleared the intersection. “Five-C, you find him?”

“Affirmative. He’s alive and lying about five minutes.”

“Copy. I’ll start with twelve.”

Dale smiled despite himself.

The man in the back woke at Parkdale, lurched forward, and got off without a word. After that, it was only Dale, Ana, and the sleeping child.

“You passed it?” Dale asked after another ten minutes. He kept his tone light. “Your sister’s stop.”

“No. It’s farther.”

“This route only goes so far before I pull in.”

“I know.”

He doubted that. “After Kenilworth I’m out of service. Garage only.”

She looked down at Theo. “Okay.”

There was nothing okay in the way she said it.

Dale’s left knee ached from the damp. He flexed it at a red light and checked the dash clock. 11:36. Good time. Too good, maybe. He could sweep fast, grab his backpack, walk out the employee gate, and catch the 12:17 with room to spare. At home there was soup in a plastic tub and a message from Emily he had not answered because she wanted to know if he could drive her to Limeridge on Saturday.

He would text: Sure, if car’s ready.

The lie had become a route of its own, regular stops, familiar turns.

“Five-C,” Carla said. “When you pull in, lane six. Maintenance wants 0912 in the morning, so don’t bury it.”

“Copy, lane six.”

“And Dale?”

He pressed the button again. “Go ahead.”

“You still want that school extra tomorrow? Five-forty-five report. I’ve got Nadeem asking.”

The school extra. Seventy-three dollars and change before deductions. He had asked for it Monday. It would mean four hours of sleep and a thermos of coffee, but it would cover his phone bill before they cut him off.

“Yes,” he said. “I’ll take it.”

“Then don’t be late on pull-in. I need you clean on hours.”

“Understood.”

Ana was watching him in the mirror now. He felt it like a hand between his shoulders.

At the last public stop, he opened the doors and turned in his seat. “This is the end of the line.”

Ana did not move. Theo’s face was pressed into her coat, his body heavy with that boneless sleep children had, as if they trusted the world to carry the weight.

“My sister’s not far,” she said.

“From here?”

She looked through the windshield. Ahead, the industrial road ran black and empty toward the garage lights. Beyond that were fenced lots, transport trucks, repair shops, a Tim Hortons with half its sign burned out.

“What street?” Dale asked.

She rubbed Theo’s back.

“Ana.”

“I don’t know the exact street.”

That was the first thing she said that felt entirely true.

Dale set the parking brake. The bus sighed around them. Tick-tick-tick went the heater. Outside, a gust pushed dry snow dust across the road.

“You got someone coming?”

She looked at the phone again. Dale could see the screen glow against her chin. No name answered there. No map. Just the pale blank of a message thread, or that was what he thought he saw before she turned it away.

“She’ll call,” Ana said.

“How long have you been saying that?”

Her mouth tightened. She didn’t answer.

He thought of the rules. No passengers past last stop. No passengers in the yard. No unscheduled stops while out of service. Call supervisor. Call police if there was a welfare concern. He imagined saying police and saw the way her shoulders would come up before he even said it.

The radio clicked. “Five-C, status?”

Dale picked it up. “At terminus. Taking a minute.”

“You’ve got three minutes to make lane six.”

“Copy.”

He put the handset back. Ana’s eyes had gone shiny, though she was not crying. That almost made it worse. Crying gave a person somewhere to put the thing. She was holding hers in her jaw, her knees, the hand clamped around the useless phone.

“There’s a women’s place downtown,” Dale said. “Good Shepherd. You know it?”

She nodded once, not quite yes and not quite no.

“I can call.”

“No police.”

“I didn’t say police.”

“No one at the hospital,” she said, and stopped.

Dale waited. The heater ticked. Somewhere under the bus, cooling metal knocked once.

No one at the hospital what? No one could know? No one should be called? He saw again the bracelet under her sleeve, the duffel packed badly, Theo without his second mitten properly on. He saw how she had stood and sat at that stop when the pickup’s headlights came in.

“You got discharged?” he asked.

She looked away. “We can get off.”

He hated that. We. As if the little boy had chosen any of it.

“Where would you go?”

“My sister’s.”

“But you don’t know the street.”

“I said I don’t know the exact street.”

Dale rubbed his thumb along the ridged edge of the steering wheel. The dash clock changed to 11:49.

“Five-C,” Carla said again. “Talk to me.”

Dale lifted the radio. His mouth was dry. “Carla, I’ve got a woman and child aboard at end of line. They need assistance.”

A pause. In the pause he heard everything: policy, paperwork, Carla leaning back, Carla closing her eyes for half a second.

“What kind of assistance?”

“Safe place. Not police at this time.”

Another pause. “You are not to bring passengers into the yard.”

“I know.”

“Nearest public location is the Tim’s on Woodward. You can unload there.”

He looked toward the burned-out sign in the distance. Open, probably. Bright, cold, full of men in work boots and teenagers with nowhere to go. Ana watched him, not asking.

“Good Shepherd family intake is downtown,” Dale said.

“You’re out of service.”

“I know.”

“You are due in lane six.”

“I know that too.”

Carla exhaled through her nose; the radio flattened it to static. “If you leave route, I can’t make your hours work for the school extra.”

There it was, neat and specific. Seventy-three dollars and change sliding away before he had even held it.

“Nadeem still want it?” Dale asked.

“He’ll take it.”

Dale looked at the clock again. 11:51. If he pulled into the garage now, he kept the extra, maybe caught his bus, maybe kept every lie stacked in place for one more day.

Theo made a soft sound and burrowed closer to his mother.

Dale pressed the radio button. “Give it to Nadeem.”

“Dale—”

“I’m taking them downtown. I’ll return the bus after.”

Carla was silent long enough that Dale pictured her deciding what not to say. Finally she said, “Log it as passenger assist. No fare service. Call me when clear. And Varga?”

“Yeah.”

“Don’t hit anything.”

He almost laughed. “Copy.”

He changed the sign to OUT OF SERVICE. The farebox beeped angrily when he entered the code wrong, then again when his finger slipped. Practical stupid thing, demanding its sequence while Ana sat behind him with all her life in a duffel. He cleared it, entered the code, and the front sign blinked.

OUT OF SERVICE.

He released the brake and turned the bus around in the wide mouth of the road. The rear wheels crunched over frozen slush. Ana said, “You don’t have to.”

Dale checked his mirror. “I know.”

“I can pay you back.”

“No.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I.”

They drove west through the same streets, emptied now. The city at midnight belonged to plows, taxis, and people with nowhere settled to be. Dale kept to the salted lanes. His hands had gone very calm.

At Sherman, Theo woke enough to whisper, “Are we at the bus nest?”

“Not yet,” Dale said. “Detour.”

“Because of the bug?”

“Because of the bug.”

Ana shifted, trying to keep him covered. “He’ll talk forever if you let him.”

“Good. Keeps the union bug company.”

Her phone buzzed once. Dale saw her flinch before she looked. She read whatever came and pressed the phone face down against her thigh. She did not type back.

That was the fact she held out of the air between them. Not the sister. Not the street. The someone on the other end of that buzzing phone. Dale did not ask. Asking would make her either lie or tell him, and neither would get the bus downtown faster.

Near Wellington, Carla radioed with a detour for another route, then forgot and gave it to Dale too. “All units, avoid Main and Victoria, collision blocking two lanes. Five-C—sorry, Dale, take King across and drop south at Mary if you’re still moving.”

“Copy. King to Mary.”

“You clear yet?”

“Ten minutes.”

“Make it nine. Maintenance is whining.”

“Tell them the bug is theirs now.”

“What bug?”

“Long story.”

Carla made a sound that might have been a laugh if she had not been on duty. “Just bring my bus back.”

Downtown, the streets narrowed and shone. Dale pulled to the curb half a block from the shelter entrance, not at a stop, flashers clicking. He knelt the bus. The front lowered with a tired groan.

Ana stood slowly, Theo sagging against her. The duffel strap caught under the seat. Dale unbuckled, left his driver’s compartment, and walked back. The bus felt strange beneath his feet when he was not driving it, longer and emptier. He freed the strap and lifted the duffel before she could tell him not to.

“It’s heavy,” she said.

“I noticed.”

At the front, the farebox screen still glowed. The heater ticked like a small impatient clock. Ana stepped down first, then turned awkwardly with Theo. Dale carried the duffel to the sidewalk.

The cold came at him hard. His breath smoked. Down the block, the shelter doors showed a rectangle of yellow light.

Ana took the duffel from him. For a second her hand closed over the handle where his had been.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded. Words felt like something he might drop.

Theo lifted his head from her shoulder. His eyes were slits. “Does the bus sleep now?”

“Soon,” Dale said.

“With the bug?”

“Yeah. With the bug.”

Theo accepted this and disappeared again into his mother’s coat.

Ana turned toward the lighted doors. After three steps she stopped and looked back, not quite at Dale, maybe at the bus, maybe at the road behind it. Her phone buzzed again. She did not take it out. She adjusted Theo higher on her hip and kept walking.

Dale stood beside the kneeling bus until the shelter door opened. Warm light spilled over Ana and Theo, then narrowed as they went inside. The door closed.

The flashers clicked. The bus breathed white exhaust into the frozen street. Dale climbed back into the driver’s seat and raised the bus, feeling the whole frame lift under him. The radio waited in its cradle.

He looked at the dash clock. 12:16.

Somewhere across the city, the Barton bus would be closing its doors. Dale pictured it pulling away without him, warm windows sliding past the stop by the garage. He reached for the handset.

“Carla,” he said. “Five-C clear. Coming back.”

The Last Pull-In

By nine o’clock the thaw had hardened into ridges of black ice along the curb lanes. Water that had run freely at noon now glazed the gutters, and every bus shelter shone under the streetlights as if sealed in glass.

Dale Varga kept both hands loose on the wheel. Eleven winters had taught him that gripping harder did nothing except make his shoulders ache. The bus knew its own weight. Let it settle before braking, ease it away from the curb, never trust the shine on the pavement.

The duty sheet clipped beside him said THU 19 FEB. Three hours left, two east-west runs, then a short final to the garage. He could already feel the twelve-thirty bus he took home waiting at the edge of his mind. The connection at King was only seven minutes, but the late driver usually held for uniforms.

Nobody at work knew he depended on it. His car had disappeared from outside his building in October, lifted by the finance company before dawn. He had told Carla the transmission had gone. He had told his daughter, Melissa, that he was thinking about something smaller.

At the General, six people got off and two got on. The first was an orderly in blue scrubs. Behind him came a woman with a duffel over one shoulder and a little boy bundled in a red coat.

The woman tapped her fare card. The reader gave a flat, accusing buzz.

She tried again.

“Funds unavailable,” Dale said, reading the display.

“I loaded it.”

“Takes time sometimes.”

She shifted the duffel higher. The boy leaned against her leg, his mitten pressed to one eye.

“I can pay cash.” She searched one coat pocket and then the other.

“You’ll hold us all night doing that. Go sit down.”

Her gaze came up sharply, as though kindness might have terms. She was young—late twenties, maybe—with damp black hair tucked behind her ears. No hat. One side of her jaw looked darker than the other, though it might have been the light from the farebox.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Where are you headed?”

“My sister’s.”

Dale waited for the useful part.

“East,” she added.

“This goes east.”

She nodded and guided the boy to the first pair of forward-facing seats.

The orderly found a place at the back. Dale checked his mirror, pulled away and felt the rear wheels slide half an inch before catching.

At Wellington, the boy climbed onto his knees to look over the seat.

“Does this bus bend?”

“Not this one,” Dale said.

“I saw one that bends.”

“Articulated bus.”

The boy considered the word. “Like an alligator?”

“More like an accordion.”

“My grandma has an accordion.”

The woman gave a tired laugh. “He means she has a picture of one.”

“Still counts,” Dale said.

“What makes the doors breathe?” the boy asked.

“Air.”

“They sound mad.”

“They are mad. Opening and closing all day.”

The boy smiled. “Do they bite?”

“Only mittens.”

He pulled his hands against his chest. His mother—Ana, Dale learned when she told him to sit properly—apologized, but Dale said it was fine. Children asked better questions than adults. Adults asked whether he was late when they could see the same traffic through the windshield.

On Barton, the bus filled with shift workers, grocery bags and two teenagers sharing headphones. Ana checked her phone at every second stop. She never typed much. Once she lifted it to her ear, listened without speaking, then put it facedown on her knee.

Carla’s voice cracked over the radio.

“Operators eastbound, avoid the curb lane between Sherman and Gage. Water main break, sanding crew requested.”

Dale pressed transmit. “Four-seventeen copied.”

“Dale, you’ve got McNally relieving the twenty-two at eleven-forty. If you see him at the loop, tell him he’s not going to the garage.”

“Thought Singh had that.”

“Singh called in.”

“Again?”

“He has the flu.”

“He had the flu two weeks ago.”

“Then he’s very unlucky.”

Carla clicked off before he could answer. Tired and practical, as always. The city could freeze solid and she would still be moving little names between little boxes.

At Ottawa Street, Ana stood suddenly.

“Is this—” She looked out at shuttered shops, dirty snowbanks and the dark mouth of a side street. “Does this go near the mall?”

“Eventually.”

She sat again.

Dale watched her in the mirror. The phone lit in her hand. He couldn’t read the message, but he saw the same number call twice in quick succession. She silenced it both times. Then she opened the settings and moved through several screens with her thumb.

Theo had gone quiet. His head rested against her arm.

At the east loop, everybody got off except Ana and the boy.

Dale set the brake and turned halfway in his seat. “End of the line.”

“I thought it went back.”

“It does. Westbound.”

“That’s okay.”

“You said east.”

“My sister isn’t answering. I’ll get off on the way back.”

There were places open near the loop—a coffee shop, a twenty-four-hour pharmacy—but the wind drove wrappers across the pavement, and Theo’s eyes were closed now. Ana had one arm around him and the other through the duffel strap.

Dale looked at the clock. He was two minutes down.

“You need to tap again,” he said.

She reached for the card.

“Forget it. Sit tight.”

On the westbound run, Theo slept against her. Ana kept the phone dark. Dale picked up three cleaners outside a long-term-care home, a man carrying a boxed microwave, and an old woman who talked loudly to herself about municipal taxes. At James, McNally was smoking under the shelter.

Dale opened the door. “Carla says you’re covering the twenty-two.”

McNally looked offended. “I’m on spare.”

“Not anymore.”

“Singh?”

“Struck down in his prime.”

McNally ground out his cigarette and climbed aboard, muttering about seniority. He noticed Ana and the sleeping child but said nothing.

Near downtown, freezing spray from a passing truck struck the windshield. The wipers dragged it into a white fan. Dale slowed, worked the washer twice and listened to the heater ticking under the dash. It had been ticking since dinner break: fast when the fan ran high, slower when he turned it down. He made a mental note for the defect card.

At the west loop, Ana did not move.

Dale waited until the other passengers had left.

“Where’s your sister live?”

“East end.”

“We just came from there.”

“I know.”

“What intersection?”

She looked at Theo instead of him. “Near Kenilworth.”

“We passed Kenilworth.”

“She hasn’t answered.”

Dale rubbed the bridge of his nose. “Ana, I’ve got one more trip and then this bus goes to the garage.”

“I know. I’m sorry.”

“If you tell me where you need to go, I can tell you how to get there.”

“She said she’d pick us up.”

“Where?”

“On the route.”

That wasn’t an answer. Her phone vibrated inside her coat. She pressed her arm against it until it stopped.

Dale thought of calling Carla. Carla would tell him the procedure: rider leaves at the terminal; if rider refuses, request a supervisor; if safety is in question, call police. Procedures liked clean categories. Ana was not refusing. She looked ready to obey anything he said, which felt worse.

“Theo need a washroom?” he asked.

Ana shook him gently. The boy woke enough to nod.

Dale pointed toward the lit coffee shop across the loop. “Five minutes. I’ll hold.”

While they were gone, he called in four minutes late and blamed the roads. Carla told him to take his recovery time and reminded him the final pull-in had changed because the service entrance was iced over.

“Use Gate Three,” she said. “Fuel lane first, then park in inspection.”

“Copied.”

“You sound cheerful.”

“Living the dream.”

“Do it quieter.”

Ana came back carrying Theo, his legs wrapped around her waist. Dale lowered the bus and opened the doors. She climbed aboard without meeting his eyes.

The final run thinned after ten-thirty. They passed storefronts with grates drawn down, bars leaking yellow light, apartment towers whose balconies were lined with frozen runoff. At a stop by Gage Park, Ana rose, looked out and sat again.

Her phone rang once more.

This time Theo stirred. “Is Dad coming?”

Ana put a hand over his hair. “Go back to sleep.”

She glanced toward Dale’s mirror. He looked away before she caught him looking.

At eleven-fifty, the last regular passenger got off with the boxed microwave. The bus became a moving room for the three of them. Heater ticking. Engine humming. Theo’s cheek flattened against Ana’s coat.

Dale called the garage and gave his pull-in number.

Carla answered. “Gate Three. Don’t forget the defect card if that heater’s still making noise.”

“It is.”

“Then write it up. Maintenance can’t fix what you don’t write up.”

He nearly told her about Ana. Instead he said, “What time’s the employee bus?”

“Twelve thirty-two sharp tonight. Why?”

“No reason.”

“You need it?”

“My car’s around.”

The lie came easily because it was an old one.

The route ended at twelve-eighteen under the sodium lights outside the east-side garage. Snowbanks hemmed the fence. Beyond it, buses stood in rows with their interiors glowing, empty and exposed.

Dale opened the doors.

Ana stayed seated.

“This is the garage,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“You can’t stay aboard.”

“I know.”

But she did not move. Theo hung heavily across her lap. Her phone was off now; he could tell by the way she kept pressing the side button and getting no light.

Dale pulled on his coat. “Is there a sister?”

Ana looked toward the open door. Cold air moved through the bus.

“Yes.”

“Does she know you’re coming?”

Her mouth tightened.

That was close enough to an answer.

“I can call somebody,” he said. “A shelter. Family services.”

“No police.”

“I didn’t say police.”

“No police.” She gripped the duffel strap. “Please.”

Carla called over the radio. “Four-seventeen, status?”

Dale picked up the handset. “At Gate Three.”

“Fuel and inspection. You’re blocking the pull-in.”

“Passenger issue.”

A pause. “Need a supervisor?”

He looked at Ana. She shook her head hard.

“Negative. Give me five.”

“Five, Dale. Fleet needs the lane.”

He closed the front door to keep the heat in and took out his personal phone. The shelter line transferred him twice. While he waited, he filled out the defect card against the steering wheel: UNIT 417. HEATER TICKING, DASH AREA, ALL FAN SPEEDS. The pen tore through the paper where he pressed too hard.

The woman on the line asked questions he relayed to Ana. Adult and child, yes. Immediate medical attention, no. Safe to send a cab, Ana hesitated, then said yes. Name optional. Pickup could be the public road outside the garage, but the wait would be twenty to thirty minutes.

The employee bus left in fourteen.

Dale looked through the windshield at the garage doors. He could put Ana and Theo in the heated security vestibule, except passengers were not allowed past the fence. He could send them to the coffee shop by the loop, a twelve-minute walk over frozen sidewalks. He could call a regular cab, but he did not know where to send it.

Carla came back. “Four-seventeen, I need you moving.”

“I’ve got transport coming for them.”

“How long?”

“Twenty minutes.”

“You can’t hold the bus twenty minutes.”

Dale watched Ana tuck Theo’s red mitten under his chin.

“I’ll fuel now,” he said. “Then park outside inspection until their ride comes.”

“You’ll miss sign-off.”

“I know.”

“And the employee bus.”

“My car’s here.”

The words sat between him and the radio.

Carla exhaled. “Fuel, then north fence. Don’t leave them unattended. Bring me the defect card before you clock out.”

Dale moved the bus. At the fuel lane he shut down, pulled on his gloves and fed the heavy nozzle into the tank while Ana and Theo waited behind the glass. The diesel fumes caught in his throat. Across the yard, the employee bus idled with its sign blank.

He finished fueling and parked by the north fence. At twelve-thirty-two, the employee bus rolled past. Dale kept his face turned toward Ana’s expected cab. The driver lifted two fingers in greeting. Dale raised his hand.

The cab arrived at twelve-forty-six, unmarked except for a small blue placard on the dash. The driver gave Ana’s first name. That was enough for her.

Dale carried the duffel down the steps while Ana carried Theo. On the pavement she took the bag from him.

“Thank you,” she said.

Theo opened one eye. “Do the doors bite?”

“Not tonight,” Dale said.

Ana held his gaze for a second, then climbed into the cab. It pulled away without showing a destination.

Dale handed Carla the defect card at twelve-fifty-three. She glanced at the clock and then at him.

“Thought your car was here.”

“Wouldn’t start.”

She looked too tired to challenge him. “Cold’s hard on batteries.”

Outside the garage, Dale ordered a taxi on his phone. The app showed fifty-one dollars and sixty cents to his building, not including tip. His account balance was sixty-eight fourteen.

When the car arrived, he got into the back.

“Where to?” the driver asked.

Dale gave the intersection one block from his apartment.

The taxi nosed onto the frozen road. On the meter, $4.75 appeared, held for a moment, then climbed.

The Last Run

By nine, the puddles along Barton Street had frozen again. The bus shuddered through them, and Dale Varga heard the hard little knocks beneath the wheel wells. In the afternoon the gutters had run brown with meltwater. Now the water had a skin on it, thin enough to break under a tire and thick enough to send a pedestrian sliding.

He had three hours left. At the end of the route he would take the bus into the east-side garage, complete his inspection, and catch the last westbound bus from the stop outside. He had eight minutes to make the connection. Eleven years driving for the city had taught him which part of an eight-minute gap was real.

Carla came over the radio while he waited at a light.

“Dale, you’re still good for the garage at twelve-oh-five?”

“That’s what the sheet says.”

“I know what the sheet says. Martin called in sick. I’m moving the spare around.”

“I’m good for twelve-oh-five.”

“Copy.”

He put the radio back in its cradle. Carla had asked the same question twice this week. If he missed the last bus, she might wonder why it mattered so much. She had seen the car he used to drive, a blue Mazda with rust around the rear wheel arch. When it was repossessed in October, he’d told no one at work. His daughter still thought it was at a mechanic’s, which had become a difficult thing to say whenever she asked how much longer a mechanic could possibly need.

At the next stop, a man got on carrying a paper bag from a bakery. He counted out his fare in coins, apologizing for the cold ones sticking together.

“No rush,” Dale said.

The man took a seat near the front and lifted the bag off the wet floor. Warm bread smelled briefly stronger than the bus heater, which had been ticking behind Dale’s left ankle since dinner.

“You think the Bulldogs will get one Friday?” the man asked.

“I think they’ll play Friday.”

“That’s the spirit.”

“They’ve got a better chance than I do.”

“Of playing?”

“Of getting out of bed Saturday.”

The man laughed. For three stops they discussed a goalie neither of them claimed to know much about. When he got off, he held the bakery bag high over a ridge of black slush and waved at Dale through the door.

The bus went quiet again. Dale knew the stops well enough to notice a change in the light from a porch or a missing shopping cart. On this run, he passed a pharmacy with one bright aisle still open, shuttered storefronts, and the hospital with its lit windows stacked against the dark. He pulled up at the hospital stop at ten seventeen.

A woman stood there with a duffel over one shoulder and a little boy leaning against her leg. The boy wore a green hat with a pom-pom that had gone crooked. The woman moved him ahead of her up the steps, keeping a hand on his back.

“Does this go through to the east end?” she asked.

“It does. Where are you going?”

“My sister’s.”

He waited for a street. She glanced at the route display above the windshield instead.

“We go east on Barton for a while,” he said. “I can tell you the stops.”

“Thanks.”

She tapped a fare card twice before it took. The boy gripped the pole beside the farebox, trying to stay upright while she found her balance. They sat halfway back, on the side away from the doors. In the mirror, Dale saw her pull the boy close, wedge the duffel against the wall, and check her phone.

At the next stop, two hospital workers in winter coats boarded and took seats behind her. One ate crackers from a packet. The other watched the dark go by. Dale drove across Victoria, then farther east past the blocks where salt had turned the snowbanks grey.

At a long red light, the boy came up the aisle, holding on to each seat in turn. His mother followed before he could reach the yellow line.

“Is this the biggest bus?” he asked Dale.

“Not the biggest one I’ve seen.”

“Where is that one?”

“In a depot, maybe. Some of them bend in the middle.”

The boy considered that. “Like a worm?”

“A very slow worm.”

“Does it get stuck?”

“Sometimes.”

The woman guided him back. “Sorry. He likes to know how things work.”

“No trouble.”

“I’m Ana,” she said. “This is Theo.”

“Dale.”

Theo waved from around Ana’s coat. He stayed awake another six minutes, long enough to ask whether the wipers got tired. Then his head slipped against her arm. She shifted him into her lap, though he looked nearly too big for it, and checked her phone once more.

At the end of the first loop, the hospital workers got off near a low apartment building. Ana stayed. She watched them cross under a streetlamp and looked down at the phone. Dale saw only the glow against her face.

The route turned back toward the hospital before running east again. That was the arrangement on late Thursdays: a shortened second pass, then a final sweep to the garage. Most passengers knew what they needed from it. They watched for their corner, not for his expression.

At eleven, Dale pulled into the timing point outside a closed grocery store. He was two minutes early. A young man with an enormous box of diapers boarded, asked if the bus stopped at Parkdale, and sat opposite Ana. Dale set the brake and looked at the schedule clipped beside him.

The heater ticked three times, paused, ticked again. He reached down and turned its dial one notch. The noise stopped. Cold air touched his ankle.

Ana came forward with Theo asleep against her shoulder. The weight of him had pulled her coat collar open.

“Are we close to Parkdale?” she asked.

“About twenty minutes. Is that where your sister is?”

“Near there.”

“There’s a stop at Barton and Parkdale, and another farther along.”

“I’ll watch.”

She had answered without quite answering. Dale could list every cross street on the run, but he couldn’t choose one for her. He pointed out the stop-request cord. Ana nodded as though she hadn’t already seen it and went back to her seat.

The young man got off at Parkdale with his diaper box braced on one knee. Ana stayed seated. Dale drew away slowly enough for her to call out if she meant to. She bent over the phone instead. Theo’s green hat had slipped over one ear.

“Parkdale,” Dale said, after the stop was behind them.

“I know.” She looked up. “She’s farther along.”

“All right.”

He drove on. There were houses beyond the shops, stretches of chain-link fence, a tire place with a security light over its locked gate. Twice Ana raised her head at a stop and lowered it again. Dale kept announcing the streets. He could hear the slight change in his own voice each time.

Carla called at eleven twenty-eight. “You’re running three down. Can you make any of that up?”

“Traffic’s clear.”

“Good. I’ve got a relief driver waiting on your bus in the morning, so don’t leave me a surprise on the defect sheet.”

“The heater’s ticking.”

“Is it heating?”

“Yes.”

“Then write ‘ticking.’ Don’t write ‘dead.’”

“Wasn’t planning to.”

“Everybody’s planning something tonight.”

The radio clicked off. Dale looked at Ana in the mirror. She had her phone angled toward her, thumb moving over it, while Theo slept with his mouth open against her coat. The screen went dark. She pressed its side button, and for a moment it lit her face again.

He thought of asking Carla for advice, then looked at the empty seats. There was nothing to report. A passenger had paid and was still riding. On a route ending at the garage, that happened often enough.

At eleven forty-two, Ana came forward again.

“Does it turn around?”

“No. After the last stop I take it in.”

“Could I stay on until then?”

“You can ride to the last stop.” He pointed ahead. “There’s a lit shelter there. After that it’s garage property.”

“How far from the hospital?”

“Too far to walk tonight.”

She looked through the windshield. The road shone black where tires had worn the frost away.

“I’m just waiting for a message,” she said.

Dale gave her the distance to the last stop. She thanked him and carried Theo back. He settled without waking. She pulled the pom-pom hat down over his ear.

At the final public stop, Dale opened the doors. Cold air moved through the bus, carrying the smell of road salt. No one stood in the shelter. Its glass was clouded except for a clear circle someone had rubbed with a glove.

“Ana,” he called. “This is it.”

She stood and lifted Theo. Her duffel slipped from her shoulder, and she caught it by its handles. She got as far as the front wheel well. Dale watched her look at the shelter, then at the road continuing east.

“Is there somewhere inside at the garage?” she asked.

“There’s an office. I can ask if you can wait there while you arrange a ride.”

She tightened her hold on Theo. “Thank you.”

Dale closed the doors. The clock above the windshield read 11:58. The garage was five minutes away. The westbound bus outside it was due at 12:12.

He called Carla.

“I’ve got a passenger and her kid still aboard,” he said. “They need a place to wait for a ride.”

A second passed. “You’ve finished service?”

“Last stop. I’m coming in.”

“Bring the bus to the entrance. Don’t take them through the bays. I’ll see who’s at the desk.”

“Thanks.”

“And Dale? Your relief’s on six tomorrow. Park in twelve, not six. I wrote it down wrong.”

“Bay twelve.”

He hung up and checked the doors in the mirror before pulling away. The heater began ticking again.

At the garage gate, the automatic arm rose slowly. Dale crossed the yard past buses parked nose to tail, their windows pale under the lights. He stopped at the pedestrian entrance, set the brake, and opened the front door. The clock said 12:04. His connection was still possible if Ana could go straight inside.

He shut off the engine. The heater gave a last series of ticks, louder without the engine beneath it. Theo stirred. Ana bounced him gently and looked toward the building.

Dale stepped down first. A patch of ice lay beside the curb, almost invisible on the wet concrete. He showed her where to put her foot. She came down sideways, clutching Theo and the bag, and followed him to the entrance.

He pressed the buzzer. No answer. He pressed it again and heard a faint ring from within.

Through the window he could see a desk, an empty chair, and a mug beside a computer. Carla came on the radio clipped to his coat.

“Night desk’s in the yard. He’s coming. You still by the entrance?”

“Yes.”

“Your pull-in hasn’t posted.”

“I haven’t parked it.”

“You’ll have to. I can’t close the run with the bus at the door.”

The bus stood behind them with its front lights on. Dale had left Ana and Theo outside while he went to the buzzer. The wind crossed the yard, and Ana tucked Theo’s hands inside her coat.

“I’ll get you in,” he told her. “I have to move the bus after.”

A man in a reflective jacket appeared on the other side of the glass and unlocked the door. He held it while Ana carried Theo through. Warm air came out around them.

“Carla said you need to wait for someone,” the man said to Ana.

Ana nodded. “I’m trying to reach her.”

“There are chairs by the desk. Give me a minute and I’ll find out what’s open.”

Dale waited until the door shut behind them. Then he ran the bus around the first row and into bay twelve. He turned off the lights, checked the seats, looked under them for dropped bags, and wrote TICKING—HEAT WORKS on the defect sheet. At 12:13, he signed out.

Outside, the westbound bus was passing the garage stop. He could see the interior lights and, for an instant, the driver’s arm on the wheel. He stood with his satchel in his hand while it went by.

He went back to the office. Ana sat beneath a noticeboard, Theo stretched across two chairs with his head in her lap. The man at the desk was on the phone. When he put it down, he told Ana there was an overnight intake desk that could speak to her and a number she could call from here. She asked to use the phone.

Dale moved away while she dialed. On his own phone, he opened the taxi app. A car could reach the garage in nine minutes. The fare to his street was estimated at $41.60. He checked the pickup point, then pressed Confirm.

When he looked up, Ana was listening with the desk phone to her ear. Theo’s hat had come off. She held it in her free hand, smoothing the damp pom-pom between her fingers.

A car’s headlights turned into the yard. Dale lifted his satchel and went to meet it before the driver took the wrong lane. Behind the office glass, Ana shifted the phone to her other ear and tucked Theo’s hat under his chin.

Deadhead

By nine o'clock the thaw had quit. All day the city had run with water, and now the slush on Barton Street was setting into ridges. The bus rode them like a washboard, and Dale Varga felt it in his wrists. The outside bank sign said minus eleven. The sign had said plus four at noon, when he left his apartment with two transfers folded in his shirt pocket.

He had three hours left. The Barton run went east from the terminal, past the hospital, past the shuttered tire places and the Portuguese bakery with its lights still on, and out to the loop at Eastgate. From there he deadheaded to the garage with the bus empty and the destination sign gone dark. He had done it so many times that his hands took the turns without him.

His phone buzzed in the cup holder. His daughter: *how's the car doing in this cold? mine wouldn't start this morning.* He left it face down. At the next red light he turned it face up, looked at it, and turned it back.

"Eleven forty-seven, Carla," the radio said.

He thumbed the mic. "Eleven forty-seven."

"Water main at Wentworth is still going, so starting tomorrow the eastbound detours at Sherman. I'm putting it on your sheet. Also, Ritchie's off again, so I need somebody on the early pullout. Fifty-two past five. You interested?"

"Fifty-two," Dale said. He did the math on the first bus in. There wasn't any. "Yeah. Fine."

"You're a saint. I'll tell Ritchie you said so."

"Don't tell Ritchie anything."

"Never do." She sounded as though she was eating something. "Heater okay on that one?"

"Ticking in the back. Still heats."

"Write it up."

At 10:41 he knelt the bus at the Hamilton General stop, and the hydraulics sighed. A woman came up the steps with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a boy on her hip. She was young, maybe thirty, in a parka that was too thin for the night. The boy wore a coat that was too big and boots with the laces trailing. She tapped her card on the reader. It gave two low beeps and the screen said *Insufficient*.

"Oh," she said. "It had money on it."

"Go ahead. Short night."

"I can find cash."

"Go ahead," he said again, and she did, and said thank you in a way that sounded practiced. She took the sideways seat behind him, the one that faced the aisle, and set the duffel between her feet. In the big mirror he could see the top of her head and the boy's.

The boy was awake. He looked at the ceiling, at the yellow poles, at the back of Dale's neck. "Why did it go down?"

"Kneeling," Dale said. "So people can get on easy."

"Does it get tired?"

"Only at night."

The boy considered this. "I'm four," he said.

"That's a good age for it."

"His name's Theo," the woman said. "I'm Ana." She said it to the mirror.

"Dale."

They pulled out. A man in a Stelco jacket got on at Sherman and rode three stops with his eyes shut. A pair of teenagers got on at Ottawa, shared a vape they thought he couldn't smell, and got off at Kenilworth. Between stops Ana looked at her phone. She looked at it the way you check a pot you don't want to boil. The screen lit her chin and went dark and lit again.

"You going far?" Dale asked at a red light.

"My sister's. Out east."

"Which stop?"

"I'll say when." She put the phone in her pocket, then took it out. "Is it always this cold?"

"It was fourteen above this afternoon. Every spring I think I'm done with it, and then it comes back and puts a hand on my chest."

She laughed, a short one. Theo had slid down against her side, and she moved his hood so it wasn't in his eyes. His sleeve rode up. Dale saw a strip of white paper around the small wrist, the kind they print at the desk. Ana saw him see it in the mirror, pulled the sleeve down over it, and looked out the window at a bank of dirty snow.

"He likes garbage trucks," she said after a while. "Like, loves them. Tuesday's the best day of his life. He stands at the window with a juice box."

"My daughter had a colander," Dale said. "Four years old. Wore it on her head everywhere. Church, the dentist. We had to let her wear it through the metal detector at the courthouse once. I was paying a ticket."

"What happened?"

"It beeped. The guy said, 'Ma'am, the hat.' She said, 'It's a helmet.' He let her through."

"How old is she now?"

"Twenty. She's in Guelph. Does something with soil."

"Soil."

"Tests it. Tells farmers what's wrong with it."

"That's a real job?"

"Apparently."

Ana smiled at her knees. The phone lit up in her hand and she turned it over fast, but not before he saw a line of white text across the top that began *HHS* and then a number. It buzzed against her palm six times. She didn't answer. When it stopped she held it a while longer, as if it might start again, and then she slid it into the duffel and zipped the bag shut.

Near Parkdale the Stelco man had gone, and the teenagers before him. The route emptied out the way it always did, one stop at a time, until the mirror showed only Ana and the boy and the rows of blue seats. Theo's head had gone heavy on her arm.

"What's the worst you've driven in?" she asked.

"Ice. Couple winters back, the one that took the Sherman access down. Buses couldn't make the hill. I put chains on at midnight in my good coat." He shook his head. "Never got the smell out."

"Of what?"

"Wet wool and diesel. Like a dog that got into something."

"Theo threw up in my coat last month and I never got it out either."

"That's a different smell."

"It's a worse smell," she agreed.

At the Eastgate loop he swung the bus around under the sodium lights and set the brake. The doors opened and the cold came in like something with a key. "End of the line," he said. "This one's going back to the garage. I can let you off here, there's a shelter."

She didn't move. She looked at the shelter, which was a plexiglass box with a cracked panel, a bench, and a schedule no one had updated. Beyond it the lot was bare and wet. "Could I ride back with you? Just to where there's a... She's coming to meet me. She said to get close to the garage."

"Close to the garage."

"She works near there."

"Does she have a street?"

"I'll text her." She didn't take out the phone.

"Copy. Bay four. Leave your sheet in the box. Mustafa's on the wash tonight and he'll want the keys."

"Copy."

He closed the doors. He did not say anything to Ana. She sat with her hand on Theo's back and her eyes on the windshield, and that was how they went east on Barton, past the empty lot of a furniture store, past the dark scrapyard, to the gate. The guard lifted the arm without looking up. Dale brought the bus into bay four, shut it down, and the engine's noise fell away until there was only the ticking, louder now.

He set the brake and turned in his seat. The garage was bright and enormous and smelled like wet concrete. Down the row a bus sat with its panels open and a man's legs sticking out from under it.

"This is it," he said. "I've got to clear it. Everybody's got to be off."

"I know." Ana had already stood. She had the duffel on her shoulder and Theo hanging against her chest like a sack of flour. "I know. I'm sorry. She's just —" She looked at the phone, which she had taken out after all. "She's on her way."

"Sure."

He opened the door to the stairwell and then didn't go through it. He picked up the defect card from the clip by the farebox and uncapped the pen. He wrote *Rear heater, ticking, continuous* and the date, the nineteenth, and then stood there with the pen on the line where the time went. Her breath was visible in the cold coming through the open door. Theo slept with his mouth open against her collar.

"Eleven forty-seven, Carla," the radio said. "You in four?"

He pressed the button. "In four."

"You got your card done? I want to get a mechanic on that before the weekend."

"Doing it now. Heater's still going. I'm gonna let it run a few minutes, see if it quits on its own."

"It won't quit on its own, honey."

"Humor me."

"Fine. Don't be an hour." The channel clicked off.

He checked the clock above the bay door. 11:41. The last bus that went near his building left Wentworth at 11:58, a six-minute walk from the gate if he didn't stop. After that nothing ran until the first bus at 5:40, which was twelve minutes before the pullout he'd just said yes to.

Ana said, "You don't have to do anything."

"I know."

"I can walk."

"To where?"

She opened her mouth and shut it. Outside the bay doors the wet on the pavement had gone silver and hard.

Dale put the pen in his pocket. From his back pocket he took his wallet, and from behind the license a folded forty, the one he'd been keeping for his daughter's birthday card, and a ten. He went down the steps and across the bay and out the side door to the guard shack, where the phone on the wall had the cab numbers taped beside it in the guard's block capitals. He dialed. He said the garage, the north gate. He said two passengers, one small.

When he came back she was standing exactly where he had left her, at the foot of the steps, with her hand flat on the boy's back. She was watching the open side door the way you watch a door that might close.

"Five minutes," he said.

"I don't have —"

"It's done."

Headlights crossed the glass. The cab was a gray minivan with a roof light, and the driver leaned across to open the back door. Dale carried the duffel to the curb and set it in the footwell, and it was heavier than he expected. Ana got in sideways, careful of the boy's head. She leaned toward the driver's window and spoke low, two or three sentences. Dale looked at the ground. He heard the driver say, "Yep," and nothing else. He put the fifty through the window and told the man to keep it, and the man said it wouldn't cover it. Dale said, "Then take it as far as it goes."

Ana looked at him through the open door. In the dome light the paper band was visible again where Theo's sleeve had fallen back. She didn't pull it down this time. "Thank you, Dale," she said. She said the name the way you say it to someone you'll be careful not to remember.

The door shut. The cab went out through the gate and turned left, away from the lights of Barton, and was gone.

He went back and finished the card. *11:49,* he wrote. He shut the door of the stairwell, pulled the key and the sheet, and dropped them in the box for Mustafa, who had not come out from under his bus. The heater was still ticking when he walked off, softer now, down the length of the empty bus, as though someone was patient inside it.

At the gate the guard said goodnight. Dale said goodnight. On Wentworth he could see the lit shelter at the corner and, a block past it, the 11:58 already pulling out with its sign glowing and two heads in the windows. He stood on the curb and watched its taillights go up the hill. Then he put his hands in his armpits and started to walk.

The slush had set so that it shattered under his shoes. About halfway down the first block his phone buzzed again, and he stopped under a streetlight and read it with his thumb. *dad? did you see my text.* He typed *Running fine,* looked at it, and held down the button until the letters went away. He wrote *Cold one. Call you Saturday,* and sent it, and put the phone away and kept walking, with the long line of the road ahead of him gone white under the lights.

No Change

The thaw had broken around suppertime, and by nine the nineteenth had turned back into February. Dale Varga walked the bus out of the terminal at 9:47 and the gate clanged shut behind him. The wipers dragged a skin of water off the windshield, then a skin of something slushier. Under the dash the heater ticked, ticked, ticked, a small hard noise with a wrongness in it he had reported twice.

He had the last three hours of his shift. The 3, east to the garage, and then home.

Carla came on the radio while he sat at the light at Wellington.

"All units, Davey's a no-show again, so the five is short. Ken's covering out of the garage at eleven. Dale, you copy?"

"Copy."

"That reader still giving you trouble?"

"It's giving everybody trouble."

"I'll put it in again."

She would, too. She would put it in again the way she put everything in again, tired and exact, her voice coming out of the speaker like it had been worn smooth by use.

Passengers came and went. A nurse at James North. Two teenagers who got off at the library and stood on the curb laughing at the cold. An old woman with a shopping cart she couldn't lift, so Dale got up and lifted it for her, and she said, "You're the tall one," as if he were a landmark.

At the Dofasco gate, Pete got on. Pete had been riding the same shift for years, a big man in a company jacket with a lunch bag and a thermos, and he sat behind the driver's side and talked the way men talk on buses at night, sideways and out of the corner.

"You're going to think I'm crazy," Pete said, "but I'm happy it's cold again. My basement's been full of the thaw since Tuesday. Sump pump's been running like a sewing machine. You could swim in the rec room."

"Two days of it," Dale said.

"Sewage smell right through the house. The wife's got candles going. It's like a funeral home."

The heater ticked. Dale watched the mirror.

"The Bulldogs," Pete said. "I got the tickets from the shop. Do not ask me what the tickets cost."

"I won't."

"Two of the guys from the mill come along, so now it's a whole production, and we're behind two-nothing in the second. I say to the guy next to me, I say, they're going to blow it. And they do not blow it. They come back. Three-two."

That was the conversation for four blocks, hockey and a flooded rec room, and Dale liked it fine, the way he liked any conversation that asked nothing of him.

At the General he pulled in to the curb. The doors opened and the cold came in like somebody had opened a window in a warm room.

She was younger than she looked in the doorway, and younger than that once she got her face in the light. The duffel was navy, new enough to still have the tag on it. The boy was maybe four, in a snowsuit with the hood up, one of his mittens clipped to his sleeve and the other gone.

She tapped the reader. It flashed red at her once, twice.

"It's been doing that all night," Dale said. "It's not you."

She dug in her bag. She had a five folded down small in her palm, and she pushed it at the slot, and it went in sideways and stuck there, half out, jammed.

"Just sit," he said. "It's all right."

"My sister's." She said it fast. "She's expecting us. She works nights, she'll be home around two, but she'll be home."

He took Cannon.

The boy woke up somewhere along and looked out at the streetlights going by. He said something Dale couldn't hear, and Ana said, "Tomorrow," and then, "That's right. Auntie's." The boy said something else. She said, "I know. I know. Look at the moon."

It sat in his head the way things did at the end of a shift. The car had gone back in October and he hadn't told Carla or his daughter. He took the 5:40 in every morning with an earbud in, like anybody going to work. On Sunday Claire had asked how the car was, and he'd said fine, and she'd said she'd come by in the spring and they'd put the summer tires on, and he'd said sure. He hadn't told her. He hadn't told anyone. In the morning he would take the 5:40 again.

"Dale, you in?" Carla said.

"In at the gate."

"Give me a minute."

The boy was asleep, his mouth open against his mother's coat. The duffel sat beside her and she kept one hand on it, the way you hold a bag you packed in a hurry. There was a version of the night where he called Carla and Carla called the shift supervisor, and the supervisor came out with his coat on and asked the woman to step off the property, and it went from there.

He worked the fare box open with his key. The folded five was still wedged in the slot and he had to dig at it, and it came out in two pieces, and he put them in the drawer, got the little metal box out, hung it on the hook.

"Ma'am," he said. The word came out wrong. "We're at the garage. End of the line."

She looked up. He watched her decide what to say.

"This is the end?"

"This is the end. I've got to bring it in and shut it down."

"I thought—" She stopped. "It's not far. From here. I can walk it."

"It's a mile and a half and it's twenty below with the wind."

"I can walk."

The radio clicked. "Dale."

"Copy."

"You're not in. I'm looking at the camera."

"I know it."

So he knew the camera was on him, and he knew what the camera looked like, and he did it anyway. He pulled his gloves off the dash and put them on.

"Come on," he said, and got up.

He carried the duffel. It was heavier than it looked. Ana carried Theo, and the boy put his head down on her shoulder and his hood slipped back, and after three steps Dale took his own gloves off and put them on the boy's hands, one and then the other, because he didn't have any. Then he walked them out of the gate and across Barton, over the ridge of ice the plow had left, and Ana slipped a little and Dale put a hand out and she said, "I've got him."

The Tim Hortons was open. The floor was wet and there was a man at the counter with a mop bucket and a yellow sign. It smelled like coffee and bleach.

He got a hot chocolate and a bagel and a tea, and the boy woke up enough to hold the cup in both hands with Dale's gloves on and said, "These are too big."

"They're a man's gloves," Dale said. "You'll grow."

Ana told him then, not like a confession, like a logistics problem. "It's the shelter on Victoria. Not my sister's. My sister's got the baby and her place is small, and she's — it's not. She doesn't know I'm coming."

"Okay," Dale said.

"They said there's a bed. They said there was a bed at midnight. I just need to get there."

"Okay."

He walked them out and gave the driver forty dollars, which was most of what was in his wallet, and said, "Take them right to the door. Not the corner." The driver said, "For forty I'll take them to the door." Ana got Theo into the back and then stood with the door open.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's a cab," Dale said.

"You know what I mean."

"Get in where it's warm."

"The bus is fine."

"I'll wait out here."

"Fresh air," he said.

Sherman, then the long cold climb, then the Mountain, then the street where he lived, the rows of houses with their porch lights on for people who weren't him.

His phone buzzed in his pocket, once, and he didn't take it out. He put his hood up and started along the sidewalk, past the shuttered tire shop, past the Tim Hortons with its lights still on, the man inside pushing his mop across the wet floor in long slow lines.

The East End Garage

Dale Varga was forty-seven and in his eleventh year with the city, and on Thursday, February 19, he had the 20 for the last three hours of his shift, eastbound from downtown to the garage on the east side. The thaw had laid a sheet of water over King Street early in the evening, and then the temperature had dropped fast enough to skin it over. Under the streetlights the road shone black and careful. Dale kept both hands high on the wheel way through the intersections, watching for the downtown slopes where the ice would take a bus sideways if he let it. The heater rattled in its housing behind his leg, a loose bolt he had mentioned to maintenance twice since Christmas.

He had not owned a car since October. The finance company had taken the Civic in the fall, on a Tuesday, while he was at work, and the empty pad in the apartment lot had stayed empty since. He had not told Carla, who dispatched him, that he now got to the garage on the 21. He had not told Maya either. When his daughter asked how the drive in had been he said fine, and when Carla asked about his parking he said the lot on Wentworth was still alright. He got up at five ten, walked the four blocks to the stop in boots that were already wet through, and rode whatever bus came first, standing by the door with his pass in his hand like everyone else. The drivers who knew him by sight nodded and said nothing.

Carla came on the radio at nine forty, just a click and her voice tired over the static. “Twenty, you copy? Relief’s running twenty minutes behind up on Barton. She’s got a kid with the ice at the school crossing. You’re good to Website?”

“Copy,” Dale said. “I’m good.”

“Don’t rush the east end for me,” she said. “Last run’s the last run.”

He clicked the mic off. Carla was fifty-two and had dispatched the east end for longer than Dale had been driving. She mostly wanted to know where the bus was, whether the relief would make it, and how the minutes were stacking up against the schedule. She had a way of saying a route number twice when she was already looking at the next day.

The hospital stop was at nine fifty-two. The platform was empty except for a woman coming down the steps with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a little boy held sideways against her hip. The boy was four, maybe, in a puffy coat that swallowed him. He was awake when she reached the curb, his legs kicking a little, and asleep by the time she had paid. She was twenty-nine, Dale guessed later, or close enough that it didn’t matter to put a number on it. The duffel was black canvas, zipped shut with a strip of silver tape on the pull. She lifted it onto the step with her free hand and set it down between her feet as if it might wander.

She tapped her fare card. The reader beeped once, declined, beeped again. She tapped it a third time and it took. She didn’t look up at the price. The card was blue, scratched white at the edges. She slid it into the pocket of a coat that was too light for the night and buttoned the coat over the boy’s legs to keep the heat in.

“How far are you going?” Dale asked, because he asked everybody after nine, to know who would still be on at the end.

“Not far,” she said. “My sister’s. Near the east end.”

“Which stop for her?”

She shifted her weight. The boy’s head had fallen forward against her collarbone. “I’ll know it,” she said. “I’ll see the street.”

Dale nodded and pulled away from the curb. In the mirror he saw her taking the second row on the curb side, the duffel between her knees, her hand resting on top of it with her fingers spread. She had no gloves. Her phone was in her other hand, screen lit. She checked it at the light on Main, checked it again at the next one, then turned the screen face down in her lap. A minute later a buzz made the phone jump. She caught it with both hands before it could fall, silenced it, and pressed the boy closer against her chest.

He fell asleep against her with his mouth open. It was a quick, heavy sleep, the kind little kids have in cars when the warmth finally gets to them. His mitten came off and hung by a thread from his sleeve. Dale watched her in the mirror tuck the mitten under his leg and then look out the window at the dark storefronts on Ottawa. She did not give a street name when he had asked. She did not ring the bell when the bus passed the stop by the arena, or the one by the tracks.

At the light on Sherman she said, without looking at him, “That smells good.”

Dale glanced at the thermos in the cup holder. “Lentil,” he said. “My mother’s. She put ham hock in it, but I just do the smoked paprika now.”

“Smoked paprika is enough,” she said.

“She made it in a pot that was always on the back burner,” Dale said. “In February the whole house smelled like it. My ex-wife used to say it was a February soup and nothing else.”

She smiled at the window. “My mother made a soup with the broth from the ham for Easter. For weeks after. Then she’d freeze it and say it was for another holiday.”

The boy snorted in his sleep and settled.

Carla came back on at eleven twenty. “Twenty, change on the board for tomorrow night. King’s closed at John for the water main. You’re on the 20 tomorrow, you’ll detour through Main. Tell your relief if you see her.”

“Copy,” Dale said. “I’ll pass it.”

“Pete’s got the early run if his kid’s fever’s down,” Carla said. “Otherwise you’re double if you want the money.”

Dale said he’d think about it. He didn’t say the double would mean he’d have to ride the bus home in the morning and then ride it back at noon, and that the boots he wore to work were the same boots he wore home, and that the sole on the left one had started to come away at the toe.

The bus was emptying out street by street. A nurse got off at the clinic with a plastic bag of belongings. Two men in coveralls got off at the plant. The woman in the second row stayed. She was still checking her phone every few minutes, the light washing her face. When the bus went through the underpass she pulled the boy’s hood up over his ears. Once, when she thought Dale was watching the road, she held the phone up to her ear and said something he couldn’t hear over the heater. She listened, then put the phone down fast and looked out the window, as if someone might be walking beside the bus.

At Kenilworth she leaned forward a little. “Does this bus go right to the garage?” she asked.

“It ends there,” Dale said.

“Do the drivers have to get off right away?”

He looked at her in the mirror. “Soon as the walkaround’s done.”

She nodded and sat back. The duffel was still between her knees. The tape on the zipper had come half loose and flapped a little when the bus hit a seam in the road. She pressed it down with her thumb.

Dale thought about Maya, nineteen and at Mohawk for a semester she might not finish, who texted him in a shorthand he pretended to understand. He thought about the last time he had been to his ex-wife’s place to fix a leaking trap under her sink, and how she had stood in the doorway with her arms folded and not come into the kitchen. He thought about the way the woman in the second row had said “my sister’s” like a word she was trying on for size. He thought about the phone buzzing and her catching it with both hands. He did not let the thought finish itself. There were enough finished thoughts in a bus at midnight.

The garage was at twelve thirty-one. The yard lights were already on, the big doors rolled up. Dale pulled into bay three, eased the bus up to the chalk line, and set the brake. The engine ticked as it cooled, a small metallic sound that got louder once the road noise was gone. He did the end-of-run things in the order he always did them: mileage, the radio log, the fare box key, the lost-and-found tag for a mitten on the floor by the second row. The fare card reader on the dash, left from her last tap, gave a thin beep every twenty seconds. Dale reached over and pressed it to stop.

Carla’s voice came on the radio while he was still in the seat. “Twenty, clear when you’re clear. I need your out time.”

Dale looked over his shoulder. The woman and the boy were still in the second row. The boy was awake now, sitting up, looking at the garage with sleep in his eyes. His mother had her coat buttoned to the throat and the duffel in her lap. She was not moving toward the door. She was not looking at Dale. She was looking at the side exit of the garage, where the maintenance gate opened onto the street on the far side of the building, away from the office windows.

According to the book he was supposed to radio “bus clear,” step out, and let the night supervisor do his walk. According to the book he was also supposed to tell Carla there were passengers who had not gotten off. Dale knew what would happen next if he did. Carla would call security. Security would come out with a clipboard and ask for names and destinations at twelve forty in the morning. The woman would have to explain the sister and the street she had not named. The boy would stand there cold in his coat. Dale tried to think of another way it could go that did not involve him deciding for her.

He picked up the mic. “Still on the walkaround, Carla,” he said. “Spec’s got a rattle. One more minute.”

“Copy,” she said. “Don’t be late for your bus.”

She meant the bus he took home, the last 21, which left the garage at one oh seven and stopped on the corner by the Tim Hortons that closed at one. He had timed his out time to it for three months so he would not have to wait in the shelter on Wentworth.

Dale turned in his seat. “Side door’s open,” he said quietly. “Maintenance gate.”

She didn’t answer at first. She stood, got the duffel up on her shoulder, and lifted the boy down to the aisle. He put his feet on the step without a sound. Dale reached down and thumbed the side door release, the one the supervisor used when he checked the tire pressure. The door sighed open on its hinge. Cold air came in and lifted the smell of lentil soup up toward the ceiling.

“Thank you,” she said, and she was gone through the gap between the bus and the wall, the boy’s hand in hers, the duffel bumping her hip.

Dale sat for another full minute with the engine off and the heater ticking and the fare reader beeping again. He logged his mileage with a pen that was stiff in the cold and wrote “clear” on the sheet. He radioed Carla at twelve forty-four. She wrote down the time without asking why it was eleven minutes late.

By the time he had his thermos and his bag and had signed out in the office, the 21 had already pulled the corner and turned onto Kenilworth. The stop was empty. The wind was moving the slush in the gutter in thin, bright sheets. Dale pulled his collar up over his ears and started walking west, toward Wentworth, in the boots with the loose sole.

Last Run East

It was Thursday, February 19, and the thaw had left Hamilton black, wet, and treacherous. Dale Varga sat high above Barton Street with both hands on the wheel, forty-seven years old and eleven years into driving for the city, and he kept his right palm near the heater vent because the metal under the dash ticked when it cooled, a sound like a fingernail on glass. The last three hours of his shift were nearly done. The sign above the windshield still read ROUTE 20, but the route was really over: ahead lay only the last few stops and then the garage on the east side, where he would key in, log off, and try not to think about how he would get home.

He had not told Carla that he now rode the bus to work. In the fall the Civic had been taken from his driveway, and since then he had been catching the early 20 downtown, standing in the aisle with his collar up, pretending to be a passenger like everyone else. Carla talked to him on the radio every morning about relief drivers and schedules, and sometimes her voice sounded like it was coming from underwater, tired and practical, and he answered her in the same short phrases he used for everyone else. He had not told her. He had not told his daughter either; she still thought the car existed, and when she asked him to pick her up after her shift he always said no, too far, the route didn’t go that way.

The hospital stop came up wet and glowing. A woman got on with a duffel bag and a little boy, about four, who climbed onto the seat and immediately folded against her shoulder. Dale watched them in the mirror. The woman—Ana—paid with coins, her fingers quick and careful. The boy said, “Hi, bus man,” and she said, “Say hi to the driver,” and when he didn’t, she smiled anyway. “We’re going to my sister’s,” she told Dale. “East end.”

“Whereabouts?” Dale asked, because that was the habit, the question drivers asked so the stop could be called.

She looked at him in the mirror. “Just east. You’ll know it.”

The boy fell asleep against her, his cheek mashed into her coat. Ana checked her phone, pocketed it, checked it again. She never gave a street name.

Carla came on the radio while Dale was still coasting past the escarpment, the mountain a dark lump against the thaw-slick sky. “Twenty, Carla. Any relief for the five at Kenilworth?” she asked. “Because if you’re running late I’ll hold him, but don’t speed on the ice, you know how Barton gets.” Dale keyed the mic. “Twenty, copy. I’m on time.” He didn’t mention the sleeping kid. He didn’t mention anything.

When the boy woke up, restless, Dale asked him what he liked, and the boy said, “Dinos.” Ana laughed, tired. “He only eats stegosaurus cereal now. Says the plates are his armor.” “T-rex has tiny arms,” the boy said. “Stegosaurus wins.” They talked about hot chocolate at the sister’s, about whether the bus went over the big bridge, about the Ti-Cats and whether they’d win if they had a dinosaur on the line. Dale drove through it, letting the slush spray off the tires, and for a few minutes the bus was warm and ordinary and nobody was talking about the thing that was wrong.

But Ana’s phone kept lighting up in her lap, and every time it did she looked at it and then out the window, and she still did not say where the sister lived. Dale noticed the duffel was zipped all the way, that she held it close when the bus braked, that she kept glancing at the mirror as if listening for something. He didn’t ask again. Some things you learned not to push.

He thought about the repossession. The letter had come in October, after the divorce had already taken the house and most of the furniture, and the Civic had gone on a flatbed while he stood in his socks on the porch. He had been putting money aside ever since, thirty-one dollars in an envelope in his locker, because the lot had said Friday. Friday was two days away. If he paid the thirty-one, maybe he could get the car back before the snow really came. He had not told Carla because the garage would have jokes about it by morning. He had not told his daughter because she would have offered him her car, and he could not explain why he couldn’t take it.

The route went east through streets that were melting and freezing at once, black ice under the streetlights. Theo dozed again. Ana stared at her phone. Dale drove with one eye on the road and one on the mirror, and he thought about how the bus would reach the garage with them still aboard, how he would have to decide something before he could leave the bus, and how whatever he chose would cost him one way or another.

Carla came back on the radio near the end: “All units, Route 2 is short-turning at Ottawa because of a watermain on the mountain. East garage, if you’re arriving, flip your sign to garage, key in, log off. Relief for the thirty-five is at Sherman, not here. Don’t dawdle.”

Dale reached the last stop before the garage. He flipped the destination sign and the motor whirred, ROUTE 20 going dark, GARAGE lighting up red. He set the parking brake. The heater under the dash ticked once, loud in the quiet bus, then again, a steady metallic clicking as the engine cooled around them.

The garage loomed ahead, a chain-link yard with lights and parked buses, cold and sodium-yellow. Dale should have opened the doors and called out that the route was done, that everyone off, but Ana and Theo were still in their seat, the boy asleep with his mouth open, the woman awake and watching him in the mirror.

He keyed the mic anyway. “Twenty at east garage,” he said. “Arriving.” Carla’s voice came back flat. “Copy, twenty. Log off and clear. Anything else?” Dale looked at Ana. He looked at the sleeping boy. Outside, the gate to the yard was locked after hours, and the wind off the lake cut through the lot. He had to decide before he could leave the bus.

“Ma’am,” he said, softer than he meant to, “I have to clear this bus here. Where exactly is your sister’s?”

Ana’s thumb stopped moving on the phone. “East end. Past the plaza. You’ll see it.” She did not give a street. Her phone lit up again, and she looked at it, and this time she did not pocket it right away. Dale could see the screen from the mirror, bright and blank to him, then dark again. He wondered if it was a message from the sister. He wondered if there was a sister. The duffel stayed zipped.

“I can’t leave you here,” Dale said. “It’s locked in, it’s freezing, and the boy’s asleep.” He didn’t say the rest. He didn’t say he had no car, no way to drive them, no address, no number.

He reached for the radio again and stopped. The heater ticked. The destination sign hummed. Carla said, “Twenty? Status?” A route change crackled through: something about a bus stuck on Kenilworth, relief shuffled. Practical things. Dale’s hand hovered over the mic.

He looked at Ana. She was crying, but quietly, the way people do when a child is asleep on their shoulder. She shook her head, almost no, and whispered something Dale couldn’t hear, and then she reached into her pocket and pulled out the coins she had paid with, and held them out. “I can pay,” she said. “I can pay you back. Just—please.”

Dale made the choice. He told Carla, “Twenty, I’ve got a situation at the garage,” and he kept the bus running, heater on, and he called a cab on his phone while Ana whispered the address to the driver, too low for Dale to hear, her eyes still on the mirror. The cab would take twenty minutes. The fare to the east end was twenty-eight dollars, and the cab company wouldn’t take coins.

It cost him thirty-one dollars. The envelope in his locker, the money for the lot. The lot had said Friday. Friday was two days away. Dale paid anyway, because Theo was asleep and Ana’s hands were shaking and the garage gate was locked and the night was just after a thaw and the sidewalks were turning to glass. When he paid, he knew the Civic would not be coming home. He knew it the way you know a door has closed. He did not say it out loud.

The cab came, headlights sweeping the lot. Ana gathered the duffel with one arm and the boy with the other; Theo woke blinking and did not cry, just held on. She paused at the cab door and looked back at Dale through the bus window, her phone still lit in her hand, still buzzing, still giving no street. “Thank you,” she said. Dale nodded. He did not know what he was thanking for. The cab pulled away toward the east end, red taillights shrinking into the dark.

Dale sat in the driver’s seat with the engine idling and the heater ticking like a clock. The garage was quiet around him. Carla came on the radio one more time, practical and tired: “Twenty, you still there? Log off.” He flipped the sign, keyed the mic, said, “Twenty, clear,” and reached for the ignition. The metal under the dash ticked once, twice, three times, then stopped. He opened his wallet. It was empty except for the receipt from the cab, twenty-eight dollars, and the bus pass that let him ride to work in the morning. Outside, the last bus toward his apartment had gone at eleven-forty-five. He turned the key.
