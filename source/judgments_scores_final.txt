JUDGES' COMMENTARY
Each judge's note on each story's biggest strength and weakness.

GEMINI HYDROGEN
A: Its greatest strength is complete emotional restraint, letting physical details carry grief and quiet generosity without a single line of explanation, as when Ana displays a screen of unanswered messages where "There wasn't a single grey one in among them." Its only weakness is leaving the October car loss slightly implicit about repossession.
B: Its biggest strength is a clean, readable transit cadence in its opening stops, while its biggest weakness is heavy-handed telling alongside jarring timeline contradictions ("The clock on the dashboard said nine forty-two" after midnight) and missing brief details like Dale's surname, eleven years of service, and Theo's age.
C: Its biggest strength is taut, unsentimental prose and wry voice, exemplified by "Woz could talk for forty years without asking anybody a question." Its biggest weakness is omitting Ana's surname and the explicit date.
D: Its biggest strength is the understated structural echo of Ana's parallel-parking instructions returning in Dale's closing text to his daughter ("Middle of the back window"). Its biggest weakness is departing from the brief by making his daughter seventeen instead of an adult and omitting his eleven years of service and the February 19 date.
E: Its biggest strength is establishing the basic hospital-to-garage setup quickly, while its biggest weakness is falling about 584 words short of the minimum and collapsing into implausible melodrama ("The twenty in his pocket burned") as Dale drives his city bus out of the garage to hunt for an unknown address.
F: Its biggest strength is sharp observational texture, such as the inverted window reflection showing "No SIM." Its biggest weakness is exceeding the 2,300-word ceiling while slipping into American miles and Fahrenheit in Ontario and giving Dale's daughter a high-school workbook.
G: Its biggest strength is natural, understated dialogue between Dale and Theo about dinosaurs ("Brontosaurus isn't a real dinosaur anymore"). Its biggest weakness is running roughly 1,000 words over the 2,300-word ceiling with thin stylistic and emotional texture in the middle stretches, alongside omitting the February 19 date and Theo's age.
H: Its biggest strength is effortless dry humor and concrete physical problem-solving, captured when Dale rakes a lost quarter from under a vending machine with his wiper part-numbers slip ("Australia"). Its biggest weakness is omitting an explicit mention that Theo is four years old.
I: Its biggest strength is the quiet, grounded honesty of Dale finally admitting the October repossession to his daughter outside the motel ("The car's gone"). Its biggest weakness is having the heater tick only after the bus is shut down at the garage rather than during the run.
J: Its biggest strength is its lived-in Hamilton transit rhythm and the quiet weight of Ana zipping Dale's cash inside Theo's snowsuit chest pocket ("Pay the next guy"). Its biggest weakness is making Dale's daughter seventeen rather than an adult and having him catch his bus home anyway, slightly blunting the risk.
K: Its biggest strength is grounded, workaday specificity, from the winter tires in Dale's basement locker to the "smooth brass teeth of the Honda key" still on his ring. Its biggest weakness is running roughly 1,370 words past the 2,300-word cap, which dilutes its pacing.
L: Its biggest strength is crisp, restrained dialogue and small, observant gestures like Dale lending Ana his power bank and Theo pressing the door button ("Not this week"). Its biggest weakness is an overly sparse middle section that omits the February 19 date and Theo's age.
M: Its biggest strength is an unsentimental, realistic ending on the cold Burlington Street curb ("either way he was going to stand here in the cold and find out"). Its biggest weakness is dropping several core brief elements, including Dale's surname, eleven years on the job, the February 19 date, and his adult daughter altogether.
N: Its biggest strength is natural conversational humor between Dale, Ana, and Theo ("Explains why they're late") paired with an unadorned closing. Its biggest weakness is an understated middle stretch that occasionally feels summarized rather than dramatized and omits Theo's age.
O: Its biggest strength is immersive transit authenticity and devastatingly human closure, culminating in Dale walking the wrong way first because "a chance was easier to stand in front of than the whole walk was." Its biggest weakness is running about 1,244 words over the 2,300-word ceiling with slightly leisurely middle transitions.
P: Its biggest strength is the tangible moral cost of Dale sacrificing a needed school extra to drive an out-of-service bus downtown to a shelter ("Union bug"). Its biggest weakness is a slightly crowded middle section with minor detours that soften the tautness of the final act.
Q: Its biggest strength is the sharp, unsentimental closing image of a taxi meter ticking upward ("$4.75 appeared, held for a moment, then climbed") after Dale gives up his employee bus for Ana's ride. Its biggest weakness is slipping into occasional explanatory telling ("Procedures liked clean categories") and omitting Theo's age.
R: Its biggest strength is steady, believable procedural pacing as Dale navigates dispatch, the night desk, and his missed connection ("I can't close the run with the bus at the door"). Its biggest weakness is muted emotional resonance and flat prose texture, alongside omitting the February 19 date and Theo's age.
S: Its biggest strength is warm, natural dialogue and character specificity, highlighted by the anecdote about Dale's daughter wearing a colander through a courthouse metal detector ("Ma'am, the hat"). Its biggest weakness is omitting Dale's eleven years of service and the October repossession backstory.
T: Its biggest strength is its stark, unsentimental ending where Dale's kindness triggers a real workplace suspension ("Three days," she said. "Pending review."). Its biggest weakness is omitting his eleven years of driving.
U: Its biggest strength is tactile atmospheric detail, such as the cold air lifting "the smell of lentil soup up toward the ceiling." GLM 5.3
A: Immaculate show-don't-tell work, with Ana's "screen full of green bubbles" doing the emotional exposition wordlessly. The visitors'-badge/age mix-up and neatened crumbs edge toward too-perfect clockwork.
B: The sacrifice is grounded in real arithmetic, "the shame of being forty-seven and riding the bus to work" lands hard. Leans on stock gestures: tears, a tightened chest, a laugh "carried on the wind like smoke."
C: The Woz and Ernie regulars and the birthday-dinner money give it a lived-in, generous texture. The cab resolves Ana's night a little too tidily for a story that otherwise resists closure.
D: Ana's rental-lot parallel-parking lesson, paid off with "in the middle of your back window. Then full lock," is the best-planted detail in the set. The menacing pickup is deftly handled but left just shy of meaning anything.
E: About two-thirds of the minimum (roughly 1,116 words against 1,700), with contradictory times and a broken heater against the brief's ticking one. Ends on pure melodrama: "The twenty in his pocket burned."
F: The "No SIM" discovery and the ID question ("Martha's") show real investigative empathy and superb texture. At 3,638 words it's over half again the limit, padded with route bookkeeping.
G: The one-word message "I can see your location" is the set's most chilling beat. The middle loops through repetitive phone-glances and Carla exposition to reach its length.
H: "A brown print the shape of Australia" sets a wry voice that the vending-machine quarter hunt pays off beautifully. The cab-app button pressed "anyway" muddies an otherwise clean sequence of choices.
I: The pay envelope with "CAR written on it in pencil" is the best single object in the set. The midnight motel solution, though priced to the dollar, still feels like an off-ramp from the harder scene.
J: Money zipped "inside the boy's snowsuit" tells Dale everything without a word of narration. Almost nothing misfires; the Benny Tran "Where's your car?" ending is the set's best final exchange.
K: Selling the winter tires off the repossessed Civic is a brilliant, bitter economy of backstory. At 3,670 words it drifts, and the sneak-out through the pedestrian gate is staged more than dramatized.
L: The passed-back power bank and "Which one is less real-estate agent?" build intimacy from props and texts. Its restraint tips into mildness; the housing-van arrival is the expected beat, competently done.
M: Clean, workmanlike execution with a nice stranger-couple texture beat around sleeping Theo. The most predictable version of the plot: no image, line, or turn rises above the plan.
N: The gym-teacher coincidence and the stitched strawberry keep warmth in a very spare frame. The suppressed emotional stakes leave the final clicks quietly affecting but low-voltage.
O: The erased texts ("we're here," "almost there") and the wasted tea buy it enormous credibility. At 3,544 words, the Carla-Petrakis-Hassan scheduling threads diffuse the final hour's tension.
P: The union-bug jokes and the $73 school extra give the sacrifice a specific price. At 3,448 words it's overlong, and Theo's cuteness is worked slightly hard.
Q: "Do they bite?" / "Only mittens" is a model of natural child dialogue. The route loops east-west-east grow hard to track, and the unmarked cab's meaning is left smudged.
R: "TICKING—HEAT WORKS" on the defect card is a perfect small joke of professional pride. Nothing is wrong with it and nothing surprises; the tension plateaus early and stays there.
S: "The way you say it to someone you'll be careful not to remember" is the set's finest single sentence. The HHS caller ID names the threat a shade more explicitly than the story needs.
T: The camera line ("You're not in. I'm looking at the camera.") and a three-day suspension give the choice real, rare consequence. "There wasn't any moon" earns its bleakness completely.
U: The lentil-soup exchange and the beeping fare reader give it quiet humanity. Sloppy sentence-making ("kept both hands high on the wheel way through the intersections," "You're good to Website?") undercuts the polish.
V: The stegosaurus-armor bit is a genuine flash of invention. It narrates its own machinery ("he knew it the way you know a door has closed"), summarizing where it should dramatize.

META MUSE SPARK 1.3
A: Biggest strength is precise, lived-in detail and restraint, with the missed westbound carried by objects like "the two arrowroot biscuits." Biggest weakness is a familiar cab-and-cash resolution that slightly softens its originality.
B: Biggest strength is a readable, conventional build and a clear attempt at generosity. Biggest weakness is incoherence in time, route, and motive, with Dale claiming "You said you were going to your sister's" when the narration confirms he hadn't heard her say that, and the ending undoing the missed-bus stakes.
C: Biggest strength is voice and specificity, from the fifty-two dollars to the walk west past the 5:40 pole. Biggest weakness is that the daughter and dinner money thread is resolved too neatly by text while Ana's destination stays thin.
D: Biggest strength is the parallel-parking exchange, where Ana briefly becomes capable and specific rather than only imperilled. Biggest weakness is a contrived extra-shift decision and a daughter thread that conveniently supplies the closing message.
E: Biggest strength is a simple, direct premise and an attempt at a haunting final image, "The twenty in his pocket burned." Biggest weakness is severe underlength, repeated prior meetings that contradict the brief, wrong shift timing, and an implausible ending in which Dale drives off to search for Ana.
F: Biggest strength is dense sensory and procedural credibility, especially the "No SIM" lock screen revealing Ana's story without exposition. Biggest weakness is major overlength and accumulation of detail that slows the final choice.
