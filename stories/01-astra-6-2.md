## The Four-Minute Walk

At nine sixteen Dale Varga turned the bus wide around a hump of ice on King. Yesterday it had been a puddle with a grocery flyer floating in it. Tonight he could read the price of pork chops in his right mirror. The radio clicked twice and Carla said, “Varga, nobody's coming to take your twelve-oh-six. I got two reliefs and they're both on the mountain.”

“Wasn't counting on anybody.”

“Good. Saves me a call.”

Three hours left. At the bottom of his paddle it said THURSDAY 19 FEBRUARY, and below that somebody had printed GARAGE EAST in red, as if it might have moved. In eleven years with the city he'd worked three routes that finished there. This was the only one that got him in before the last westbound went past the gate. Four minutes from the farebox to the stop if he didn't go to his locker. At forty-seven he could still do it in three.

His daughter had sent him a picture at supper of a used Mazda. Thoughts Dad? He'd blown it up to look at the wheel wells and found himself looking instead at a reflection of the photographer in the blue passenger door. A man in slippers on a patch of churned-up snow. He'd sent back Ask for the service records. She still thought he had the Corolla. So did Carla, who once a week asked the drivers to leave the spaces by the salt shed clear and always added, “That includes you, Dale.” He'd parked crooked there exactly once, four years ago.

The hospital stop had a shelter with no glass on its street side. A boy in a green snowsuit stood in the gap, his boots placed carefully together. The woman behind him held a black duffel across her front with both hands as if somebody were about to take a picture of the three of them.

“Does he need to pay?” she asked.

“Not till he's bigger.”

“I'm four,” the boy said, in case there had been a mistake.

“That'll do it.”

The woman's tap went through. She got the boy into the first forward-facing seat and worked his hood down around his shoulders. His hair stood up fine and white in the light, then settled dark against his head. She put the bag at her feet, between her ankles. In the mirror Dale saw her hold her phone close to her mouth. She didn't speak into it. The light went out.

At Wellington he got a red long enough to take a drink. The tea had gone cold and tasted mostly of the rubber ring in the lid. Behind him the woman said, “Theo, if you pull it off you have to carry it yourself.”

“My head is cooking.”

“Fair enough.”

She reached out a hand and took the hat anyway. The boy watched Dale replace the cup in its wire holder. “What's your biggest button?”

“My biggest button? Probably the one for the doors.”

“It's not a button,” the boy said. “It's a little arm.”

“Well, you got me there.”

“What's your biggest actual button?”

Dale had to think about it. He looked over his left elbow. “The horn. Middle of the wheel. That whole part's a button.”

Theo sat back satisfied. Dale was glad he hadn't asked to hear it. Two girls got on with a slab of pink bakery cardboard held level between them. All the way to Ottawa Street he could smell the buttercream. When they stood up the boy said softly, “Cake going,” and by the time the girls made the sidewalk he was asleep against the woman's sleeve.

“Which stop do you want?” Dale asked. “I can give you a heads-up.”

“I'm going to my sister's.”

He waited. Sometimes people gave you the colour of a house or the name of a beer store that had closed in 1998. You could usually work it out.

“She's going to text me,” the woman said.

Out east they had five minutes at the loop. Dale pulled the brake, got out and walked to the back wheel, stepping across the frozen brown ruffles the bus had pushed up on its last trip through the slush. He put his hands in his pockets. Far down the dark there was the long pale building where he would leave the bus. He could see one lit upstairs window. A length of fencing separated him from a shortcut he wasn't supposed to take and never had.

When he got back in, the woman had moved with the boy one row farther from the door. “We go back the other way now,” he said.

“That's okay. Is it okay?”

“Sure.”

On the trip west a man in a quilted vest got on, said “Beautiful out” in a tone that required an answer, and sat down before Dale could give him one. The heater under the first long bench had begun to tick. It did six quick ticks and then waited just long enough for Dale to believe it had quit. He had written it up twice. Once he'd got the bus back with the words UNABLE TO DUPLICATE on a tag round the wheel. He wondered how long they'd sat there.

At the hospital the woman put her hand on the window. The shelter was empty. A plow had come along and shaved the road ice to a pale flour. Dale didn't stop. In the mirror she was wiping away the five little ovals her fingers had made.

His daughter wanted a car because her mother's place was nowhere near her new job. Dale had said he'd go with her to look when she had it down to two. That was the extent of the promise, though he knew she'd counted on him driving them there. He'd have to pick a day and tell her. In October the man who'd come for the Corolla had waited while Dale worked the little plastic garage remote off the visor. Funny thing to have saved. It opened the door at the house where he didn't live anymore.

At the west loop the woman asked, “Do you get to decide how hot it is?”

“Only up here. Out there it's its own boss.”

“It's nice. Our apartment, you can set it for whatever you want. What you get is twenty-six.”

“My daughter used to put her pyjamas in the freezer.”

The woman laughed. “To put on?”

“No, just to take out and complain that now they smelled like fish sticks.”

The laugh woke the boy a little. He pushed his chin deeper into her sleeve. She looked at her phone, turned it over and picked at a peeling corner of the case. Dale changed the sign for the last run east.

By Gage he had the two of them and a young fellow taking home a mop still wrapped in clear plastic. The fellow got off at Parkdale and the woman raised her head at the sound of the doors. It was twelve minutes after midnight. The bus was due in the bay at sixteen. His westbound passed at twenty-three. He'd left his lunch bag looped over his backpack strap so he could pick up both at once.

“Last public stop,” he said. “Next one's inside the garage.”

“I thought there'd be someplace open here.”

“There's the gas station but it's through a drawer this time of night.”

“Right.” She leaned over to get the duffel. The zip had parted along one side; Dale saw a little stack of picture books with library stickers on the spines. There was a bottle brush with a blue handle. She pulled the two halves together and ran the slider back and forth until the teeth caught.

“Stay sitting for now,” he said. “Gets rough going over the tracks.”

Carla came on to tell him bay six was blocked. “Come in on eight and leave it running. Relief's taking it out at quarter to one.”

“Copy eight.”

He brought them in past the long wash windows. Yellow brushes hung there in pairs. The heat inside the garage brought up the smell of the wet grit under his pedals. He pulled into eight beside a bus with its whole back end open and set the brake. In the mirror the woman still had her hand round the zip.

“Is there actually somebody coming?” he asked.

“Could I just wait on here till she answers?”

“They take this one out again.”

“Okay.” She said it like he'd offered her something. The heater ticked away. He opened the lid of the farebox keyboard and keyed in the end of his run. It asked for his mileage, which he got wrong, then it wouldn't let him go back. He put his finger on CLEAR and held it there while the woman took out her phone and showed him a screen full of green bubbles. He couldn't read them from where he sat. There wasn't a single grey one in among them.

“That's my sister,” she said.

The keyboard gave one long beep. Dale put in the right numbers. On the clock above the wash windows it was eighteen past.

“Carla, I've got two passengers for the office. Can I bring them through?”

“What do they need?”

“A warm place to make a call.”

There was a gap, then Carla said, “Visitors have to be signed in and sat with. I can't come down. I'm trying to get this relief in a cab at the moment.”

He could direct them to the gate. There was time to get them on the westbound with him; she'd have another forty minutes to sit and look at her phone. He pictured himself standing to pull the cord at his own corner, putting his gloves on while she watched.

“I'll sit,” he said, and unclipped his belt.

He took the duffel so she could carry the boy. At the steps Theo opened his eyes and said, quite distinctly, “Not here.”

“Just for a minute,” she said.

The office had a wired-glass door and two orange chairs bolted to a length of board. Dale filled in the date on the visitors' sheet, crossed out Thursday and put Friday instead. She printed ANA RUIZ in the first space and THEO in the second. In the column marked BADGE she wrote 29, then said, “Oh. I thought it said age.”

“It's for me anyway.” He put his number in next to hers. Through the glass the westbound went past on the street, glowing like a shop somebody had forgotten to close. The cab home last time he'd missed it had been thirty-eight dollars before tip. He set his lunch bag down.

Ana plugged her phone into the outlet by the chairs. “Could I use a phone that isn't mine?”

He pushed the desk set across to her and showed her the nine for an outside line. She didn't consult the screen of her own phone to dial. He turned his chair toward the window to give her what privacy there was. Behind him he heard, “It's me. No, don't hang up. Can you just listen for a second?”

Theo had slipped down off her lap. He stood by Dale's knee holding his hat. Across the bay a mechanic lowered the back panel of the broken bus, wiped both palms down his thighs and tried it again when it didn't catch.

“Is this where you wash them?” Theo whispered.

“Through there.” Dale pointed out the yellow brushes. “See the black marks? That's where they go along the windows.”

The boy pushed a finger against the wired glass and traced the height of a bus that wasn't there. Behind them Ana was quiet. Dale could hear a voice now, thin and busy in the earpiece. He drew his lunch bag over with his foot and felt in the side pocket for the two arrowroot biscuits he always brought and never wanted. He held up the packet where Ana could see. She nodded without taking the phone away.

Theo accepted one biscuit, considered the other through the cellophane, then broke his own in half against the edge of the desk. He slid half under his mother's free hand. Crumbs lodged in the ruled lines of the visitors' sheet. Dale lifted the clipboard so the boy could brush them into his palm.
