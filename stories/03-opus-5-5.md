## Pull-In

The thaw had lasted two days. It was long enough to wear the snowbanks down to gravel and grey lace, and now the cold had come back to lock everything in place. Dale Varga felt it through the floor every time the front doors folded open: a breath of air that smelled like wet iron and the lake.

Three hours left. Then pull-in at the east garage, the vault, the defect card, and the walk across the yard to the gate. Nobody at the garage knew what he did once he was past the gate. He had kept it that way since October, when a man with a clipboard and a tow truck took the Ranger out of the lot behind his building at six in the morning while Dale stood on the step in his socks.

Around nine the heater over the front door had started ticking. It wasn't a rattle. It was a tick, steady as a clock somebody had hidden in the ceiling panel. He'd tried thumping the panel at a red light and it kept going. He added it to the list in his head for the defect card: heater, front, ticking. Wiper, driver side, chatters.

At 10:40 he pulled into the hospital stop on Barton. A woman stood under the shelter light with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a small boy pressed against her leg. She waited until the doors were fully open before she moved, as if she didn't trust them to stay that way.

She tapped her card. The reader beeped low and flashed red.

"Try it again," Dale said.

She tried. Red.

"Go on, sit down."

"I had money on it," she said. "I'm going to my sister's."

He hadn't asked. He nodded anyway and waited while she steered the boy down the aisle. She took the first forward-facing seat behind the wheelchair area, where he could see her in the big mirror without turning his head. The boy was maybe four. He had on a snowsuit jacket, flannel pyjama pants with rockets on them, and running shoes with soles that blinked red each time his feet swung. No boots. A pillowcase stuck out of the duffel's zipper like a tongue.

When she pushed her hair back, Dale saw a white plastic band on her wrist. She saw him see it and tugged her sleeve down over it, and he looked at the road.

The radio clicked. "Four-one-two, dispatch."

"Four-one-two."

"Varga, it's Carla. Delgado called off for tomorrow. I've got the five-forty sitting empty. You want it?"

Five-forty meant signing on at five-ten. He'd be pulling in around one. He did the arithmetic on getting home and back and didn't like it.

"Can I think about it?"

"Think fast. I've got a whole board to fill and Pritchard's back is out again." She sounded like she was eating something. "And the Mountain's glazing over, so if anybody tells you they're running late up the Jolley Cut, believe them."

"Copy."

He went east. Barton was mostly dark storefronts and the bright square of a pizza place, then a long stretch where the houses sat right up against the sidewalk with their porch lights off. A man got on at Sherman, rode four stops, got off. Two teenagers in the back shared earbuds and laughed at something on a phone. Every few minutes, Dale glanced up and saw the woman's face lit blue from below. She would check the phone, put it face down on her thigh, then turn it over and check it again.

By Ottawa Street the boy had fallen asleep against her. His mouth hung open and his hand was fisted in her coat. One running shoe had come half off his heel and kept blinking.

At the Kenilworth light a pickup pulled up in the lane beside him. It was dark, with a lift kit and a rack of lights across the cab roof that wasn't switched on. In the mirror Dale watched the woman bend down over the boy as if she was fixing his zipper. She stayed bent over through the whole red. When the light changed, the pickup went off ahead of them, turned right at the next corner, and was gone. She sat up slowly. She didn't look out the window. She looked at her phone.

Dale drove.

At the loop where the teenagers got off, the bus was quiet except for the heater. Tick. Tick. He could hear the boy breathing, a small wet click at the end of each breath.

"Does it always do that?" the woman said.

"The heater? Started tonight."

"It's like a turn signal that won't turn off."

"I'll write it up. They'll ignore it for a month." He checked the mirror. "What's your name?"

She paused a little, then said, "Ana."

"Dale."

"I know. It's on the sign." She pointed at the operator card in its plastic sleeve by the door. Then she smiled for the first time, a quick tired smile that came and went. "Sorry. I'm not usually like this."

"Like what?"

"On a bus at midnight." She shifted the boy's weight. "This is Theo. He'll sleep through anything except me trying to put him down."

"My daughter was like that. Still is, kind of. She's seventeen."

"Oh no."

"Yeah." He signalled and pulled out around a parked hydro truck. "She failed her road test last week. Parallel parking. She's taking it again Saturday."

"Nobody in Hamilton parallel parks. They just stop in the middle of the road with the four-ways on."

He laughed, which surprised him. "That's what I told her."

"I used to move cars at the airport. For one of the rental places." Ana sat up a little, interested in something for once. "Tell her this. Pull up even with the car in front, mirrors lined up. Back up slow until you see that car's back bumper in the middle of your back window. Then full lock. When your front door's even with their bumper, straighten out, then full lock the other way."

"Back bumper, middle of the window."

"It works every time. They make you practise it with a shuttle van, and if you can do it in that, you can do it in anything."

"I'll tell her."

"Do you drive her? To practise?"

The question was simple and he answered it the way he'd been answering it since October. "When I can. Weekends, mostly."

"And you drive home after this? After driving all night?"

"Truck's in the lot," he said.

She nodded and went back to her phone. He watched her thumb hover over the screen, type something, delete it, type again. She didn't send it, or if she did, nothing came back. She put the phone face down.

The stops went by with nobody at them. Dale called them anyway, the way he'd been trained, his voice going out over the empty seats. Past the plaza with the shuttered Zellers sign still ghosted on the brick. Past the rail bridge where the slush had frozen into ruts that rocked the bus side to side. Theo didn't wake.

Two stops from the end, he said, "Ana. Where's your sister's? I'll let you off closest."

"It's okay. Keep going."

"Next one's the last real stop. After that I'm out of service to the garage."

"I know." She was looking at the phone. "She's going to text me."

He let the last stop go by. He told himself it was because he was waiting for her to ring the bell, but she didn't ring it, and he didn't stop. He flipped the destination sign to NOT IN SERVICE and turned into the garage lane. The yard was a big sodium-orange rectangle of parked buses lined up in rows, their roofs frosted where the melt had refrozen. Benny was in the fuel lane in his reflective coat, waving him in with a flashlight.

Dale stopped at the pump and set the brake. The heater ticked.

"Ana."

"I know." She didn't move. "Is there one going back the other way?"

"Not from here. Not till about five."

Benny knocked on the door glass. Dale opened it, and the cold came in with him.

"You're heavy tonight," Benny said, looking past him down the aisle. He kept his voice low because of the boy.

"Give me a minute."

"I don't see nothing. But Rourke's in the office." Benny stuck the nozzle in and walked off to the next lane.

Dale pulled the vault key off his ring and bent to the farebox. You had to seat the probe and hold it until the light went green, and tonight the light kept blinking amber, so he pulled the probe and seated it again. He could feel Ana watching him in the mirror the way he'd watched her for two hours.

Rule was rule. No passengers in the yard. If he radioed it in, Carla would do what she was supposed to do, which was call the supervisor, and the supervisor would call the police to do a wellness check. A cruiser would pull up in the fuel lane with its lights going across all those frosted roofs. Ana would have to say a street name then, and a last name, and whatever the bracelet on her wrist said.

Or he could open the doors at the gate and let her walk out onto Barton at one in the morning with a sleeping four-year-old in running shoes, and it was minus eleven.

The probe light went green. He pulled it, logged the vault, and wrote on the defect card: *Heater front ticking. Wiper DS chatters.* His hand was steady. He was almost proud of that.

"Four-one-two, dispatch. You in? I've got you sitting at the pump."

"I'm in. Fuelling."

"Did you think about the five-forty?"

He looked at Ana. She had turned the phone over again, and the screen was empty.

"Put me down for it," he said.

"Bless you. Five-ten sign-on. And the Mountain's a skating rink, so drive safe going home."

"Will do."

He took out his phone and called Hamilton Cab. They said twenty minutes, maybe thirty with the ice. Then he took out his wallet. Inside, folded separately from his other money so he wouldn't spend it, were three twenties. That was the rebooking fee for Saturday, plus a little extra so Mia could get a coffee afterwards, pass or fail. He'd promised her that on the phone on Sunday, and he'd said it like it was nothing.

He held the money out toward the mirror before he could start thinking about it.

"Cab's coming to the gate. That's for the cab. And whatever."

Ana looked at the bills and didn't take them. "I can't."

"You can tell him where you're going. You don't have to tell me."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood up carefully with Theo still on her shoulder, came to the front, and took the money. She folded it into her coat pocket without counting it.

"She works nights," Ana said. "My sister. That's why she's not answering."

"Okay."

They waited on the bus with the doors shut and the heater ticking until he saw headlights swing in at the gate. Dale carried the duffel. It was heavier than it looked, and something hard was rolled up in the middle of the clothes, maybe a picture frame. Ana carried Theo. The boy's shoe blinked red against her hip the whole way across the yard. Dale didn't look toward the office window, where Rourke might or might not be standing.

The cab driver popped the trunk without getting out. Dale put the duffel in and shut it. Ana slid into the back seat with Theo on her lap and leaned forward to say something to the driver. Dale stepped back so he wouldn't hear it.

She rolled the window down a few inches.

"Middle of the back window," she said. "Then full lock."

"Got it."

The cab pulled out onto Barton and went west, toward the city. Its tail lights got small, then turned and disappeared.

Across the road, the last westbound was sitting at the stop with its interior lights on and no one inside. Dale lifted his hand. The doors closed. It pulled away from the curb, and he watched it go all the way to the light at the next corner, where it turned green for it.

He checked his phone. It was 1:14. He opened the thread with Mia. Her last message was from Sunday, a thumbs-up and the word *Saturday!!* He typed: *Pull up even with the car in front. Back up till you see their back bumper in the middle of your back window. Then full lock.*

He read it twice. He didn't add anything about the money. He sent it.

Then he zipped his jacket up to the chin, put his hands in his pockets, and started west along Barton, walking on the frozen ruts where the salt hadn't reached.
