## No Change

The thaw had broken around suppertime, and by nine the nineteenth had turned back into February. Dale Varga walked the bus out of the terminal at 9:47 and the gate clanged shut behind him. The wipers dragged a skin of water off the windshield, then a skin of something slushier. Under the dash the heater ticked, ticked, ticked, a small hard noise with a wrongness in it he had reported twice.

He had the last three hours of his shift. The 3, east to the garage, and then home.

Carla came on the radio while he sat at the light at Wellington.

"All units, Davey's a no-show again, so the five is short. Ken's covering out of the garage at eleven. Dale, you copy?"

"Copy."

"That reader still giving you trouble?"

"It's giving everybody trouble."

"I'll put it in again."

She would, too. She would put it in again the way she put everything in again, tired and exact, her voice coming out of the speaker like it had been worn smooth by use.

Passengers came and went. A nurse at James North. Two teenagers who got off at the library and stood on the curb laughing at the cold. An old woman with a shopping cart she couldn't lift, so Dale got up and lifted it for her, and she said, "You're the tall one," as if he were a landmark.

At the Dofasco gate, Pete got on. Pete had been riding the same shift for years, a big man in a company jacket with a lunch bag and a thermos, and he sat behind the driver's side and talked the way men talk on buses at night, sideways and out of the corner.

"You're going to think I'm crazy," Pete said, "but I'm happy it's cold again. My basement's been full of the thaw since Tuesday. Sump pump's been running like a sewing machine. You could swim in the rec room."

"Two days of it," Dale said.

"Sewage smell right through the house. The wife's got candles going. It's like a funeral home."

The heater ticked. Dale watched the mirror.

"The Bulldogs," Pete said. "I got the tickets from the shop. Do not ask me what the tickets cost."

"I won't."

"Two of the guys from the mill come along, so now it's a whole production, and we're behind two-nothing in the second. I say to the guy next to me, I say, they're going to blow it. And they do not blow it. They come back. Three-two."

That was the conversation for four blocks, hockey and a flooded rec room, and Dale liked it fine, the way he liked any conversation that asked nothing of him.

At the General he pulled in to the curb. The doors opened and the cold came in like somebody had opened a window in a warm room.

She was younger than she looked in the doorway, and younger than that once she got her face in the light. The duffel was navy, new enough to still have the tag on it. The boy was maybe four, in a snowsuit with the hood up, one of his mittens clipped to his sleeve and the other gone.

She tapped the reader. It flashed red at her once, twice.

"It's been doing that all night," Dale said. "It's not you."

She dug in her bag. She had a five folded down small in her palm, and she pushed it at the slot, and it went in sideways and stuck there, half out, jammed.

"Just sit," he said. "It's all right."

She sat halfway back, the boy by the window. He was asleep before the next stop, slid down, his cheek on her arm. She held him with one hand and her phone in the other.

In the mirror: she typed, she held the phone to her ear, she put it down and typed again, she held it up again, and then she sat with it in her lap a while and leaned her head back against the glass.

"Whereabouts?" he said, at the light at Sherman.

"My sister's." She said it fast. "She's expecting us. She works nights, she'll be home around two, but she'll be home."

"You want the Ottawa stop, or—"

"Past the centre," she said. "I'll tell you."

The reader was still red. The heater ticked.

"All east-end units," Carla said, "Barton's closed at Sherman, water main's gone in the ground, take Cannon to Gage."

He took Cannon.

The boy woke up somewhere along and looked out at the streetlights going by. He said something Dale couldn't hear, and Ana said, "Tomorrow," and then, "That's right. Auntie's." The boy said something else. She said, "I know. I know. Look at the moon."

There wasn't any moon.

Her phone lit her face again. "Hey. Hey, it's me. Call me. It's—" She listened. Then, to the phone, not to Dale: "I know what time it is."

Dale drove.

It sat in his head the way things did at the end of a shift. The car had gone back in October and he hadn't told Carla or his daughter. He took the 5:40 in every morning with an earbud in, like anybody going to work. On Sunday Claire had asked how the car was, and he'd said fine, and she'd said she'd come by in the spring and they'd put the summer tires on, and he'd said sure. He hadn't told her. He hadn't told anyone. In the morning he would take the 5:40 again.

At 12:36 he turned in at the yard. The gate lights were on and the chain-link shone. Vic was in the little black booth with the window up. The fuel lane lights were bright and wet, and the buses sat in their rows with their noses to the fence.

The bus reached the garage with Ana and Theo still aboard.

He stopped at the barrier and put it in park. The doors stayed shut. The heater ticked.

"Dale, you in?" Carla said.

"In at the gate."

"Vic's got the gate. You're the last one. Come on in and I'll sign the board." Then, when he didn't answer right away: "Dale."

"Give me a minute."

The boy was asleep, his mouth open against his mother's coat. The duffel sat beside her and she kept one hand on it, the way you hold a bag you packed in a hurry. There was a version of the night where he called Carla and Carla called the shift supervisor, and the supervisor came out with his coat on and asked the woman to step off the property, and it went from there.

He worked the fare box open with his key. The folded five was still wedged in the slot and he had to dig at it, and it came out in two pieces, and he put them in the drawer, got the little metal box out, hung it on the hook.

"Ma'am," he said. The word came out wrong. "We're at the garage. End of the line."

She looked up. He watched her decide what to say.

"This is the end?"

"This is the end. I've got to bring it in and shut it down."

"I thought—" She stopped. "It's not far. From here. I can walk it."

"It's a mile and a half and it's twenty below with the wind."

"I can walk."

The radio clicked. "Dale."

"Copy."

"You're not in. I'm looking at the camera."

"I know it."

So he knew the camera was on him, and he knew what the camera looked like, and he did it anyway. He pulled his gloves off the dash and put them on.

"Come on," he said, and got up.

He carried the duffel. It was heavier than it looked. Ana carried Theo, and the boy put his head down on her shoulder and his hood slipped back, and after three steps Dale took his own gloves off and put them on the boy's hands, one and then the other, because he didn't have any. Then he walked them out of the gate and across Barton, over the ridge of ice the plow had left, and Ana slipped a little and Dale put a hand out and she said, "I've got him."

The Tim Hortons was open. The floor was wet and there was a man at the counter with a mop bucket and a yellow sign. It smelled like coffee and bleach.

"Sit down," Dale said. "I'll get something."

He got a hot chocolate and a bagel and a tea, and the boy woke up enough to hold the cup in both hands with Dale's gloves on and said, "These are too big."

"They're a man's gloves," Dale said. "You'll grow."

Ana told him then, not like a confession, like a logistics problem. "It's the shelter on Victoria. Not my sister's. My sister's got the baby and her place is small, and she's — it's not. She doesn't know I'm coming."

"Okay," Dale said.

"They said there's a bed. They said there was a bed at midnight. I just need to get there."

"Okay."

He called the cab from his phone. It took eight minutes. He stood at the window and watched the street. The cab came, a white sedan with a light on the roof.

He walked them out and gave the driver forty dollars, which was most of what was in his wallet, and said, "Take them right to the door. Not the corner." The driver said, "For forty I'll take them to the door." Ana got Theo into the back and then stood with the door open.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's a cab," Dale said.

"You know what I mean."

"Get in where it's warm."

The cab went west and Dale walked back across Barton. The wind came down off the bay and the cold was so dry it made the inside of his nose ache. Vic was out of his booth now, standing by the gate with his hands in his pockets, looking at the asphalt.

Carla was at the garage door with her coat over her shoulders and her purse under her arm. She had the board in her hand.

"Three days," she said. "Pending review. You know the policy, Dale. I have to write it up."

"The bus is fine."

"The bus is fine. That's not the thing." She looked at him a long time. "You want to come in? It's warm in there."

"I'll wait out here."

"For what?"

"Fresh air," he said.

She stood a moment more, and then she went in, and the door came down slow behind her, the glass rattling in it.

The yard lights buzzed. Inside the fence the buses sat in their rows, catching it. His phone said 1:12. The last eastbound had gone through at 1:08 and the next one was at 5:12, if the 5:12 was running, which on a Thursday after a thaw it usually was. He looked up the hill. Sherman, then the long cold climb, then the Mountain, then the street where he lived, the rows of houses with their porch lights on for people who weren't him.

His phone buzzed in his pocket, once, and he didn't take it out. He put his hood up and started along the sidewalk, past the shuttered tire shop, past the Tim Hortons with its lights still on, the man inside pushing his mop across the wet floor in long slow lines.
