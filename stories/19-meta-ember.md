## The East End Garage

Dale Varga was forty-seven and in his eleventh year with the city, and on Thursday, February 19, he had the 20 for the last three hours of his shift, eastbound from downtown to the garage on the east side. The thaw had laid a sheet of water over King Street early in the evening, and then the temperature had dropped fast enough to skin it over. Under the streetlights the road shone black and careful. Dale kept both hands high on the wheel way through the intersections, watching for the downtown slopes where the ice would take a bus sideways if he let it. The heater rattled in its housing behind his leg, a loose bolt he had mentioned to maintenance twice since Christmas.

He had not owned a car since October. The finance company had taken the Civic in the fall, on a Tuesday, while he was at work, and the empty pad in the apartment lot had stayed empty since. He had not told Carla, who dispatched him, that he now got to the garage on the 21. He had not told Maya either. When his daughter asked how the drive in had been he said fine, and when Carla asked about his parking he said the lot on Wentworth was still alright. He got up at five ten, walked the four blocks to the stop in boots that were already wet through, and rode whatever bus came first, standing by the door with his pass in his hand like everyone else. The drivers who knew him by sight nodded and said nothing.

Carla came on the radio at nine forty, just a click and her voice tired over the static. “Twenty, you copy? Relief’s running twenty minutes behind up on Barton. She’s got a kid with the ice at the school crossing. You’re good to Website?”

“Copy,” Dale said. “I’m good.”

“Don’t rush the east end for me,” she said. “Last run’s the last run.”

He clicked the mic off. Carla was fifty-two and had dispatched the east end for longer than Dale had been driving. She mostly wanted to know where the bus was, whether the relief would make it, and how the minutes were stacking up against the schedule. She had a way of saying a route number twice when she was already looking at the next day.

The hospital stop was at nine fifty-two. The platform was empty except for a woman coming down the steps with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a little boy held sideways against her hip. The boy was four, maybe, in a puffy coat that swallowed him. He was awake when she reached the curb, his legs kicking a little, and asleep by the time she had paid. She was twenty-nine, Dale guessed later, or close enough that it didn’t matter to put a number on it. The duffel was black canvas, zipped shut with a strip of silver tape on the pull. She lifted it onto the step with her free hand and set it down between her feet as if it might wander.

She tapped her fare card. The reader beeped once, declined, beeped again. She tapped it a third time and it took. She didn’t look up at the price. The card was blue, scratched white at the edges. She slid it into the pocket of a coat that was too light for the night and buttoned the coat over the boy’s legs to keep the heat in.

“How far are you going?” Dale asked, because he asked everybody after nine, to know who would still be on at the end.

“Not far,” she said. “My sister’s. Near the east end.”

“Which stop for her?”

She shifted her weight. The boy’s head had fallen forward against her collarbone. “I’ll know it,” she said. “I’ll see the street.”

Dale nodded and pulled away from the curb. In the mirror he saw her taking the second row on the curb side, the duffel between her knees, her hand resting on top of it with her fingers spread. She had no gloves. Her phone was in her other hand, screen lit. She checked it at the light on Main, checked it again at the next one, then turned the screen face down in her lap. A minute later a buzz made the phone jump. She caught it with both hands before it could fall, silenced it, and pressed the boy closer against her chest.

He fell asleep against her with his mouth open. It was a quick, heavy sleep, the kind little kids have in cars when the warmth finally gets to them. His mitten came off and hung by a thread from his sleeve. Dale watched her in the mirror tuck the mitten under his leg and then look out the window at the dark storefronts on Ottawa. She did not give a street name when he had asked. She did not ring the bell when the bus passed the stop by the arena, or the one by the tracks.

At the light on Sherman she said, without looking at him, “That smells good.”

Dale glanced at the thermos in the cup holder. “Lentil,” he said. “My mother’s. She put ham hock in it, but I just do the smoked paprika now.”

“Smoked paprika is enough,” she said.

“She made it in a pot that was always on the back burner,” Dale said. “In February the whole house smelled like it. My ex-wife used to say it was a February soup and nothing else.”

She smiled at the window. “My mother made a soup with the broth from the ham for Easter. For weeks after. Then she’d freeze it and say it was for another holiday.”

The boy snorted in his sleep and settled.

Carla came back on at eleven twenty. “Twenty, change on the board for tomorrow night. King’s closed at John for the water main. You’re on the 20 tomorrow, you’ll detour through Main. Tell your relief if you see her.”

“Copy,” Dale said. “I’ll pass it.”

“Pete’s got the early run if his kid’s fever’s down,” Carla said. “Otherwise you’re double if you want the money.”

Dale said he’d think about it. He didn’t say the double would mean he’d have to ride the bus home in the morning and then ride it back at noon, and that the boots he wore to work were the same boots he wore home, and that the sole on the left one had started to come away at the toe.

The bus was emptying out street by street. A nurse got off at the clinic with a plastic bag of belongings. Two men in coveralls got off at the plant. The woman in the second row stayed. She was still checking her phone every few minutes, the light washing her face. When the bus went through the underpass she pulled the boy’s hood up over his ears. Once, when she thought Dale was watching the road, she held the phone up to her ear and said something he couldn’t hear over the heater. She listened, then put the phone down fast and looked out the window, as if someone might be walking beside the bus.

At Kenilworth she leaned forward a little. “Does this bus go right to the garage?” she asked.

“It ends there,” Dale said.

“Do the drivers have to get off right away?”

He looked at her in the mirror. “Soon as the walkaround’s done.”

She nodded and sat back. The duffel was still between her knees. The tape on the zipper had come half loose and flapped a little when the bus hit a seam in the road. She pressed it down with her thumb.

Dale thought about Maya, nineteen and at Mohawk for a semester she might not finish, who texted him in a shorthand he pretended to understand. He thought about the last time he had been to his ex-wife’s place to fix a leaking trap under her sink, and how she had stood in the doorway with her arms folded and not come into the kitchen. He thought about the way the woman in the second row had said “my sister’s” like a word she was trying on for size. He thought about the phone buzzing and her catching it with both hands. He did not let the thought finish itself. There were enough finished thoughts in a bus at midnight.

The garage was at twelve thirty-one. The yard lights were already on, the big doors rolled up. Dale pulled into bay three, eased the bus up to the chalk line, and set the brake. The engine ticked as it cooled, a small metallic sound that got louder once the road noise was gone. He did the end-of-run things in the order he always did them: mileage, the radio log, the fare box key, the lost-and-found tag for a mitten on the floor by the second row. The fare card reader on the dash, left from her last tap, gave a thin beep every twenty seconds. Dale reached over and pressed it to stop.

Carla’s voice came on the radio while he was still in the seat. “Twenty, clear when you’re clear. I need your out time.”

Dale looked over his shoulder. The woman and the boy were still in the second row. The boy was awake now, sitting up, looking at the garage with sleep in his eyes. His mother had her coat buttoned to the throat and the duffel in her lap. She was not moving toward the door. She was not looking at Dale. She was looking at the side exit of the garage, where the maintenance gate opened onto the street on the far side of the building, away from the office windows.

According to the book he was supposed to radio “bus clear,” step out, and let the night supervisor do his walk. According to the book he was also supposed to tell Carla there were passengers who had not gotten off. Dale knew what would happen next if he did. Carla would call security. Security would come out with a clipboard and ask for names and destinations at twelve forty in the morning. The woman would have to explain the sister and the street she had not named. The boy would stand there cold in his coat. Dale tried to think of another way it could go that did not involve him deciding for her.

He picked up the mic. “Still on the walkaround, Carla,” he said. “Spec’s got a rattle. One more minute.”

“Copy,” she said. “Don’t be late for your bus.”

She meant the bus he took home, the last 21, which left the garage at one oh seven and stopped on the corner by the Tim Hortons that closed at one. He had timed his out time to it for three months so he would not have to wait in the shelter on Wentworth.

Dale turned in his seat. “Side door’s open,” he said quietly. “Maintenance gate.”

She didn’t answer at first. She stood, got the duffel up on her shoulder, and lifted the boy down to the aisle. He put his feet on the step without a sound. Dale reached down and thumbed the side door release, the one the supervisor used when he checked the tire pressure. The door sighed open on its hinge. Cold air came in and lifted the smell of lentil soup up toward the ceiling.

“Thank you,” she said, and she was gone through the gap between the bus and the wall, the boy’s hand in hers, the duffel bumping her hip.

Dale sat for another full minute with the engine off and the heater ticking and the fare reader beeping again. He logged his mileage with a pen that was stiff in the cold and wrote “clear” on the sheet. He radioed Carla at twelve forty-four. She wrote down the time without asking why it was eleven minutes late.

By the time he had his thermos and his bag and had signed out in the office, the 21 had already pulled the corner and turned onto Kenilworth. The stop was empty. The wind was moving the slush in the gutter in thin, bright sheets. Dale pulled his collar up over his ears and started walking west, toward Wentworth, in the boots with the loose sole.
