## The Last Connection

At nine-oh-three, the heater under Dale Varga’s seat began ticking again. Three ticks, a pause, then a fourth, heavier one. He pressed his heel against the metal housing until it stopped.

Outside, Hamilton shone under a skin of ice. The thaw had left puddles across the sidewalks, and now the cold had tightened them around cigarette ends and frozen leaves. At each stop he brought the bus close to the curb, but not too close. Ice could carry a person’s foot straight under a tire.

Three hours left. Then the garage, the westbound night bus, a transfer downtown. If everything held, he would be in his kitchen by one.

He still caught himself calculating how long the drive would take.

At the hospital stop, a woman stood back from the curb with a duffel over one shoulder and a little boy leaning against her leg. Dale lowered the bus.

“Watch that first bit,” he said.

She lifted the boy over the gutter. Her boot slid, caught, and she came aboard without looking at Dale.

“Two,” she said, holding her bank card against the reader.

“He’s free.”

The reader flashed red.

She tried again.

“Give it a second.”

Behind her, the boy examined the yellow line with grave attention. His coat zipper had been started crooked, leaving one side higher than the other.

The card flashed red again.

“Go ahead,” Dale said.

“I have money.”

“It’s the machine.”

It might have been. Eleven years driving for the city had taught him not to make promises on behalf of a fare reader.

She took the first forward-facing seat behind the accessible area. The boy climbed beside her, then across her lap.

“Feet down, Theo.”

He lowered one boot.

Dale closed the doors.

At the next light he saw her reflection. Twenty-nine, perhaps thirty; about the age his daughter would be in another seven years. Dark hair pulled through the back of a cap. Hospital bracelet? No, a pale cuff showing between glove and sleeve. He looked back to the road.

“Do you go near the east end?” she asked.

“Eventually.”

“I’m going to my sister’s.”

“What street?”

She bent to adjust the boy’s boot. “I’ll know when we’re close.”

He met that answer several times a week. Usually from someone who had been drinking, sometimes from someone who hadn’t ridden a bus in twenty years. He told her they would turn north after the plaza.

“Okay.”

Her phone lit her face. She held it near her mouth, then lowered it without speaking.

***

Carla came over the radio at nine-thirty.

“Varga, your relief tomorrow is Singh, not Bell. Bell’s still off.”

“Copy.”

“And keep your running time through the construction. We’ve had complaints about early departures.”

“I’m four down.”

“Then you’re doing beautifully.”

He smiled despite himself.

At the plaza he waited through his recovery time while two men argued outside the closed pharmacy. The woman’s boy had fallen asleep against her chest. One small hand rested inside the open collar of her coat.

She caught Dale looking in the mirror.

“He likes buses,” she said.

“Picked a good night for it.”

“He likes the accordion ones better.”

“Everybody does until they have to reverse one.”

“You have to reverse them?”

“On the test. They put cones out and make you feel bad about yourself.”

She smiled. It changed her face enough that he looked away.

“My father drove a truck,” she said. “He could back it into a space this big.”

She held her hands a foot apart.

“Did he tell you that, or did you see it?”

“He told me.”

“There you go.”

She laughed once, quietly, so as not to wake the boy.

“What’s his name?” Dale asked.

“Theo.”

“Good name.”

“He’s decided it’s short for dinosaur.”

“Not Theodore?”

“Not this week.”

Dale’s daughter had once insisted she was changing her name to Sparkle. He nearly told the woman, but the radio clicked and Carla started moving somebody’s Friday shift to Saturday. By the time she finished, the woman was looking at her phone again.

At nine-forty-six, she made a call.

“Hi. It’s me.”

Dale kept his eyes on the illuminated strip of road.

“No, I’m—”

A pause.

“Can you just call when you get this?”

She ended the call and put the phone beneath her thigh.

He released the brake.

***

By ten-thirty the bus had emptied twice and filled again with a different assortment of people: a cleaner carrying work shoes in a grocery bag, three students smelling of fries, a man with a rolled carpet under his arm. The carpet man wanted to stand beside Dale.

“Behind the line, please.”

“It won’t fit.”

“It’ll fit farther back.”

The woman lifted her duffel out of the aisle. The man went past her, brushing the carpet against the poles.

At a long red light Dale flexed his right hand. The knuckle below his thumb had begun aching in December. His doctor had suggested driving less, then laughed when Dale looked at him.

His phone buzzed inside his lunch bag.

He didn’t touch it until the terminal.

His daughter had sent a photograph of two coats on a shop rack.

Which one is less real-estate agent?

He enlarged the picture. One was beige. One was green.

Green, he wrote.

Knew you’d say that.

You asked.

She sent a heart.

He had told her the car was in for work in October. In November, when she asked whether he could pick up a desk she’d found online, he said the transmission was waiting on a part. She hadn’t asked since. He disliked that more than another question.

The repossession had happened on a Tuesday morning while he was asleep after a split shift. He still had the second key in his kitchen drawer.

“Is this the last stop?”

The woman was standing near the front with Theo against her shoulder. His open mouth pressed a damp mark into her coat.

“Not my last. I go back east from here.”

“Could we stay on?”

“You haven’t passed your sister’s?”

“She’s not home yet.”

He glanced at the clock. Ten-fifty-one.

“I’m going out of service at midnight. East-side garage.”

“That’s okay.”

He wanted to ask how it was okay. Instead he pointed to the seat.

“You might as well put him down.”

“He’ll wake.”

She sat with the boy still held against her.

Before Dale pulled out, she came forward again.

“Ana,” she said.

“Sorry?”

“My name. In case you need it for the fare.”

“No report for that.”

“Oh.”

“I’m Dale.”

She nodded and went back.

***

On the final eastbound trip, the roads were nearly bare. Wind pushed loose grains of salt across the intersections. The bus windows had clouded above the heater vents, turning the storefront lights into blurred rectangles.

Ana plugged a cable into her phone and searched along the wall beside her seat.

“No outlets on this one,” Dale said.

“Right.”

“How much battery?”

“Eight.”

“There’s a cord here. If it’s the right kind.”

At the next stop he took his power bank from his bag and passed it back. She studied the little row of blue lights before connecting her phone.

“You’ll need this.”

“Not till later.”

He had bought it after missing a connection with a dead phone in November. His own battery was at thirty-one percent.

At eleven-thirty-eight, the last other passenger got off.

Dale left the doors open a moment.

“Ana.”

She looked up.

“I need an actual stop now. We’re heading into the garage after the next turn.”

Her thumb moved across the phone.

“Can we do one more time around?”

“There isn’t another time around.”

“I could pay.”

“It’s not that.”

The heater ticked. He pressed his heel against it, too hard, and pain jumped up through his knee.

Ana looked out at the dark storefronts. A laundromat had chairs along its window, but its lights were off.

“Is there somewhere open?” she asked.

“The hospital.”

“Not there.”

It came quickly. She shifted Theo higher and rubbed his back, though he had made no sound.

Dale closed the doors before the cold reached them.

“Let me check something.”

He called Carla.

“Any late eastbound service from the garage?”

“Not unless you’re volunteering to drive it.”

“I’ve got a woman and a small child. Their ride’s fallen through.”

Ana looked down.

“Where are they going?” Carla asked.

“Working on that.”

A pause, then the scratch of paper near Carla’s microphone.

“I’ve got the after-hours housing number here.”

Dale glanced in the mirror.

Ana was watching him now. She shook her head, barely.

“Hang on,” he told Carla.

He turned in his seat.

“It doesn’t have to mean anything gets decided tonight. You could ask what they’ve got.”

Ana rubbed the edge of her phone case.

“Will they call somebody?”

“I don’t know. You can ask that first.”

For several seconds she said nothing. Then she nodded.

***

The garage gates were open when they arrived at eleven-fifty-four. Beyond the fence, buses stood beneath white yard lights, their wet sides streaked with salt. He stopped in the holding lane outside the wash entrance.

He could not take passengers through. He could not leave them aboard and sign out.

His connecting bus would pass the stop on the main road at twelve-twelve. The next one was at five.

Ana had been on the housing line for nine minutes. Mostly she listened. Once she spelled her surname. When they asked for an address, she gave the hospital’s.

Dale looked away from the mirror.

Carla called his unit.

“Wash crew needs that coach.”

“I know.”

“You got an arrangement?”

He held the microphone against his chest until Ana looked up.

“They’re checking a room,” she whispered. “They said don’t hang up.”

He brought the microphone back to his mouth.

“Still waiting.”

“I can give you till twelve-ten in the holding lane. After that, bring them into the drivers’ entrance. You’ll have to stay with them.”

The drivers’ entrance was six minutes from his stop if he hurried. Maybe four if he ran across the employee lot.

Theo stirred and began to cry without opening his eyes.

“Nearly there,” Ana whispered.

Dale took his hand off the gear selector.

“Okay, Carla.”

“And sign your sheet at twelve. I can’t authorize the extra.”

“Understood.”

He opened the taxi app on his phone. The fare home was forty-eight dollars and sixty cents, more than he spent on groceries most weeks. He had sixty-three in the account until Tuesday.

He watched the little booking button.

Behind him Ana said, “Yes. We can wait inside.”

He booked.

At twelve-oh-eight, he shut down the bus. The heater gave two final ticks.

***

The staff vestibule smelled of wet boots and burnt coffee. Dale put his lunch bag beneath the bench so Theo could lie along it with his head in Ana’s lap.

She handed back the power bank.

“Keep it connected till they get here.”

“They said a grey van. Twenty minutes.”

“Good.”

“Your wife’ll wonder where you are.”

“No wife.”

“Sorry.”

“It’s all right.”

He folded his time sheet against the wall and wrote midnight in the last box. Through the wired glass, the wash crew drove his bus away.

Ana’s phone rang. She answered at once.

“Yes?”

He lifted his bag, giving her room, but she touched his sleeve.

“It’s them,” she said. “They’re at the other gate.”

He called Carla for directions, then went outside to wave the van around. The cold caught the sweat beneath his uniform shirt. On the main road, a westbound bus passed beyond the fence.

When he returned, Ana had Theo upright. The boy’s eyes were open, unfocused.

“Another bus?” he asked.

“A van this time,” she said.

Dale held the door while she manoeuvred the duffel onto her shoulder. She stopped beside him.

“Thank you for the fare.”

Before he could answer, Theo reached out and pushed the heavy door’s silver button.

The mechanism hummed. Dale kept his hand against the glass until the boy was through.
