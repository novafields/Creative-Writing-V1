## The Last Stop

By nine o’clock, the thaw had turned the streets of Hamilton into a long, dirty reflection.

Dale Varga watched the traffic lights smear across the wet windshield as he brought the bus to a stop outside the General. Water ran along the curb in black streams, carrying cigarette ends and scraps of salt-stained paper. The temperature had dropped again. A skin of ice was forming over the puddles.

Three hours left.

He checked the mirror, the doors, the mirror again. The bus was half full. An old man in a blue work jacket sat behind the front wheel well, holding a paper bag between his boots. Two teenagers shared earbuds near the back. A woman in a long coat stood by the middle doors, looking at the hospital entrance.

Dale had driven this route for eleven years. He knew the pauses in the traffic, the stops where people ran for the bus with their coats half buttoned, the corners where the rear wheels needed room. He knew which intersections flooded after a thaw and which stretch of Barton always seemed to be under construction.

He also knew how long it took to get home from the garage without a car.

The thought came automatically now, whenever he approached the end of a shift. He'd learned the connections, the last departures, the stops where he could wait indoors. He'd memorized the schedules on his phone and stopped looking at them in public. There was no reason for anyone at work to know. Carla had never asked. His daughter hadn't either.

The repossession had happened in October, while he was at the dentist. By the time he got home, the car was gone and the neighbours had seen the truck. He'd told his daughter the transmission had finally died. She'd said she'd known it was coming. He hadn't corrected her.

The woman at the hospital stop boarded with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a small boy pressed against her hip. The boy's head lolled sleepily against her coat. She had a paper cup in one hand and a phone in the other.

She tapped a fare card against the reader. It gave a long red beep.

"Sorry," she said.

"Try again."

She did. Red.

"Card's not reading," Dale said.

She turned it over, wiped the chip against her sleeve and tried once more. The reader flashed green.

"Thank you."

He nodded and closed the doors.

The woman took a seat near the front, leaving the duffel on the floor between her shoes. The boy was wearing a grey knitted hat with a little pom-pom. One of his mittens was missing. She tucked his bare hand into the sleeve of her own coat.

Dale pulled away.

The old man in the blue jacket got off at James. The teenagers stayed on, their heads bent together. The woman checked her phone, turned it face down, then picked it up again.

At the next red light, Dale glanced over.

"Long night?"

She looked up, startled. "A bit."

"Hospital shift?"

"No. Just leaving."

He let the light change before he answered. "Right."

The boy stirred and lifted his head. His eyes were dark and unfocused.

"Are we there?"

"Not yet, Theo."

"Is this the one with the big stairs?"

"Not this one."

He settled against her again.

Dale watched them in the mirror. The woman's hair was pulled into a loose knot. There was a small white strip of tape near her wrist, the kind used to cover a puncture mark. Her coat was open, though it was cold enough to make his own fingers ache.

"Where are you headed?" he asked when the traffic slowed.

"My sister's."

"Which part of the city?"

"East end."

"Okay. This one runs out to Parkdale and then finishes at the garage."

She nodded.

"There's a connection at Parkdale, depending where you're going."

"That's fine."

He waited for more, but she had returned to her phone.

The bus rattled over a patch of broken pavement. Somewhere behind him, the heater began its familiar ticking. It had done that for three weeks, a little metallic sound that came from beneath the driver's side panel. Tick, tick, tick. The shop had replaced a valve, then a relay, and the sound had returned the following morning.

Dale tapped the side of the panel with his boot. It stopped.

"Is something wrong?" the woman asked.

"Just the heater."

"It sounds like a clock."

"That's what I keep telling them."

She smiled faintly. The boy opened one eye.

"What's wrong with the clock?"

"Nothing," she said. "It's just noisy."

He looked at Dale. "Our clock doesn't make noise."

"Then you've got a good one."

The boy considered this, then looked toward the windows.

"Do you like dinosaurs?" Dale asked.

A small nod.

"Which one's your favourite?"

"Triceratops."

"Good choice."

"Because it's got three horns."

"Can't argue with that."

The woman looked at him, and this time her smile reached her eyes.

"He knows more about them than I do," she said.

"He's probably got a better collection than me."

"You have dinosaurs?"

"Not anymore. My daughter had a plastic one when she was little. Used to leave it in the bathtub. Nearly gave me a heart attack one night."

"What was it?"

"Some kind of long-necked thing. Big enough to make me think there was an animal in the water."

"Brontosaurus," Theo said.

"Could've been."

"Brontosaurus isn't a real dinosaur anymore."

Dale raised his eyebrows. "What happened to it?"

"They changed its name."

"That's rough."

Theo gave a solemn nod.

For a few minutes, the bus moved through the east end without conversation. The boy watched the passing shops. The woman held her phone low in her lap, her thumb resting against the screen.

Dale passed a pharmacy with its metal shutters down. A convenience store was still open, its fluorescent lights making the wet pavement look green. At the next stop, a young man boarded carrying a pizza box. He sat across from the woman and spent the next five minutes trying to keep the box level while texting.

The heater started ticking again.

Dale turned the temperature control down a notch. The noise continued.

His radio crackled.

"Thirty-eight, you copy?"

"Go ahead, Carla."

"Relief for the King run is confirmed. Don't need you to take the extra loop. Just finish regular and bring it in."

"Copy."

"And make sure you log the defect on that heater again. Maintenance says they haven't seen it."

"Tell them to sit in it for a shift."

Carla gave a tired laugh. "I'll pass that along."

The radio went quiet.

He'd known Carla for eight years. She had worked dispatch for longer than he'd been driving. She could tell from his voice whether he'd had coffee, whether a passenger had been difficult, whether he'd been stuck behind a delivery truck. She didn't ask personal questions, which was one reason he liked her.

The woman checked her phone again. The screen lit her face blue.

A call came in. She looked at the name, then rejected it.

A moment later the phone vibrated. She read the message and pressed the side button.

Theo shifted in his seat.

"Can I have my drink?"

"Not right now."

"I'm thirsty."

"I know. We'll get you one."

"When?"

"Soon."

Dale watched the road.

At Parkdale, the young man with the pizza box got off. The woman stood, gathering the duffel. She looked at the stop sign, then at the dark stretch of road beyond it.

"This is the connection?" she asked.

"No. You want to stay on."

She sat again.

"How much farther?"

"About twenty minutes."

She lowered herself into the seat with a carefulness that made Dale think her legs hurt. The boy was asleep now, his cheek against her coat. She pulled the missing mitten from a side pocket of the duffel and slipped it onto his hand.

The phone vibrated again.

She didn't look at it.

Dale turned onto the next stretch of Barton. The traffic had thinned. A truck passed in the opposite direction, throwing a sheet of water against the curb. The bus rocked through the spray.

He thought of the meal waiting in his refrigerator: half a container of soup, bread that was probably stale, a carton of eggs. He'd told his daughter he would call her after work. She wanted to know whether he could come by on Sunday. He'd said he'd see how he felt. She'd taken that to mean he might be driving.

He'd considered telling her the truth more than once. The first time had been in November, when she'd asked if he could pick her up from a friend's house. The second had been Christmas, when she'd given him a small emergency car kit. He'd put it in a drawer.

The bus hit a pothole. The woman caught the duffel before it slid.

"Sorry," Dale said.

"It's okay."

"Road's a mess tonight."

"I know."

A few stops later, Theo woke. He stared at the window, then at the dark reflection of his own face.

"Is Daddy coming?"

The woman went still for a moment.

"No, sweetheart."

"When?"

"Not tonight."

"He said he'd come."

"I know."

Theo looked down at his mitten.

Dale kept his eyes on the road. He could hear the heater ticking, louder now that the other passengers had got off. He wondered whether to say something, then decided against it.

The phone lit again. A preview appeared across the top of the screen.

I can see your location.

The woman turned it face down.

Dale checked the mirror. She was looking at him, not the road.

"How much longer?" she asked.

"Ten minutes or so."

She glanced toward the windshield. "Does this go past the station?"

"Which station?"

"The bus station."

"No. We're heading to the garage now."

"Oh."

"If you need to get downtown, I can tell you where to transfer."

"No. That's okay."

He wondered why she'd asked. There was nowhere to go from the garage except into the yard or back onto the road. He'd explained the route when she'd boarded.

At the next stop, a man in a brown parka climbed aboard, shook the wet off his hood and dropped into the first empty seat. He was carrying a thermos and a folded newspaper.

"Cold one," he said.

"Getting colder," Dale answered.

"After all that rain, too. My driveway's a skating rink."

"Watch your step."

The man laughed and opened his newspaper. The front page was creased down the middle. He glanced at the woman's duffel, then at the sleeping boy, and returned to the paper.

The bus continued east.

Dale's final time point came and went. He called out the stop, waited, opened the doors. No one got off. When he pulled away, the clock above the dashboard read 11:47.

He had twenty minutes before the garage.

Carla came back on the radio.

"Thirty-eight, your pull-in is showing twelve-oh-eight."

"That's right."

"Security needs the bay clear by twelve-fifteen. They're bringing a contractor through."

"Understood."

"Don't forget your paperwork."

"I won't."

The woman had gone back to her phone. Her thumb moved across the screen, stopped, moved again. She seemed to be reading something for a long time.

Dale glanced at the route display. The bus was nearly empty. The man with the thermos had got off. Only the woman and Theo remained.

At the next red light, the boy woke again.

"Are we at Auntie's?"

"Almost."

"Is she making pancakes?"

"Maybe."

"With chocolate chips?"

"We'll see."

He smiled, then rested his head against her arm.

Dale caught the woman's eye in the mirror. She looked away.

The heater clicked in a steady rhythm. He left it alone.

By the time the bus reached the industrial stretch near the garage, the temperature had fallen below freezing. The roads shone under the streetlights. Dale took the turn toward Wentworth, slowed for the railway crossing and watched a freight train crawl through the dark. Its wheels hammered one after another, a long uneven rhythm that filled the bus.

Theo slept through it.

The garage gates were open. A security light swept across the windshield. Beyond the fence, buses stood in rows, their windows dark. A mechanic in reflective coveralls walked between two parked vehicles with a clipboard tucked under one arm.

Dale brought the bus to a stop outside the entrance.

The clock read 12:09.

He set the brake, put the transmission in neutral and switched off the route display. The bus gave a little shudder as the engine dropped to idle.

"End of the line," he said.

The woman looked up.

"Sorry?"

"We're at the garage."

She stared through the windshield. The duffel was still at her feet. Theo was asleep against her shoulder.

"Oh."

Dale opened the front doors.

She didn't move.

He waited. The cold entered around the door seals, carrying the smell of wet concrete and diesel. He could see the security guard in the booth beyond the gate, looking down at a monitor.

"Ma'am?"

She looked at him.

"You need to get off here."

"Can I stay a minute?"

"I have to bring the bus in."

"Just a minute."

He looked at the clock. Twelve-ten. His bus connection left from Barton at twelve-eighteen. He had enough time to walk to the stop if he got out now. The next bus wasn't until after midnight-thirty, and the stop had no shelter. He'd be standing in the wind, waiting for headlights.

"Where's your sister?" he asked.

"She lives near here."

"Which street?"

The woman lowered her eyes.

"Just near here."

"Do you know the address?"

She pressed her lips together. Her phone vibrated in her hand. She didn't look at it.

Dale could hear the traffic from the road behind them. A car passed through the intersection and disappeared. The bus idled, heater ticking.

"Does she know you're coming?" he asked.

The woman smoothed Theo's hair.

"She's probably asleep."

It was an answer, but not to the question.

"Can you call her?"

"I don't want to wake her."

Dale looked at the duffel. It was small, too small for a week away, with one strap twisted around the handle. There was a child's water bottle in the side pocket. A hospital envelope stuck out of the top, its corner bent and damp.

He looked back at the woman. Her hands had started to shake. She put her phone in her coat pocket and wrapped both arms around Theo.

He had seen people sleep on buses before. People who didn't want to go home. People who'd missed the last connection and sat with their faces against the glass until the driver told them to leave. Most accepted it. Some got angry. A few asked to stay until the first morning service.

The garage wasn't a place to stay. The drivers weren't supposed to bring passengers inside. Security would see them, and there would be questions. He could open the doors, point toward the pavement and get on with his shift.

He could also leave them standing beside the fence, with the boy half asleep and the woman holding a duffel bag she hadn't put down all night.

His radio crackled.

"Thirty-eight, you're sitting at the gate. Everything okay?"

Dale took the microphone.

"I've got two passengers still aboard."

A pause.

"Two passengers?"

"Mother and child. I'm trying to sort it out."

"You're clear to bring it in. Security's waiting."

"I need a few minutes."

"Dale, we need that bay."

He looked at the woman. She was watching him now, but she didn't ask him to do anything.

"Can you put me through to the supervisor?" he said.

Carla exhaled over the radio. "I'll call him. Don't open the doors again until you've got it sorted."

Dale put the microphone down.

The woman bent to pick up the duffel.

"I can get off," she said.

"Where are you going?"

She looked toward the dark road.

"Somewhere."

He didn't answer.

He reached across to the console and switched the passenger heater up. The ticking grew louder. He tried the control again, then gave up. He took his phone from the pocket of his jacket and opened the transit employee page. There was a number for after-hours incident support. He'd used it twice in eleven years, once after a passenger had fallen and once when a man had punched a window.

He called.

The first person who answered asked for his operator number, bus number and location. Dale gave them. He explained that two passengers had remained on board at the garage and that the woman seemed unsure where she was going. He didn't mention the message on her phone. He didn't know whether it mattered. He said the child was asleep and the woman hadn't asked for emergency services.

The person on the line asked him to stay with them while they arranged assistance.

Dale looked at the clock.

Twelve-fifteen.

His connection had gone.

He put the phone down and rubbed his thumb along the edge of the steering wheel. There was a crack in the vinyl where the seam had split. He'd been meaning to report it.

The woman was still watching him.

"You don't have to do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Wait."

"Looks like I do."

She looked down at Theo. "He's tired."

"Yeah."

"He doesn't usually fall asleep like this."

Dale nodded. He didn't ask what she meant.

The phone rang again. He answered. The woman on the line asked whether Ana had somewhere safe to wait. Dale hadn't known her name until then. He repeated the question to her.

She looked toward the windshield.

"There's a place," she said.

"Where?"

"Downtown."

"Do you know the address?"

She shook her head.

Dale gave the phone a small distance from his ear. The woman on the line asked if he could bring them to the security office. He said he could. She told him not to leave the vehicle unattended and said someone would meet him at the gate.

He hung up.

The security guard had come out of the booth. He was standing near the fence with his hands in his pockets, looking at the bus.

Dale switched off the engine.

The silence made the heater's ticking stop.

"All right," he said to Ana. "We'll go inside for a minute. There's someone coming."

She didn't move immediately. Then she bent and lifted the duffel.

Dale opened the front doors and stepped down onto the wet pavement. The cold caught him across the face. He walked around to the curb and held out his hand toward Theo.

Ana hesitated.

"He can stay with you," Dale said.

She shifted the boy carefully. Theo's head rolled onto Dale's shoulder, his breath warm against the collar of Dale's uniform. He weighed less than Dale expected. His mitten had slipped halfway off.

The security guard approached.

"Everything okay?"

"Just need somewhere warm while we get a call sorted."

The guard glanced at Ana, then at the child.

"Office is open."

Dale followed him toward the pedestrian gate. Behind them, the bus stood with its front doors open and the headlights shining on the wet concrete. The duffel bumped against Ana's leg as she walked.

At the gate, Dale's phone vibrated.

A message from his daughter.

Sunday still good? I'll make coffee.

He read it, then put the phone away without answering. Theo stirred against his shoulder.

"Don't put me down," the boy mumbled.

"Not yet," Dale said.

The guard held the gate open. Beyond it, the garage smelled of hot rubber and old diesel. A radio played somewhere inside, low enough that Dale couldn't make out the song.

He stepped through with the boy in his arms, and Ana followed, carrying the bag.
