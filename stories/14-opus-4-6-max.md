## Deadhead

The heater in the 2200 had been ticking since Dundurn, a metallic knock every eight or nine seconds that Dale couldn't fix and couldn't stop hearing. He'd written it up twice. The first time the note came back with *unable to replicate* in someone else's handwriting. The second time it just came back.

He turned onto King Street and the ticking paused, then resumed at a slightly different interval, as if the bus were adjusting to the grade. Three passengers sat spread out behind him. A man in a parka near the rear doors who'd been riding since Westdale. A woman with grocery bags in the priority seats. A teenager with earbuds whose transfer had expired twenty minutes ago, and Dale had decided not to care.

It was quarter past nine. He had the route until twelve-fifteen, then the deadhead back to the garage on Kenilworth. Three hours. The thaw had broken two days ago and everything that had melted was now a skin of black ice under a half-inch of dry snow. He took the turns wide. The 2200 was rear-heavy when it was empty, and tonight it was close.

Carla came on the radio. "Dale, Fournier called in. I need you to hold the 5 at Eastgate until ten-oh-five for a connection."

"Copy."

"And shut down the reader at the rear door, it's been flagging ghost taps all week."

He reached over without looking and toggled the switch. The woman with the grocery bags pulled the cord and he eased to the stop at Wellington. She gathered her things, looked at the step down, looked back at Dale.

"It's icy," he said.

"I can see that."

She went down sideways, holding the rail. He waited until she was on the sidewalk before he closed the doors.

Ana Ruiz got on at the hospital stop at twenty to ten. She was carrying a blue duffel bag and a boy who was mostly asleep, his head against her neck. She had her Presto card out but the front reader took three taps before it caught.

"Sorry," she said. The boy stirred.

"Machine's slow when it's cold," Dale said, which wasn't true, but it filled the moment.

She sat in the second row, on the right side, and arranged the boy so he was lying across the seat with his head in her lap. She unzipped his jacket but didn't take it off. The duffel bag she kept between her feet.

Dale pulled away from the curb. In the mirror he saw her take out her phone, look at it, put it in her coat pocket. Thirty seconds later she took it out again.

The man in the parka got off at Ottawa Street. The teenager rode to Eastgate and left without looking up. Dale held the bus at the terminal as Carla had asked, engine running, the heater ticking. The mall was dark. A security guard walked past the glass doors and didn't look out.

Ana's boy — Dale could see now that he was maybe four, sandy hair, one sneaker untied — was fully asleep, mouth open. Ana was looking at her phone again. She caught Dale's eye in the mirror and put it away.

"Are you going far?" Dale asked. He said it because the route was thinning and she had a duffel bag and a sleeping child and had gotten on at a hospital.

"My sister's. She's on the east end."

"Okay. I go all the way to the garage on Kenilworth. You'll want to get off before that, unless you're transferring."

"Right. I'll figure it out."

She didn't ask about connections. She didn't name a street.

At ten-oh-five Dale closed the doors and pulled out. Nobody had been waiting for the connection Carla had mentioned. The bus was empty except for Ana and Theo.

Between Eastgate and Queenston he picked up a couple coming from the arena, still wearing their coats unzipped, flushed from the heat inside. They sat across from Ana and the man said something to her about the boy.

"He's out cold," the man said. "Ours are the same age. Put them in a moving vehicle and they're gone."

"He can sleep through anything," Ana said. She was smiling. It was the first time Dale had seen her face without the phone in front of it.

"How old?" the woman asked.

"Four in March."

"The best age," the woman said, and her husband or boyfriend said, "You say that about every age," and she said, "Because it's true about every age," and they laughed quietly. Ana laughed too, low, careful not to shake the boy.

The couple got off at Parkdale. The woman waved at the sleeping Theo through the window.

Then it was the three of them. Dale, Ana, and the boy.

Carla came on at eleven. "Dale, you good? You're running four minutes hot."

"Light traffic."

"Must be nice. I've got Fenton stuck on the mountain with a mechanical. Take your time on the eastbound, let the schedule catch you."

"Copy."

He slowed through the Barton stretch, took the lights easy. The street was quiet. A few men outside a bar on Gage, smoking in shirt sleeves as though the thaw were still on. A cat bolted across four lanes and made it.

In the mirror Ana was sitting very still, one hand on Theo's back. Her phone was face-down on the seat beside her. It buzzed once, twice. She didn't pick it up.

Dale watched the road.

At eleven-thirty he started the last eastbound run. Two more people got on and off. A nurse in scrubs riding three stops. A man who asked if this bus went to Stoney Creek and Dale said no and the man got off and stood on the sidewalk looking at his phone like it had lied to him.

By quarter to twelve the route was done. The last scheduled stop was Nash Road, and after that he'd deadhead south on Kenilworth to the garage. Ana hadn't moved. Theo hadn't woken up.

Dale pulled to Nash and opened the doors. Cold air came in. He looked in the mirror.

"This is the last stop on the route," he said. "After this I head to the garage."

Ana looked at him. She had her hand on the duffel bag strap.

"I know," she said. "I'm sorry. I thought — I thought she'd have texted by now."

Dale kept the doors open. The heater ticked. The cold was coming in fast and Theo shifted in his sleep, curling tighter.

"Your sister," Dale said.

"Yeah."

"You have her address?"

A pause. Not long, but enough. "She moved recently. I haven't been to the new place."

Dale closed the doors. He sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel. Outside, the snow was blowing sideways under the streetlight, fine and dry, the kind that wouldn't stick.

The radio crackled. "Dale, you at Nash?"

"Just left."

"Okay, bring her in. I'm locking the board at twelve-thirty."

"Copy."

He pulled out and turned south on Kenilworth. The garage was six minutes away. He had his walkthrough to do — lights, fare equipment, damage check, the write-up on the heater that he'd submit for the third time. After that he'd have fourteen minutes to reach the stop on Burlington Street where the 4 still ran at twelve-fifty-one, the last bus that would get him to his apartment on the west side. If he missed it he'd be walking, or calling a cab with money he'd already counted and allocated down to the dollar, because that was how February worked when you were still making payments on a car you no longer had.

He glanced in the mirror. Ana was putting Theo's hat on without waking him, working it over his ears with the particular care of someone who'd done this in worse situations than a cooling bus.

Dale pulled into the garage lot and parked in the third bay. He cut the engine. The ticking stopped.

He turned in his seat.

"Where does your sister live. Roughly."

"Off Cannon somewhere. East of Sherman, she said."

He knew the area. Fifteen blocks north. Not far in a car. Very far at midnight with a duffel bag and a sleeping four-year-old and nothing waiting at the other end of the phone.

"Sit tight," he said.

He did the walkthrough fast. Lights, fare readers, the rear door seal that stuck in cold weather. He wrote up the heater: *metallic tick from unit, 8–10 second intervals, persisted full shift, third report*. He signed the clipboard and hung it on the hook inside the garage office. Carla was at the desk with a Thermos and a sudoku book.

"Quiet night," she said.

"Mostly."

"You good to deadhead tomorrow's first if Fenton's still down?"

"I'm on at six anyway."

"Good man."

He walked back to the bus. Ana was standing in the aisle with Theo on her hip, the duffel over her shoulder. The boy was awake now, blinking under the fluorescent bay lights.

"Is there a bus from here?" she asked.

"Not until five."

She absorbed that. She hitched Theo higher on her hip.

Dale took out his phone and called Beck Taxi. He gave them the garage address and said one passenger, Cannon and Sherman area. They said twelve to fifteen minutes. He said fine.

"I'll wait with you," he said. "They'll come to the lot entrance."

"You don't have to do that."

"I know."

They stood outside the bay doors. The wind had picked up and the snow was finding the gaps between his collar and his neck. Theo put his face against Ana's shoulder and she turned so her back was to the weather, shielding him without deciding to.

"What's his name?" Dale asked. He'd heard her say it once, quietly, when the boy had stirred on the Barton stretch. But it was something to say while they stood in the cold.

"Theo."

"Good name."

"It was his grandfather's."

Dale put his hands in his pockets. His phone said twelve-forty-one. The 4 left Burlington Street in ten minutes. He could make it if he left now. He'd have to jog. He'd done it before, in October, the week after the car was towed, when the whole machinery of his life had shifted and he'd started learning the transit schedules not as a driver but as a rider, memorizing connections he'd never needed when he could just drive himself home.

Headlights turned into the lot. The cab stopped and the driver leaned across to look at them through the passenger window. Dale walked over and handed him a twenty and a ten.

"Cannon and Sherman area," he said. "She'll direct you from there."

The driver took the bills and nodded.

Ana was at the cab door. She looked at Dale. She didn't say thank you, which he was grateful for, because he didn't want to be thanked, he wanted to be standing somewhere warm.

"Text your sister," he said.

She got in. Theo's face appeared in the rear window for a moment, pale and round, and then the cab turned out of the lot and the taillights shrank down Kenilworth and were gone.

Dale walked to Burlington Street. His hands were already numb. He checked the time — twelve-forty-nine — and he could see the stop from here, the shelter with the cracked ad panel, and there was no bus yet, which meant it was either late or he was early, and either way he was going to stand here in the cold and find out.
