The Last Pull-In

By nine o’clock the thaw had hardened into ridges of black ice along the curb lanes. Water that had run freely at noon now glazed the gutters, and every bus shelter shone under the streetlights as if sealed in glass.

Dale Varga kept both hands loose on the wheel. Eleven winters had taught him that gripping harder did nothing except make his shoulders ache. The bus knew its own weight. Let it settle before braking, ease it away from the curb, never trust the shine on the pavement.

The duty sheet clipped beside him said THU 19 FEB. Three hours left, two east-west runs, then a short final to the garage. He could already feel the twelve-thirty bus he took home waiting at the edge of his mind. The connection at King was only seven minutes, but the late driver usually held for uniforms.

Nobody at work knew he depended on it. His car had disappeared from outside his building in October, lifted by the finance company before dawn. He had told Carla the transmission had gone. He had told his daughter, Melissa, that he was thinking about something smaller.

At the General, six people got off and two got on. The first was an orderly in blue scrubs. Behind him came a woman with a duffel over one shoulder and a little boy bundled in a red coat.

The woman tapped her fare card. The reader gave a flat, accusing buzz.

She tried again.

“Funds unavailable,” Dale said, reading the display.

“I loaded it.”

“Takes time sometimes.”

She shifted the duffel higher. The boy leaned against her leg, his mitten pressed to one eye.

“I can pay cash.” She searched one coat pocket and then the other.

“You’ll hold us all night doing that. Go sit down.”

Her gaze came up sharply, as though kindness might have terms. She was young—late twenties, maybe—with damp black hair tucked behind her ears. No hat. One side of her jaw looked darker than the other, though it might have been the light from the farebox.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Where are you headed?”

“My sister’s.”

Dale waited for the useful part.

“East,” she added.

“This goes east.”

She nodded and guided the boy to the first pair of forward-facing seats.

The orderly found a place at the back. Dale checked his mirror, pulled away and felt the rear wheels slide half an inch before catching.

At Wellington, the boy climbed onto his knees to look over the seat.

“Does this bus bend?”

“Not this one,” Dale said.

“I saw one that bends.”

“Articulated bus.”

The boy considered the word. “Like an alligator?”

“More like an accordion.”

“My grandma has an accordion.”

The woman gave a tired laugh. “He means she has a picture of one.”

“Still counts,” Dale said.

“What makes the doors breathe?” the boy asked.

“Air.”

“They sound mad.”

“They are mad. Opening and closing all day.”

The boy smiled. “Do they bite?”

“Only mittens.”

He pulled his hands against his chest. His mother—Ana, Dale learned when she told him to sit properly—apologized, but Dale said it was fine. Children asked better questions than adults. Adults asked whether he was late when they could see the same traffic through the windshield.

On Barton, the bus filled with shift workers, grocery bags and two teenagers sharing headphones. Ana checked her phone at every second stop. She never typed much. Once she lifted it to her ear, listened without speaking, then put it facedown on her knee.

Carla’s voice cracked over the radio.

“Operators eastbound, avoid the curb lane between Sherman and Gage. Water main break, sanding crew requested.”

Dale pressed transmit. “Four-seventeen copied.”

“Dale, you’ve got McNally relieving the twenty-two at eleven-forty. If you see him at the loop, tell him he’s not going to the garage.”

“Thought Singh had that.”

“Singh called in.”

“Again?”

“He has the flu.”

“He had the flu two weeks ago.”

“Then he’s very unlucky.”

Carla clicked off before he could answer. Tired and practical, as always. The city could freeze solid and she would still be moving little names between little boxes.

At Ottawa Street, Ana stood suddenly.

“Is this—” She looked out at shuttered shops, dirty snowbanks and the dark mouth of a side street. “Does this go near the mall?”

“Eventually.”

She sat again.

Dale watched her in the mirror. The phone lit in her hand. He couldn’t read the message, but he saw the same number call twice in quick succession. She silenced it both times. Then she opened the settings and moved through several screens with her thumb.

Theo had gone quiet. His head rested against her arm.

At the east loop, everybody got off except Ana and the boy.

Dale set the brake and turned halfway in his seat. “End of the line.”

“I thought it went back.”

“It does. Westbound.”

“That’s okay.”

“You said east.”

“My sister isn’t answering. I’ll get off on the way back.”

There were places open near the loop—a coffee shop, a twenty-four-hour pharmacy—but the wind drove wrappers across the pavement, and Theo’s eyes were closed now. Ana had one arm around him and the other through the duffel strap.

Dale looked at the clock. He was two minutes down.

“You need to tap again,” he said.

She reached for the card.

“Forget it. Sit tight.”

On the westbound run, Theo slept against her. Ana kept the phone dark. Dale picked up three cleaners outside a long-term-care home, a man carrying a boxed microwave, and an old woman who talked loudly to herself about municipal taxes. At James, McNally was smoking under the shelter.

Dale opened the door. “Carla says you’re covering the twenty-two.”

McNally looked offended. “I’m on spare.”

“Not anymore.”

“Singh?”

“Struck down in his prime.”

McNally ground out his cigarette and climbed aboard, muttering about seniority. He noticed Ana and the sleeping child but said nothing.

Near downtown, freezing spray from a passing truck struck the windshield. The wipers dragged it into a white fan. Dale slowed, worked the washer twice and listened to the heater ticking under the dash. It had been ticking since dinner break: fast when the fan ran high, slower when he turned it down. He made a mental note for the defect card.

At the west loop, Ana did not move.

Dale waited until the other passengers had left.

“Where’s your sister live?”

“East end.”

“We just came from there.”

“I know.”

“What intersection?”

She looked at Theo instead of him. “Near Kenilworth.”

“We passed Kenilworth.”

“She hasn’t answered.”

Dale rubbed the bridge of his nose. “Ana, I’ve got one more trip and then this bus goes to the garage.”

“I know. I’m sorry.”

“If you tell me where you need to go, I can tell you how to get there.”

“She said she’d pick us up.”

“Where?”

“On the route.”

That wasn’t an answer. Her phone vibrated inside her coat. She pressed her arm against it until it stopped.

Dale thought of calling Carla. Carla would tell him the procedure: rider leaves at the terminal; if rider refuses, request a supervisor; if safety is in question, call police. Procedures liked clean categories. Ana was not refusing. She looked ready to obey anything he said, which felt worse.

“Theo need a washroom?” he asked.

Ana shook him gently. The boy woke enough to nod.

Dale pointed toward the lit coffee shop across the loop. “Five minutes. I’ll hold.”

While they were gone, he called in four minutes late and blamed the roads. Carla told him to take his recovery time and reminded him the final pull-in had changed because the service entrance was iced over.

“Use Gate Three,” she said. “Fuel lane first, then park in inspection.”

“Copied.”

“You sound cheerful.”

“Living the dream.”

“Do it quieter.”

Ana came back carrying Theo, his legs wrapped around her waist. Dale lowered the bus and opened the doors. She climbed aboard without meeting his eyes.

The final run thinned after ten-thirty. They passed storefronts with grates drawn down, bars leaking yellow light, apartment towers whose balconies were lined with frozen runoff. At a stop by Gage Park, Ana rose, looked out and sat again.

Her phone rang once more.

This time Theo stirred. “Is Dad coming?”

Ana put a hand over his hair. “Go back to sleep.”

She glanced toward Dale’s mirror. He looked away before she caught him looking.

At eleven-fifty, the last regular passenger got off with the boxed microwave. The bus became a moving room for the three of them. Heater ticking. Engine humming. Theo’s cheek flattened against Ana’s coat.

Dale called the garage and gave his pull-in number.

Carla answered. “Gate Three. Don’t forget the defect card if that heater’s still making noise.”

“It is.”

“Then write it up. Maintenance can’t fix what you don’t write up.”

He nearly told her about Ana. Instead he said, “What time’s the employee bus?”

“Twelve thirty-two sharp tonight. Why?”

“No reason.”

“You need it?”

“My car’s around.”

The lie came easily because it was an old one.

The route ended at twelve-eighteen under the sodium lights outside the east-side garage. Snowbanks hemmed the fence. Beyond it, buses stood in rows with their interiors glowing, empty and exposed.

Dale opened the doors.

Ana stayed seated.

“This is the garage,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“You can’t stay aboard.”

“I know.”

But she did not move. Theo hung heavily across her lap. Her phone was off now; he could tell by the way she kept pressing the side button and getting no light.

Dale pulled on his coat. “Is there a sister?”

Ana looked toward the open door. Cold air moved through the bus.

“Yes.”

“Does she know you’re coming?”

Her mouth tightened.

That was close enough to an answer.

“I can call somebody,” he said. “A shelter. Family services.”

“No police.”

“I didn’t say police.”

“No police.” She gripped the duffel strap. “Please.”

Carla called over the radio. “Four-seventeen, status?”

Dale picked up the handset. “At Gate Three.”

“Fuel and inspection. You’re blocking the pull-in.”

“Passenger issue.”

A pause. “Need a supervisor?”

He looked at Ana. She shook her head hard.

“Negative. Give me five.”

“Five, Dale. Fleet needs the lane.”

He closed the front door to keep the heat in and took out his personal phone. The shelter line transferred him twice. While he waited, he filled out the defect card against the steering wheel: UNIT 417. HEATER TICKING, DASH AREA, ALL FAN SPEEDS. The pen tore through the paper where he pressed too hard.

The woman on the line asked questions he relayed to Ana. Adult and child, yes. Immediate medical attention, no. Safe to send a cab, Ana hesitated, then said yes. Name optional. Pickup could be the public road outside the garage, but the wait would be twenty to thirty minutes.

The employee bus left in fourteen.

Dale looked through the windshield at the garage doors. He could put Ana and Theo in the heated security vestibule, except passengers were not allowed past the fence. He could send them to the coffee shop by the loop, a twelve-minute walk over frozen sidewalks. He could call a regular cab, but he did not know where to send it.

Carla came back. “Four-seventeen, I need you moving.”

“I’ve got transport coming for them.”

“How long?”

“Twenty minutes.”

“You can’t hold the bus twenty minutes.”

Dale watched Ana tuck Theo’s red mitten under his chin.

“I’ll fuel now,” he said. “Then park outside inspection until their ride comes.”

“You’ll miss sign-off.”

“I know.”

“And the employee bus.”

“My car’s here.”

The words sat between him and the radio.

Carla exhaled. “Fuel, then north fence. Don’t leave them unattended. Bring me the defect card before you clock out.”

Dale moved the bus. At the fuel lane he shut down, pulled on his gloves and fed the heavy nozzle into the tank while Ana and Theo waited behind the glass. The diesel fumes caught in his throat. Across the yard, the employee bus idled with its sign blank.

He finished fueling and parked by the north fence. At twelve-thirty-two, the employee bus rolled past. Dale kept his face turned toward Ana’s expected cab. The driver lifted two fingers in greeting. Dale raised his hand.

The cab arrived at twelve-forty-six, unmarked except for a small blue placard on the dash. The driver gave Ana’s first name. That was enough for her.

Dale carried the duffel down the steps while Ana carried Theo. On the pavement she took the bag from him.

“Thank you,” she said.

Theo opened one eye. “Do the doors bite?”

“Not tonight,” Dale said.

Ana held his gaze for a second, then climbed into the cab. It pulled away without showing a destination.

Dale handed Carla the defect card at twelve-fifty-three. She glanced at the clock and then at him.

“Thought your car was here.”

“Wouldn’t start.”

She looked too tired to challenge him. “Cold’s hard on batteries.”

Outside the garage, Dale ordered a taxi on his phone. The app showed fifty-one dollars and sixty cents to his building, not including tip. His account balance was sixty-eight fourteen.

When the car arrived, he got into the back.

“Where to?” the driver asked.

Dale gave the intersection one block from his apartment.

The taxi nosed onto the frozen road. On the meter, $4.75 appeared, held for a moment, then climbed.
