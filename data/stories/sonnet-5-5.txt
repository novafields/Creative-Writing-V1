Deadhead

By nine o'clock the thaw had quit. All day the city had run with water, and now the slush on Barton Street was setting into ridges. The bus rode them like a washboard, and Dale Varga felt it in his wrists. The outside bank sign said minus eleven. The sign had said plus four at noon, when he left his apartment with two transfers folded in his shirt pocket.

He had three hours left. The Barton run went east from the terminal, past the hospital, past the shuttered tire places and the Portuguese bakery with its lights still on, and out to the loop at Eastgate. From there he deadheaded to the garage with the bus empty and the destination sign gone dark. He had done it so many times that his hands took the turns without him.

His phone buzzed in the cup holder. His daughter: *how's the car doing in this cold? mine wouldn't start this morning.* He left it face down. At the next red light he turned it face up, looked at it, and turned it back.

"Eleven forty-seven, Carla," the radio said.

He thumbed the mic. "Eleven forty-seven."

"Water main at Wentworth is still going, so starting tomorrow the eastbound detours at Sherman. I'm putting it on your sheet. Also, Ritchie's off again, so I need somebody on the early pullout. Fifty-two past five. You interested?"

"Fifty-two," Dale said. He did the math on the first bus in. There wasn't any. "Yeah. Fine."

"You're a saint. I'll tell Ritchie you said so."

"Don't tell Ritchie anything."

"Never do." She sounded as though she was eating something. "Heater okay on that one?"

"Ticking in the back. Still heats."

"Write it up."

At 10:41 he knelt the bus at the Hamilton General stop, and the hydraulics sighed. A woman came up the steps with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a boy on her hip. She was young, maybe thirty, in a parka that was too thin for the night. The boy wore a coat that was too big and boots with the laces trailing. She tapped her card on the reader. It gave two low beeps and the screen said *Insufficient*.

"Oh," she said. "It had money on it."

"Go ahead. Short night."

"I can find cash."

"Go ahead," he said again, and she did, and said thank you in a way that sounded practiced. She took the sideways seat behind him, the one that faced the aisle, and set the duffel between her feet. In the big mirror he could see the top of her head and the boy's.

The boy was awake. He looked at the ceiling, at the yellow poles, at the back of Dale's neck. "Why did it go down?"

"Kneeling," Dale said. "So people can get on easy."

"Does it get tired?"

"Only at night."

The boy considered this. "I'm four," he said.

"That's a good age for it."

"His name's Theo," the woman said. "I'm Ana." She said it to the mirror.

"Dale."

They pulled out. A man in a Stelco jacket got on at Sherman and rode three stops with his eyes shut. A pair of teenagers got on at Ottawa, shared a vape they thought he couldn't smell, and got off at Kenilworth. Between stops Ana looked at her phone. She looked at it the way you check a pot you don't want to boil. The screen lit her chin and went dark and lit again.

"You going far?" Dale asked at a red light.

"My sister's. Out east."

"Which stop?"

"I'll say when." She put the phone in her pocket, then took it out. "Is it always this cold?"

"It was fourteen above this afternoon. Every spring I think I'm done with it, and then it comes back and puts a hand on my chest."

She laughed, a short one. Theo had slid down against her side, and she moved his hood so it wasn't in his eyes. His sleeve rode up. Dale saw a strip of white paper around the small wrist, the kind they print at the desk. Ana saw him see it in the mirror, pulled the sleeve down over it, and looked out the window at a bank of dirty snow.

"He likes garbage trucks," she said after a while. "Like, loves them. Tuesday's the best day of his life. He stands at the window with a juice box."

"My daughter had a colander," Dale said. "Four years old. Wore it on her head everywhere. Church, the dentist. We had to let her wear it through the metal detector at the courthouse once. I was paying a ticket."

"What happened?"

"It beeped. The guy said, 'Ma'am, the hat.' She said, 'It's a helmet.' He let her through."

"How old is she now?"

"Twenty. She's in Guelph. Does something with soil."

"Soil."

"Tests it. Tells farmers what's wrong with it."

"That's a real job?"

"Apparently."

Ana smiled at her knees. The phone lit up in her hand and she turned it over fast, but not before he saw a line of white text across the top that began *HHS* and then a number. It buzzed against her palm six times. She didn't answer. When it stopped she held it a while longer, as if it might start again, and then she slid it into the duffel and zipped the bag shut.

Near Parkdale the Stelco man had gone, and the teenagers before him. The route emptied out the way it always did, one stop at a time, until the mirror showed only Ana and the boy and the rows of blue seats. Theo's head had gone heavy on her arm.

"What's the worst you've driven in?" she asked.

"Ice. Couple winters back, the one that took the Sherman access down. Buses couldn't make the hill. I put chains on at midnight in my good coat." He shook his head. "Never got the smell out."

"Of what?"

"Wet wool and diesel. Like a dog that got into something."

"Theo threw up in my coat last month and I never got it out either."

"That's a different smell."

"It's a worse smell," she agreed.

At the Eastgate loop he swung the bus around under the sodium lights and set the brake. The doors opened and the cold came in like something with a key. "End of the line," he said. "This one's going back to the garage. I can let you off here, there's a shelter."

She didn't move. She looked at the shelter, which was a plexiglass box with a cracked panel, a bench, and a schedule no one had updated. Beyond it the lot was bare and wet. "Could I ride back with you? Just to where there's a... She's coming to meet me. She said to get close to the garage."

"Close to the garage."

"She works near there."

"Does she have a street?"

"I'll text her." She didn't take out the phone.

The heater in the back started its ticking again, a fast dry click like a pen being pressed. He had the radio in one hand. "Eleven forty-seven, Carla. Deadheading in."

"Copy. Bay four. Leave your sheet in the box. Mustafa's on the wash tonight and he'll want the keys."

"Copy."

He closed the doors. He did not say anything to Ana. She sat with her hand on Theo's back and her eyes on the windshield, and that was how they went east on Barton, past the empty lot of a furniture store, past the dark scrapyard, to the gate. The guard lifted the arm without looking up. Dale brought the bus into bay four, shut it down, and the engine's noise fell away until there was only the ticking, louder now.

He set the brake and turned in his seat. The garage was bright and enormous and smelled like wet concrete. Down the row a bus sat with its panels open and a man's legs sticking out from under it.

"This is it," he said. "I've got to clear it. Everybody's got to be off."

"I know." Ana had already stood. She had the duffel on her shoulder and Theo hanging against her chest like a sack of flour. "I know. I'm sorry. She's just —" She looked at the phone, which she had taken out after all. "She's on her way."

"Sure."

He opened the door to the stairwell and then didn't go through it. He picked up the defect card from the clip by the farebox and uncapped the pen. He wrote *Rear heater, ticking, continuous* and the date, the nineteenth, and then stood there with the pen on the line where the time went. Her breath was visible in the cold coming through the open door. Theo slept with his mouth open against her collar.

"Eleven forty-seven, Carla," the radio said. "You in four?"

He pressed the button. "In four."

"You got your card done? I want to get a mechanic on that before the weekend."

"Doing it now. Heater's still going. I'm gonna let it run a few minutes, see if it quits on its own."

"It won't quit on its own, honey."

"Humor me."

"Fine. Don't be an hour." The channel clicked off.

He checked the clock above the bay door. 11:41. The last bus that went near his building left Wentworth at 11:58, a six-minute walk from the gate if he didn't stop. After that nothing ran until the first bus at 5:40, which was twelve minutes before the pullout he'd just said yes to.

Ana said, "You don't have to do anything."

"I know."

"I can walk."

"To where?"

She opened her mouth and shut it. Outside the bay doors the wet on the pavement had gone silver and hard.

Dale put the pen in his pocket. From his back pocket he took his wallet, and from behind the license a folded forty, the one he'd been keeping for his daughter's birthday card, and a ten. He went down the steps and across the bay and out the side door to the guard shack, where the phone on the wall had the cab numbers taped beside it in the guard's block capitals. He dialed. He said the garage, the north gate. He said two passengers, one small.

When he came back she was standing exactly where he had left her, at the foot of the steps, with her hand flat on the boy's back. She was watching the open side door the way you watch a door that might close.

"Five minutes," he said.

"I don't have —"

"It's done."

Headlights crossed the glass. The cab was a gray minivan with a roof light, and the driver leaned across to open the back door. Dale carried the duffel to the curb and set it in the footwell, and it was heavier than he expected. Ana got in sideways, careful of the boy's head. She leaned toward the driver's window and spoke low, two or three sentences. Dale looked at the ground. He heard the driver say, "Yep," and nothing else. He put the fifty through the window and told the man to keep it, and the man said it wouldn't cover it. Dale said, "Then take it as far as it goes."

Ana looked at him through the open door. In the dome light the paper band was visible again where Theo's sleeve had fallen back. She didn't pull it down this time. "Thank you, Dale," she said. She said the name the way you say it to someone you'll be careful not to remember.

The door shut. The cab went out through the gate and turned left, away from the lights of Barton, and was gone.

He went back and finished the card. *11:49,* he wrote. He shut the door of the stairwell, pulled the key and the sheet, and dropped them in the box for Mustafa, who had not come out from under his bus. The heater was still ticking when he walked off, softer now, down the length of the empty bus, as though someone was patient inside it.

At the gate the guard said goodnight. Dale said goodnight. On Wentworth he could see the lit shelter at the corner and, a block past it, the 11:58 already pulling out with its sign glowing and two heads in the windows. He stood on the curb and watched its taillights go up the hill. Then he put his hands in his armpits and started to walk.

The slush had set so that it shattered under his shoes. About halfway down the first block his phone buzzed again, and he stopped under a streetlight and read it with his thumb. *dad? did you see my text.* He typed *Running fine,* looked at it, and held down the button until the letters went away. He wrote *Cold one. Call you Saturday,* and sent it, and put the phone away and kept walking, with the long line of the road ahead of him gone white under the lights.
