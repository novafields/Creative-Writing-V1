The Heat Left Over

At nine fourteen, the heater began ticking again.

Dale Varga held his glove against the grille beside his knee. Heat came through, so he left it alone. A mechanic had told him once what made the noise, a flap or an actuator, and for a week he had known the proper word.

Outside, Hamilton had frozen in the shape the thaw had left it. Ridges of black snow stood along Barton Street. Water lay hard and clear in the footprints at the stops. Thursday, February 19: three hours left, two more runs, then the east-side garage.

At a red light, he checked the folded timetable in his shirt pocket. He knew the times. He checked them anyway.

If he signed off at twelve seventeen and walked quickly, he could catch the westbound at twelve twenty-six. At the transfer, he would have four minutes. Missing that connection meant a forty-three-dollar cab or sitting somewhere until morning service started.

He had been making this calculation since October, when the finance company took his Hyundai. Carla still sometimes asked whether the shop had finished with it. His daughter thought he parked behind his building, out of sight from her mother’s car.

The light changed. He put the timetable away.

At the hospital stop, a woman lifted a duffel bag onto the step, then helped a small boy climb after it. The boy wore a blue hat with two soft spikes. One mitten hung from his sleeve by a string.

The woman tapped a fare card.

It chirped a refusal.

“Try it flat,” Dale said.

She pressed it flat. The machine refused it again.

“There should be—”

“Come on in. We’ll sort it out.”

He said that perhaps twice a week. Most people kept moving before he could change his mind.

She stayed beside the reader, holding the card.

“You can sit down.”

The boy had his forehead against her thigh. She shifted the duffel with her foot and took the first forward-facing pair behind the accessible seating. Dale waited until they were down.

In the mirror, she looked younger than he’d first thought, perhaps thirty. Her coat was buttoned wrong near the bottom. The boy climbed sideways into her lap, his boots resting on the empty seat.

Dale pulled away carefully. Even at that speed, something slid under the rear wheels.

“Does this go all the way through?” she asked.

“As far as the end of the line.”

“And then?”

“Comes back.”

She looked at her phone.

“My sister’s,” she said. “We’re going to my sister’s.”

“Which stop?”

“I’ll know it.”

He watched the road.

At the next light, Carla came over the radio asking whether anybody had seen the relief driver for the twenty-four. Dale picked up the handset.

“He was at the coffee place on Wellington about ten minutes ago.”

“Of course he was.”

“He had his coat on.”

“That’s encouraging.”

Her transmission ended with a little burst of static. Dale could picture the paper cups around her keyboard, although he hadn’t been inside dispatch for months.

Behind him, the boy said, “Is it a dinosaur bus?”

“No,” the woman said.

Dale glanced in the mirror. “Depends which part you’re looking at.”

The boy raised his head.

“Engine’s pretty old.”

“Dinosaurs don’t have engines.”

“Explains why they’re late.”

The woman laughed once through her nose. The boy considered the aisle.

“He likes the bendy ones,” she said.

“Those get away from you. Front end goes to work, back end wants to stay home.”

“Do you drive those?”

“Sometimes.”

“Which is better?”

“This one, tonight.”

He couldn’t have said why he had added the last two words.

***

By ten, the boy was asleep against her. His mouth had opened, and one hand lay palm-up on her coat.

She checked the phone often. She held it low, cupping the screen, then put it away without typing.

The other passengers thinned out. A man with a grocery cart got off beside a dark bakery. Two hospital workers rode seven stops arguing about an air fryer. Dale listened long enough to learn that chicken skin was possible but toast was disappointing.

At the eastern loop, he stopped under a streetlight.

“This is the end here,” he said.

The woman looked outside.

“Already?”

“We head back in six minutes.”

She pulled the boy higher against her. His hat slid over one eye.

“Could we stay on?”

“You missed your stop?”

“I’m waiting to hear.”

Dale set the brake.

The heater ticked faster with the engine idling. He turned the fan down, then up. It made no difference.

“You know which street your sister’s on?”

“She moved.”

He waited.

“It’s different from where she was.”

“All right.”

He took his thermos out and unscrewed the lid. The coffee smelled burnt and metallic.

The woman watched him.

“Is there anywhere to get water?”

He had an unopened bottle in his lunch bag. He held it out. She looked at it a moment before taking it.

“Thank you.”

“Cap’s a bit tight.”

She opened it easily.

“I’m Ana,” she said. “That’s Theo.”

“Dale.”

Theo drank without properly waking. Ana held the bottle while he swallowed, then wiped his chin with her sleeve.

Dale’s daughter had once slept through a fire alarm at a motel in Niagara Falls. He had carried her outside wrapped in the bedspread, eleven years old and heavier than he expected. She was nineteen now. On Sunday she wanted to come over and make lasagna.

He had told her he had a proper baking dish. He still needed to buy one.

Ana looked at the nameplate above his window.

“Varga,” she said. “My gym teacher was Mr. Varga.”

“Was he a handsome fellow?”

“He made us run outside in November.”

“Doesn’t sound like me.”

“He wore shorts.”

“Definitely not.”

She smiled, then checked her phone again.

Dale drank the coffee and watched the six minutes disappear from the dashboard clock.

***

On the return trip, Ana moved Theo to the window side and tucked the duffel under his boots. There was a small embroidered strawberry on one end of the bag. Its green leaves had come unstitched.

Dale tried not to keep looking.

He had driven for the city eleven years. He had carried people through breakups, payday drinking, bad diagnoses. Sometimes he knew because they told him. More often he knew nothing and invented a reason for their face.

He was forty-seven. He had learned not to put much trust in those inventions.

At ten forty-one, Ana came forward while Theo slept against the window.

“How late do you go?”

“Tonight? Back out once more. Last stop’s the garage.”

“Is that far?”

“Depends where you’re going.”

She glanced down at the fare reader.

“Can I try the card again?”

He nodded.

She tapped it. The red light flashed.

“There was money on it yesterday.”

“Could be the card.”

“Can you see how much?”

“Not on that screen.”

She rubbed its edge with her thumb.

He had eight dollars in change, a ten, and two twenties in his wallet. Fifty-eight dollars to carry him through until next Thursday. There was food at home, though not lasagna food.

“You’re fine for tonight,” he said.

She went back to her seat.

Near the hospital, she sat straighter. Dale slowed at the stop even though no one waited there. She watched the illuminated entrance until it passed behind them.

Her phone rose in her hand, then dropped.

He pressed the radio button.

“Carla, confirm I’m out of service at the garage after this one?”

“That’s what the board says. Unless you want the first two hours of the night piece.”

“No.”

“Worth a try.”

“Relief show up?”

“Eventually. Apparently coffee takes longer now.”

He put the handset back.

In the mirror, Ana was still looking toward the rear window.

***

At eleven thirty-six, he had only three passengers. At eleven fifty, he had two.

Dale made the last scheduled stop before the garage and opened the doors. Cold moved along the floor.

“This is the last public stop,” he said.

Ana sat with her arms around Theo.

He waited, then closed the doors again.

“Your sister get back to you?”

“She’s got work in the morning.”

“Is she coming?”

Ana looked down at her phone. From the front, its glass showed the ceiling lights, two white bars crossing each other. He hadn’t heard it make a sound all evening.

“I just need a little more time.”

“I have to park this bus.”

“I know.”

“There’s another one westbound at twelve twenty-six.”

“Would it let us stay on?”

“That’s its last trip too.”

She nodded as if he had given her directions.

He could put them off here. The shelter had three glass sides, and the hospital was several stops back. There were procedures for a passenger who wouldn’t leave. He had used them once with a drunk who insisted the bus was going to Brantford.

Ana bent to collect the dangling mitten. The string had caught under Theo’s boot. She eased it free without waking him.

Dale released the brake.

***

The garage clock said twelve twelve when he brought the bus into the arrivals lane.

Light filled every window. Beyond the windshield, a man in orange coveralls guided another bus toward the wash bay. Water shone underneath it and steamed in the cold.

Dale opened his logbook.

Mileage. Defects. Fuel gauge.

He wrote *heater ticks continuously*.

The pen dug through the thin paper on the last word.

Carla called his number.

“You in?”

“In.”

“Leave it in lane three. Wash is backed up.”

He held the handset.

“I’ve got a woman and a little kid still aboard.”

“Sleeping?”

“The kid.”

A pause.

“They need medical?”

“No.”

“Refusing to get off?”

He looked at Ana. She had her bag upright between her knees, ready.

“No.”

Carla exhaled close to the microphone.

“Bring them into the drivers’ entrance. They can’t stay in the lane.”

“Is there somewhere we can call?”

“For what?”

“Somewhere inside.”

Paper moved on Carla’s end.

“Hold on.”

He set the handset down without replacing it.

The heater ticked. He switched it off, then remembered Theo and switched it on again.

Ana watched his hand.

“I can walk,” she said.

“Where?”

She pulled the duffel’s handles together.

He waited, but she didn’t answer.

Carla came back with a number. There was an overnight intake office that took families, she said. They would assess them there. She had already called; someone would let them in.

“No transport tonight. Their van’s down.”

“What’s the address?”

She gave it to him. South and west. Not on his way, though not far off.

Ana said, “Will they let him sleep?”

“I can ask.”

“Don’t wake him to ask.”

Dale looked at the clock. Twelve nineteen.

He would need to park, check the bus, take his bag inside, sign off. Then seven minutes to the stop if he ran.

The boy’s mitten lay on the floor again.

“I’ll get a cab,” Dale said.

Carla’s voice returned. “You’re clear on the sheet at twelve seventeen. I can’t add waiting time.”

“I know.”

***

He walked the aisle, lifting a discarded transfer with two fingers. Ana struggled into the duffel strap with Theo asleep over her shoulder.

“Leave the bag,” Dale said.

He checked beneath the rear seats, shut the windows, and took the keys. When he switched off the engine, the heater gave three slower ticks.

In the drivers’ vestibule, Ana sat on a bench under the safety notices. Dale phoned the cab company and gave both addresses.

“About fifty-two,” the dispatcher said.

“Cash?”

“Cash is fine.”

He had hoped for less.

Outside the glass doors, a bus crossed the far end of the yard. He checked his watch, though he already knew.

Ana adjusted Theo’s hat.

“I can pay you back.”

“Keep hold of his mitten.”

She caught it before it slipped from his sleeve.

The taxi arrived at twelve thirty-four. Dale carried the duffel out first. The driver opened the boot, but Ana asked for the bag beside her, so he put it on the back seat.

He gave the driver fifty-five dollars.

“Two stops. Family intake first.”

The driver counted the notes under the dome light, then found three dollars in change.

Dale put the coins into his pocket with the six he had left.

Ana lowered herself into the back, turning sideways to protect Theo’s head. Dale held the door against the wind while she found the seat belt.

“Can you reach it?” he asked.

“Nearly.”

He waited with his hand on the cold door edge.

There was a click.

“Got it,” she said.
