Deadhead to Wentworth

At ten-fifteen on Thursday night, the slush along Main Street East had finished turning back into stone. Two days of forty-degree rain had stripped the grey snowbanks down to their gravel ribs, and now the temperature had dropped hard enough that every puddle at the curb wore a skin of black glass. When Dale Varga brought the forty-foot Nova bus into the bay at MacNab, the front tires cracked the crust in the gutter with a sound like a boot going through a crate.

He popped the kneel switch, watched the amber light blink on the dash, and waited while two teenagers in soaked canvas sneakers clambered aboard. One of them tapped a Presto card that chirped red. The boy tapped it again, slower, as if the plastic needed time to think, while the defroster fan under Dale’s left knee kept up its dry, rhythmic tick—a bent fin catching the housing every third revolution.

"Go ahead," Dale said, before the kid could start digging through his hoodie. "Just take a seat."

The radio handset beside the steering column gave a short squelch. "Control to fifty-one-oh-four," Carla said. Her voice had the flat, gravelly drag it always got after nine o’clock, fuelled by weak breakroom coffee and three packs of menthols a week. "You copy, Dale?"

Dale keyed the thumb switch. "Fifty-one-oh-four. At MacNab, heading east."

"Put thirty seconds of drag on your loop at Gage. Eleven-twelve threw an alternator belt behind the Centre on Barton, and I got nobody in the yard to cover the gap till Henderson comes off split. Keep your spacing wide."

"Ten-four," Dale said. "Wide to the barn."

"You still parking near the Wentworth gate?" Carla asked. "My nephew’s Civic has a soft tire in the back lot. Wanted to know if you still had that little twelve-volt compressor in your trunk."

Dale’s thumb stayed half an inch above the transmit button. A draft leaked around the rubber seal of the driver’s window, cold against his jaw. The blue Hyundai Elantra—along with the twelve-volt compressor, an ice scraper with a broken red handle, and an extra pair of winter work gloves in the spare-tire well—had been winched onto a flatbed out of his apartment lot on Concession back in October.

"Left it at my place," Dale said into the mic. "Trunk latch has been sticking since the freeze."

"Figures," Carla said. "See you at one-ten. Control out."

He released the air brakes and eased out into the lane. Thursday the nineteenth of February: three hours left on the board, two full loops down to Eastgate and back to the downtown transfer, then the deadhead run into the Wentworth garage. At forty-seven, after eleven years with the city, Dale knew the grade of every crown in the asphalt between Dundurn and Stoney Creek. He knew which manholes sat half an inch too high after the frost heaved them, and he knew that if he clocked out at one-ten sharp and walked the three blocks down to Barton with his collar turned up over his transit patch, he could catch the last westbound night bus at one-twenty-two without any of the relief guys seeing him board.

His phone vibrated once inside his breast pocket. When he hit the red light at Wellington, he glanced down at the screen without taking it all the way out. It was a text from Paige: Still good for Saturday? Need to get the calc workbook from Indigo before five.

He typed back Yes with his thumb before the light turned green. The workbook was thirty-eight dollars plus tax, and they always went to the noodle place on King William afterward. Inside his wallet, tucked behind his commercial licence, were two twenties and a ten that he had taken out of the machine on payday so the bank’s automatic overdraft sweep wouldn’t eat them before Saturday afternoon.

At Victoria Avenue, the amber beacon above the St. Joseph’s clinic shelter was flashing.

Most nights after ten, the hospital stop meant a nurse coming off shift in fleece-lined clogs or an orderly smoking ten feet from the glass. Tonight a woman stood just inside the windbreak with a navy canvas duffel wedged between her shins and a small boy hoisted against her collarbone. She wasn't wearing a toque. Her dark hair was pulled back with a plastic claw clip, and a grey wool coat hung open over a thin cotton scrub top that didn't look like it belonged to her—the V-neck sagged sideways across one collarbone, showing a crescent of yellow bruise just above the hollow of her throat.

Dale pulled the nose of the bus tight to the curb and dropped the suspension.

The woman hefted the duffel by one strap—the zipper down the middle was safety-pinned shut over the bulge of a stuffed dinosaur tail—and stepped onto the treadplate. The boy was maybe four. He had on rubber slush boots with no laces and a red puffer jacket zipped to his chin, and his face was buried in the wool below her ear, fast asleep.

She set the bag down between her feet and fumbled in her coat pocket with her free hand, her fingers stiff from the wind. She pulled out a crumpled five-dollar bill and a loonie, then dropped the loonie. It rolled on its edge past the yellow line and clattered against the metal base of Dale’s seat cage.

"Sorry," she said, starting to bend with the sleeping boy slipping down her hip. "Hold on, Theo, don't—"

"Leave it," Dale said, reaching down with his boot heel to trap the coin and kick it back toward her toe. "Little guy’s under five, he rides free anyway. Drop the five in if you don't need change, or keep it until you get one of the new cards."

She hesitated, looking at the farebox glass as if she expected an alarm to go off, then pushed the five through the slot. "I'm Ana," she said, though drivers didn't ask names. She hitched the boy higher. "We're just going to my sister's out east."

"Take a seat behind the bulkhead, out of the draft," Dale said, nodding toward the sideways priority bench. "What cross street are you looking for?"

"Out past Kenilworth," she said quickly. Her eyes flicked to the green digital clock above the windshield, then down to the smartphone clutched in her left hand. "She's just texting me which corner to walk from. She moved last month."

"Plenty of time," Dale said, pulling the door lever shut. "Sing out when you know."

They rolled past Emerald and Wentworth, the tires humming on the salted patches where the city spreaders had come through at dusk. In the big convex interior mirror, Dale watched Ana settle onto the priority bench. She didn't take her coat off. She wedged the duffel beneath her knees, kept one arm locked around Theo’s back, and tapped her thumb against the side button of her phone. The screen lit her chin with a pale blue square. She stared at it for five seconds, scrolled half an inch with her thumb, then locked it again.

The two teenagers got off at Gage, leaving a wet ring of road salt on the ribbed rubber floor. At Ottawa Street, an old man in a tweed cap boarded with two plastic grocery bags full of oranges and rode four stops to Harmony. As the old man stood by the rear doors waiting for the brake interlock to hiss, he looked down at Theo’s red puffer coat.

"Out like a stone," the old man said to Ana. "My grandson used to sleep best over a diesel engine. You could run a vacuum under his crib, he'd scream the roof down. Put him in the back of an Econoline van, he was dead to the world by the end of the driveway."

Ana managed a tight, polite smile, her hand coming up automatically to cover the boy’s ear, though the knit cuff of his sleeve was already pulled down over his fingers. "He had a long afternoon," she said.

"Wait till they're sixteen," the old man said cheerfully as the rear doors folded open. "Then they sleep till two on a Sunday and eat a whole loaf of bread standing by the sink. Night, driver."

"Watch the ice on the curb," Dale called back.

At Kenilworth, Dale slowed by the illuminated shelter outside the discount pharmacy. Nobody was waiting. In the convex mirror, Ana didn't look up at the street sign. Her thumb tapped the side button of her phone again.

"That's Kenilworth," Dale said over his shoulder, pitching his voice low so it wouldn't carry past the boy’s hood. "We're coming up on Parkdale in five minutes, then Queenston."

"Right," Ana said. She didn't look up into the mirror. "She said closer to the mall. She’s still finishing her shift at the bakery, she's gonna meet us at the corner."

Dale looked at the dashboard clock. It was ten-fifty-two. No bakeries on the Queenston strip stayed open past eight on a Thursday in February.

He didn't say so. When Paige was seven, the first winter after the split, Dale had stood with her in the lobby of the Westdale theatre for forty minutes after a matinee, telling her that her mother was just stuck behind a train at the Aberdeen crossing, while he stared at a dead phone screen with twelve percent battery and a voicemail he’d already listened to twice. You didn't poke holes in what someone said when a kid was within earshot, even a sleeping one.

They reached Eastgate Square at eleven-fifteen. The terminal platform was nearly empty, swept by a raw wind coming off the escarpment that smelled of wet slag from the steel mills down by the harbour. Dale set the parking brake and stood up to stretch his lower back, rolling his shoulders until the cartilage popped.

"End of the line for ten minutes," he said, turning around in the aisle. "Then this bus turns back west toward downtown, makes one more loop out here, and pulls into the garage."

Ana’s knees tightened around the canvas duffel. Theo stirred against her chest, his mouth opening slightly, a damp dark curl sticking to his forehead. On his left wrist, peeking out half an inch from the frayed elastic of the red jacket, was a strip of white plastic hospital tape with a barcode printed on it.

"Can we stay on till it warms up?" she asked. Her voice stayed level, almost casual, as if they were discussing a transfer window. "My sister’s car wouldn't start in the lot. She’s waiting for a boost from her neighbour, then she’s picking us up back down near Sherman."

Dale looked at the white plastic band on the boy's wrist, then at the safety pin holding the duffel shut. "Sure," he said. "Heat works better when the engine's idling high anyway. I'm just stepping out to check the tires."

He walked the length of the coach on the concrete island, kicking the rear duals out of habit and listening to the heater fan tick through the open door. In his pocket, he touched the edge of his wallet. On his last loop west to MacNab and back east again, only four other riders boarded the entire way—a line cook from a bar on James who smelled of fryer oil, a woman with a small terrier zipped inside her parka, and two men in reflective vests who rode from Victoria to Gage without speaking.

Through it all, Ana sat behind the bulkhead. Every three or four blocks she pressed the side button on her phone. At midnight, when they stopped at the red light under the railway bridge on Birch, the overhead interior lamps reflected off the dark glass behind her head. Dale glanced into the mirror and caught the inverted reflection of her phone screen against the window.

There were no text bubbles on the glass. She was looking at the lock screen wallpaper—a photograph of the boy in grass with a yellow bucket—and in the upper left corner, where the carrier name belonged, the tiny white letters read No SIM.

The light turned green. Dale took his foot off the brake and fed the diesel.

"Control to all late units," Carla’s voice crackled over the speaker at twelve-forty. "Salt trucks are back out on the Claremont access and the Jolly Cut. Watch your braking distance on the downgrades. Fifty-one-oh-four, you're cleared direct to Wentworth garage from Eastgate. Skip the deadhead loop on Barton, they want your unit over the pit by one-fifteen so the night crew can swap that blower motor."

Dale reached for the handset. The heater fan under his knee went tick-tick-tick, faster now that he had the blower on high to keep the frost off the lower windshield.

"Fifty-one-oh-four," Dale said. "Copy direct to Wentworth."

At Eastgate for the final time, the platform lights clicked onto half-power, casting long orange shadows across the frozen slush. Twelve-fifty-five. Dale didn't open the rear doors. He left the transmission in neutral, set the yellow parking knob, and turned in his seat.

"Last run's done," he said. "They called me straight into the barn at Wentworth and Sanford."

Ana’s head came up. The claw clip in her hair had slipped crooked against the vinyl headrest, and the skin under her eyes had the grey, papery look of someone who hadn't slept since Wednesday. "You go past Sherman on the way?"

"Down Main to Wentworth, then north to the yard," Dale said. "Look, it’s twenty below with the wind off the bay, and nothing's open on Sherman."

"She's almost there," Ana said. Her knuckles went white around the phone, her thumb pressing the side button so hard the nail turned pale. She didn't turn the screen toward him. "She just—the traffic on the mountain access was backed up."

"The salt trucks are the only thing on the access right now," Dale said quietly.

She looked down at the crown of Theo’s head. The boy’s breathing was slow and heavy, his small boots dangling six inches above the floor. For three seconds nobody spoke, and the only sound in the bus was the bent fin of the defroster fan striking its metal housing—tick, tick, tick—and the air dryer underneath the chassis letting go with a sharp pneumatic sneeze.

"I can take you as far as the front gate at Wentworth," Dale said, turning back to the wheel before she had to answer. "After that I have to pull over the inspection pit."

He drove the last two miles in the curb lane at twenty-five miles an hour, catching every red light on purpose so the boy could have another ten minutes of eighty-degree air from the floor vents. When he swung the nose of fifty-one-oh-four through the open chain-link gates of the Wentworth garage at one-oh-eight, the yard floodlights turned the bus interior as bright as an operating room. Rows of striped city buses sat nose-to-tail along the fence, exhaust pluming white into the dark.

Dale stopped just short of the big yellow rollup doors of Bay Three. Inside the glass booth by the pedestrian gate, he could see Miller from the night maintenance crew holding a clipboard and waiting to wave him onto the grease pit.

If Dale cleared the bus now, signed his log sheet at the dispatch window by one-twelve, and walked fast down Wentworth to Barton, he would make the one-twenty-two night bus heading west with four minutes to spare. He’d be inside his apartment by two, with eight hours before his Thursday-Friday turnaround and fifty dollars still folded in his wallet for Paige on Saturday. If he missed the one-twenty-two, there wasn't another westbound bus until the five-fifteen steelworker run.

He reached overhead, flipped the destination sign toggle to NOT IN SERVICE, and pulled the main farebox vault key on its chain to print the end-of-shift tally slip. The tiny thermal printer whirred, jammed on a curled edge of paper, and beeped twice.

"Come on," Dale muttered. He popped the small metal cover with his thumb, pinched the paper roll to straighten the feed, and clicked the latch shut until the three-inch strip of numbers spat out into his palm.

In the mirror, Ana was standing up, trying to slide her left arm through the duffel strap without waking Theo. When the boy’s weight shifted, his head rolled back against her shoulder and he whimpered once, his eyes staying shut, one hand clutching a fistful of her grey coat.

"Door's open?" she asked, her voice small against the roar of the garage exhaust fans outside.

Dale looked at the tally slip in his hand, then at the digital clock: 1:11.

"Hold on," Dale said.

He didn't open the door. He pulled his own phone from his breast pocket, opened the browser, and typed in the dispatch number for the Hamilton East cab stand on Barton. Before he hit call, he turned halfway around in the seat.

"That hotel by the Red Hill ramp—the one with the weekly rooms and the twenty-four-hour desk," Dale said. "Or the St. Martha’s shelter on Wellington. Which one do you have ID for?"

Ana froze with the duffel strap halfway up her forearm. The defensive stiffness went out of her shoulders all at once, leaving her looking five years younger and half as tall. "Martha's," she whispered. "My health card's in the bag. My—he took my purse off the table before we left, so I only had what was in my coat."

"Sit down a second," Dale said. "Don't wake him up yet."

He dialed the cab company and waited through four rings while Miller stepped out of the maintenance booth in his earmuffs and slapped his gloved hands together, pointing at the Bay Three rollup door. Dale held up one finger through the windshield—one minute—and spoke into his phone when the cab dispatcher picked up.

"Need a sedan at the Wentworth transit garage, pedestrian gate on the north side," Dale said. "Going to Wellington North. Yeah, right now."

He hung up, unclipped his seatbelt, and stood in the stairwell by the farebox. He took out his wallet, angled his body so the overhead camera by the destination box only saw his back, and slid the two twenties and the ten out from behind his commercial licence.

He stepped back to the priority bench and held the three bills out flat, folded once to the size of a matchbook.

"Cab's twelve dollars from here to Wellington," Dale said. "Give him the ten and five out of a twenty. Use the rest for a phone card at the variety store in the morning so you can call your sister for real."

Ana stared at the green and purple bills in his hand. Her throat worked. "I can't pay you back. I don't even know your name."

"Take it before the mechanic comes out here and writes me up for blocking the wash bay," Dale said. When she still didn't move, he tucked the folded bills into the side pocket of the navy duffel bag, right beside the safety pin. "They have cribs at Martha's, and the night coordinator's name is Brenda. Used to ride my route on the mountain."

He turned back to the console and hit the pneumatic release. The front doors folded open with a rush of frigid air that smelled of diesel soot and frozen mud.

Ana stepped down the stairwell carefully, turning sideways so Theo’s slush boots wouldn't catch on the yellow handrail. On the pavement outside, she adjusted the boy’s hood over his ears and looked back up at the high driver’s window. She didn't say thank you; she just gave a single, sharp nod, as if they had agreed on a schedule, and walked toward the lit pedestrian gate where a bruised white Crown Victoria taxi was already turning into the drive with its roof lamp glowing orange.

Dale closed the doors, released the brake, and eased fifty-one-oh-four through the rollup bay onto the steel tracks over the inspection pit.

"Took your time, Varga," Miller called up from the concrete apron, hooking the yellow exhaust hose onto the tailpipe. "Blower motor's ticking like a grandfather clock."

"Bent fin on the squirrel cage," Dale said, shutting down the master ignition switches one by one until the dashboard went dark and the ticking under his left knee finally stopped. "Needs a five-sixteenths wrench and ten minutes."

By the time he dropped his defect card and the thermal tally slip into Carla’s tray at the dispatch window, the clock on the cinderblock wall read one-twenty-four. Down on Barton, four blocks south, the last westbound bus was already past Sherman.

"Night, Dale," Carla said without looking up from her run sheets. "Watch the lot, it’s slick by the back fence."

"Night, Carla," he said.

He went into the locker room, pulled his navy wool toque down to his eyebrows, and wrapped his scarf twice around his chin before zipping his winter shell to the throat. Outside the pedestrian gate, the white taxi was gone, leaving two fresh tracks through the grey frost at the curb.

Dale took his phone out with his gloved hand before the battery could freeze on the walk. He opened his messages to Paige and typed: Hey kiddo. Let's do pancakes at my place Saturday morning instead of King William. Pick up the workbook Monday after school, my treat.

He hit send, shoved the phone deep into his inside pocket, and turned west down the middle of the sidewalk on Wentworth. It was four miles to the mountain stairs at Dundurn and three hundred steps up the steel risers after that, and his boots crunched loud and steady through the rime with every stride.
